## 5. The Precedent (Living Treaty)

- **The Marshall decisions (DFO official summary, 2021)** [dfo-mpo.gc](https://www.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/fisheries-peches/aboriginal-autochtones/moderate-livelihood-subsistance-convenable/marshall-overview-apercu-eng.html)
  This federal summary explains how the Supreme Court’s first *Marshall* decision affirmed Mi’kmaq treaty rights to fish, hunt, and gather for a “moderate livelihood,” and how *Marshall II* clarified that these rights can only be limited for conservation or other compelling and substantial public objectives. It notes that the Court allowed government to consider regional and economic fairness when regulating, which forms the legal basis for DFO’s conservation and safety justifications. [dfo-mpo.gc](https://www.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/fisheries-peches/aboriginal-autochtones/moderate-livelihood-subsistance-convenable/marshall-overview-apercu-eng.html)

- **The Marshall Decision (Union of Nova Scotia Mi’kmaq explainer, 2026)** [unsm](https://www.unsm.org/accomplishments/the-marshall-decision)
  The UNSM explainer states that *Marshall* recognized Donald Marshall Jr.’s treaty right to secure a “moderate livelihood” by hunting, fishing, and gathering, grounding it in 18th‑century treaties. It emphasizes that federal and provincial governments have authority to regulate the exercise of treaty rights where justified on conservation or other grounds, stressing that conservation is the “paramount regulatory objective” and responsibility of the minister. [unsm](https://www.unsm.org/accomplishments/the-marshall-decision)

It quotes *Marshall II* for the proposition that treaty rights are subject to regulation “on conservation or other grounds of public importance,” framing current management plans as implementing this doctrine. - **The “empty box” vs. “full box” metaphor in Canadian Indigenous rights law (academic sources)**  
  - A UBC law dissertation on patriation and Indigenous self‑government discusses whether section 35 of the Constitution is an “empty box” to be filled incrementally through negotiation/litigation or a “full box” already containing robust Indigenous self‑determination, citing Walkem & Bruce’s *Box of Treasures or Empty Box?*. This metaphor is useful for framing how courts and governments could either narrowly read *Marshall* (empty box, minimal content) or treat it as already recognizing broad, living treaty rights (full box). This helps students contrast incremental, case‑by‑case recognition of moderate livelihood rights with more expansive treaty‑based understandings. [digitalcommons.osgoode.yorku](https://digitalcommons.osgoode.yorku.ca/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1260&context=all_papers)

- **Indigenous Fishing Rights and International Human Rights Law (IHRP, University of Toronto)** [ihrp.law.utoronto](https://ihrp.law.utoronto.ca/indigenous-fishing-rights-and-international-human-rights-law)
  This legal analysis situates Mi’kmaw treaty fishing rights within international human rights frameworks, including UNDRIP, to argue that states must respect Indigenous peoples’ rights to improve economic conditions and control resource development in their territories. While not limited to lobster, it provides a bridge between UNDRIP Articles 21 (improvement of economic and social conditions) and 32 (control over resource development) and Canadian debates about regulating moderate livelihood fisheries. - **Yellowhead Institute – Lobster Fishing & the Continued Oppression of L’nu’k in Mi’kma’ki (2022)** [yellowheadinstitute](https://yellowheadinstitute.org/2021/colonizers-being-colonizers-lobster-fishing-the-continued-oppression-of-lnuk-in-mikmaki/)
  Yellowhead’s piece critiques Canada’s response to the moderate livelihood fishery, describing DFO’s insistence that Mi’kmaw fishing occur only in commercial seasons as a refusal to respect Mi’kmaw‑regulated treaty fisheries and calling for recognition of Indigenous jurisdiction. It documents events like the threatened removal of Treaty Truckhouse #3 and emphasizes the need for protocols that respect Mi’kmaw governance, aligning with UNDRIP‑style commitments to free, prior, and informed consent over resource use.
