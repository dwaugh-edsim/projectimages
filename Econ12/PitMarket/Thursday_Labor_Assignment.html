In the Pit Market, we saw how competition forces prices to the lowest possible point. Today, we apply that same logic to the global labor market.

Task 1: The Tag Check

Identify the "Made in" label for two items of clothing you are wearing or have in your bag.

See if your country is in the Top 8:

  • China: The undisputed global leader in apparel, offering unparalleled supply chain efficiency, capacity, and infrastructure.
  • Bangladesh: A key player for fast fashion, known for low-cost, high-volume apparel manufacturing.
  • Vietnam: A leading exporter specializing in high-quality sportswear, formal wear, and knitwear, often chosen for its high-value manufacturing.
  • India: A major textile hub offering diverse products, specializing in cotton, and providing both high-quality textiles and competitive pricing.
  • Turkey: A major manufacturer for Europe, recognized for high-quality production, speed, and premium materials.
  • Pakistan: Renowned for its strong, specialized production in cotton garments, denim, and knitted items.
  • Indonesia: Growing rapidly, specializing in both knit and woven garments with a skilled workforce.
  • Cambodia: A competitive manufacturing hub offering low labor costs and increasing production capacity for export.
Task 2: The Race to the Bottom

The shipping container has effectively deleted the cost of distance. If a worker in Dhaka can afford to live on a wage that wouldn't even cover basic rent in Halifax, what happens to the "market price" for labor when both workers are competing for the exact same job?

Task 3: Are YOU a Commodity?

In a perfectly competitive global market, unskilled labor is treated like a commodity (identical product). How can a worker in Canada "exit" this competition and become a "Price Maker" instead of a "Price Taker"?

Task 4: The Factory Trace (Advanced Intelligence)

Moving beyond the "Made in" label: Can you identify the exact facility where your clothing or shoes were produced? Search the brand's corporate website for "Supply Chain Transparency" or "Factory List."

Note: You may not get very far. Many supply chains are intentionally opaque. The "dead end" itself is an important piece of economic data.
