THEMATIC LINKS

CONNECTIONS ACROSS STUDENT PROJECTS

1. The Housing Affordability and Cost-of-Living Crisis

A central theme across several projects is how systemic issues have made housing and basic living expenses unattainable for the average person.

[Jack & Alex]

demonstrate that the average income is no longer enough to cover exorbitant rent or mortgages in cities like Halifax, a problem worsened by corporate monopolies buying up properties and reducing competition.

[Angelique]

connects this to population growth, noting that Canada's push for economic growth through immigration has far outpaced the development of housing and infrastructure, driving up prices and heavily impacting low- and middle-income groups.

[Ana & Anna]

argue that because traditional paths to homeownership are broken, the "Great Wealth Transfer" (an inheritance from Baby Boomers) is now the only realistic way for Millennials and Gen Z to acquire a home.

[Reyha & Nahla]

explain that to survive this gap between wages and living costs, younger generations are forced into "side stacking," taking on multiple jobs just to afford basic necessities.

2. Artificial Intelligence as a Disruptor of Labor and Education

The rapid advancement of AI and algorithms is actively reshaping how people work, study, and earn money.

[Maggie & Sivakami]

point out that AI is highly proficient at replacing human labor in entry-level and repetitive roles, fundamentally altering the job market.

[John & Peyton]

link this directly to the education system through "degree inflation," explaining that because AI can do basic tasks cheaply, businesses now require expensive university degrees for entry-level roles, leaving fresh graduates underpaid and in debt.

[Efe]

discusses how Universal Basic Income (UBI) may become a necessary safety net to protect people from the sudden job displacement caused by AI and automation.

[Adarsha & Josh]

look at the media landscape, noting that while "going viral" is now a career, it is heavily controlled by AI algorithms that dictate engagement, making income highly unstable.

3. Systemic Inequality and the "Rich-Get-Richer" Dynamic

Many of the presentations highlight structures that consolidate wealth and power at the top while leaving the working class and young people behind.

[Adarsha & Josh]

highlight the extreme inequality of the Attention Economy in the digital space, where just 1% of creators make 99% of the money, creating an oligopoly controlled by a few massive platforms.

[Ana & Anna]

show how the impending wealth transfer in real estate will drastically widen the wealth gap between individuals born to "housing-rich" parents and those who are not.

[Jack & Alex]

also touch on housing inequality, noting that large corporate ownership of local housing creates monopolies that shut out regular buyers.

[Maggie & Sivakami]

note that AI development in the tech sector increases the gap between the rich and the poor, as high-skilled workers benefit while low-skilled workers face job losses.

4. Economic "Traps" and the Decline of Financial Stability

A final major theme is how modern economic conditions force individuals into unwinnable "traps" where their efforts to get ahead actually set them back.

[Reyha & Nahla]

explore the "Side Hustle Trap" and the "health poverty trap," showing how young people working multiple jobs sacrifice their education and free time, only to suffer burnout and chronic illness, forcing them to spend their hard-earned money on medical bills.

[John & Peyton]

highlight an educational trap where students invest four years into a bachelor's degree to get a job, only to discover their degree has a negative Return on Investment (ROI).

[Efe]

points out a structural trap in traditional welfare—the "poverty trap"—where citizens are discouraged from earning more money because they will lose their benefits, an issue UBI attempts to fix.

[Seth]

illustrates a policy trap regarding EV tariffs; while tariffs are meant to protect domestic jobs, placing a 100% tax on affordable Chinese EVs simply punishes middle-class consumers by trapping them with expensive North American options.
