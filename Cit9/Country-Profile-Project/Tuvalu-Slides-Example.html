Country Profile: Tuvalu

Where it is, the people, and how they live
Slide 2 / 6
  • Geography: A tiny country in the Pacific Ocean, halfway between Australia and Hawaii. It is made of 9 small coral islands.
  • Super Low Land: Very little land area (only 26 km² total!). On average, the islands are only 1.5 metres above the sea.
  • People & Culture: Polynesian heritage. Families are close-knit, and they have a tradition called "Soli" where everyone helps and shares with each other.
  • Local Economy: Most people fish or farm cocoanuts and taro. The country also rents out its ".tv" internet code to streaming companies for extra revenue.

The Global Issue: Sea Level Rise

Why the ocean is rising and threatening the islands
Slide 3 / 6

Global greenhouse gas emissions are warming the planet, causing polar ice to melt and ocean water to expand.

  • Faster Rise Rate: Oceans around Tuvalu are rising by about 5 mm every year—faster than the global average of 3.4 mm.
  • Flooding Danger: Scientists project that half of the capital, Funafuti, will flood daily during high tides by 2050.
  • Salt in the Soil: Sea water seeps through the sandy ground, ruining drinking water wells and killing local food crops like taro.

How is Tuvalu Responding?

Protecting the shores and planning for the future
Slide 4 / 6
  • TCAP Project: A coastal defense project that reclaimed 7.3 hectares of land in Funafuti, raising the ground 3.5 metres to make a safe zone for homes.
  • UN Climate Speeches: Tuvalu leaders actively speak at global climate meetings, urging larger countries to cut emissions and limit warming to 1.5°C.
  • Digital metaverse nation: The government is building a virtual copy of Tuvalu online to preserve their culture and boundaries if the physical islands submerge.
  • The Falepili Union (2023): A treaty allowing 280 Tuvaluan citizens to move to Australia every year to work or study as sea level risks increase.
Sources: (1) UNDP TCAP Progress Report (2023). (2) Australian DFAT (2023) — Australia-Tuvalu Treaty. APA Style Citation

Evaluating the Consequences

The intended and unintended results of Tuvalu's actions
Slide 5 / 6

Intended Consequences (Successes)

  • Wave Protection: Seawalls and raised land protect homes and buildings from high waves during big storms.
  • Sovereignty: Pushing for international rules that say Tuvalu keeps its sea territory rights even if the islands disappear.

Unintended Consequences (New Problems)

  • Coastal Erosion: Hard concrete seawalls block natural sand, causing nearby unprotected beaches to erode faster.
  • Health Issues: Since crops are ruined by salt, people rely on imported canned foods. This has caused high diabetes rates.
  • Family Splits: The migration treaty helps people move, but it splits up extended families, leaving elders behind.
Sources: (1) SPREP Vulnerability Study (2022). (2) UN ReliefWeb Pacific Mobility (2023). APA Style Citation

Tuvalu vs Canada & Reflection

Comparing actions, carbon footprints, and why we should care
Slide 6 / 6
Dimension Tuvalu Canada
Key Actions Seawalls, migration treaty, digital statehood. National Adaptation Strategy ($1.6B), dikes, flood maps.
Adaptation Budget Very low; relies on international aid. Very high; G7 economy.
Existential Risk High: Loss of the entire country and displacement. Medium: Protects specific coastal areas (e.g. Richmond, BC).
Carbon Footprint Almost zero (less than 0.001% of global total). One of the highest per-capita historical emitters.

Reflection: Why should Canadians care?

Shared Air: Greenhouse gases emitted in Canada go into the same atmosphere and cause sea level rise that floods Tuvalu. We have a responsibility to help.

Shared Coastlines: Canada has sea level rise risks too (Nova Scotia and Richmond, BC). Learning from Tuvalu helps us protect our own shores.
