Citizenship 9 Election Unit — Marking Guide

This document is a reference for the LLM or teacher conducting outcome-based grading of the Halifax West 2026 Election Simulation. It maps every evidence artifact, explains the grading rationale, and provides the student-by-student breakdown.

1. Unit Overview

What Happened

Grade 9 Citizenship students formed political parties, wrote platforms, ran campaigns, debated in a Town Hall, voted, and reflected. The simulation ran over approximately 3 weeks (early April to mid-May 2026).

The unit phases were:

  1. Political Spectrum Activity — 20 agree/disagree statements to self-place on left/right spectrum
  2. Party Formation — groups of 2-3 registered parties with name, slogan, color, PIN
  3. War Room — 3 deliverables: Communications message, 3 policy promises, funding plan
  4. Press Scrum — AI Journalist challenges party on their platform via live chat
  5. Campaign — 2-minute speech, slide deck, opposition research
  6. Primary Vote — all parties present; top 4 advance to Final Four
  7. Final Four Town Hall — moderated debate with audience questions
  8. General Election — secret ballot from Final Four
  9. Post-Election News — fictional news articles about the aftermath
  10. Reflection — 4-question Google Form reflection

2. Outcomes Being Assessed

All three outcomes are from Unit 5: Governance in the Citizenship 9 Foundational Outcomes.

Outcome 5.1 — Issue Valuation

Learners will analyse how issues become valued within and across all areas of government and society.

What to look for: Did the student choose meaningful issues for their platform? Can they explain why those issues matter? Do they understand how issues gain political traction? Can they identify common ground between parties?

Outcome 5.2 — Government Structure

Learners will investigate the structure, operation, and selection of government in Canada, including federal, provincial, territorial, indigenous, and municipal government models.

What to look for: Does the student's platform reflect realistic understanding of how government works? Do they understand funding mechanisms, jurisdictional scope, electoral dynamics? Can they distinguish between classroom and real-world elections?

Outcome 5.3 — Democratic Engagement

Learners will evaluate strategies to meaningfully engage as citizens within a democratic process.

What to look for: Did the student take a meaningful role in the simulation? Can they describe specific contributions? Do they understand civic agency? Did they participate in the Town Hall? This outcome is explicitly referenced in the project files as "Outcome 5.3: Platform & Policy Advocacy."

4. Evidence Hierarchy

  1. Teacher observation PRIMARY — Who showed up, who drove the work, who was present and engaged day after day. The teacher's direct knowledge of classroom dynamics trumps all other evidence.
  2. Work submissions STRONG — Platform text, presentation slides (with author credits), opposition research, speech scripts, war room deliverables.
  3. Town Hall question data STRONG — The 42 questions in the TH_Questions sheet provide objective evidence of individual engagement during the Final Four debate. Number of questions, targets, and quality all matter.
  4. Reflection responses IMPORTANT — The 4-question post-election reflection. Important individual evidence but not the sole measure. Some highly engaged students (Delisha) were absent during the reflection process.
  5. Party platform quality CONTEXTUAL — Party-level evidence. Must be mapped to individuals based on role and self-reported contribution. A strong platform doesn't mean every member contributed equally.
  6. Voting data ENGAGEMENT ONLY — Confirms students voted. NOT used as a grading factor. Vote counts reflect junior-high social dynamics, not platform quality.

5. Grading Scale

GradeMeaning
4Fully committed. Completed their role, contributed meaningfully, reflection is thoughtful and reveals genuine engagement with the democratic process.
3+Strong engagement with standout moments. Not quite full commitment across all areas but shows clear effort and understanding.
3Some meaningful engagement. Participated, contributed something real, reflection shows decent understanding.
2+Emerging engagement. Some contribution but limited depth or follow-through. Minimum floor for students who participated at all.
2Very little done. Minimal contribution, reflection is shallow or perfunctory. (Not used for students who made any effort.)
1+Marginal presence. Almost no evidence of engagement.
1Essentially absent — no reflection, no contribution, no-show.

6. Key Grading Decisions

Group Dynamics

Platform completeness varied because of missing students on various days and disproportionate distribution of motivated vs less-motivated students. This is a bigger factor than tech barriers. An incomplete platform does not mean the student didn't learn or try. A complete platform doesn't mean every member contributed equally.

How this was handled: Individual contribution (from reflections and Town Hall data) was assessed separately from platform completeness (party-level). A student in an incomplete party who described specific efforts was not penalized for the party's failure. A student in a complete party who described minimal contribution did not get full credit.

ICA Test Exclusion

The April 17 ICA written test is a separate assessment piece. Its scores and feedback are NOT used for election unit grading. Only election-specific evidence is considered.

Vote Counts as Non-Evidence

Voting data confirms students participated in the act of voting. It is NOT used as a grading factor. Vote counts reflect junior-high social dynamics, not platform quality. Students like Zankia and Kendra produced some of the best projects but received few votes.

Reflection as Key but Not Sole Artifact

The 4-question reflection is the one piece of work that is unambiguously individual. It is weighted heavily. However, teacher discretion is the primary artifact. Teacher observation of who showed up, who drove the work, and who was engaged day after day trumps all other evidence. Students who were absent during the reflection process (Delisha) but were known by the teacher to be major participants were graded accordingly.

Town Hall Questions as Hidden Evidence

The 42 questions in the TH_Questions sheet provide objective evidence of individual engagement during the Final Four debate. This data was initially overlooked but proved critical for several students:

  • Ali — 8 questions (most in class), upgraded 5.3 from 2+ to 3 despite no reflection
  • Leo — 6 questions (2 on_stage), upgraded 5.1 to 3 and 5.3 to 3, notably more effort than peers
  • Lachlan Mac — 6 questions, upgraded 5.3 from 3+ to 4
  • Yunho — 2 questions (1 on_stage), upgraded 5.3 from 3 to 3+
  • Remy — 1 on_stage question, upgraded 5.3 from 2+ to 3

Minimum Grade Floor

All students who participated at any level receive a minimum of 2+ across all outcomes. The only 1s are for students who were largely absent (Brody).

10. How to Use This Guide

For an LLM reviewing the grades

  1. Read the grading matrix at Cit9/election-polparties-marking/election-grading-matrix.md for the full student-by-student breakdown with justifications and evidence quotes.
  2. Read the student feedback HTML at Cit9/election-polparties-marking/election-feedback.html for the personalized feedback each student will see (login with name + PIN from the roster in Code.gs).
  3. Cross-reference with the reflection CSV at Cit9/election-polparties-marking/election-reflection-1.csv for raw student responses.
  4. Check the TH_Questions sheet in Cit9/election-polparties-marking/Cit9 Politics.xlsx for Town Hall engagement data.
  5. Review party platforms in HTMLs/political-spectrum/party-summaries.md or the embedded data in opposition-research.html.
  6. The Final 4 slide images in HTMLs/political-spectrum/assets/Final4/ show author credits in the corners — these can confirm individual contributions.
