📜 Exhibit A: The Truckhouse Defense (Treaty Rights)

The Core Claim: Leo's legal defense rests on a 265-year-old agreement between the British Crown and the Mi'kmaq Nation. This isn't a new invention—it's a contract. And contracts don't expire.

📜

The 1760 Treaty Promise

"The Mi'kmaq people shall have the liberty to hunt, fish, and trade for a moderate livelihood in their territories. This right is guaranteed in perpetuity."

The Defense argues: This isn't about cannabis specifically. It's about the fundamental right to earn a living through commerce on our land.

The Historical Evolution

Leo's legal team argues that the right to trade is timeless, even though the items traded and the locations have changed. This is the core of the "Truckhouse" argument:

1760 - The Original Truckhouse

🏘️

Item Traded: Furs, fish, medicinal plants

Location: Log cabin or wooden structure

Purpose: Economic survival of the Mi'kmaq Nation

→

2025 - The Modern Truckhouse

🏪

Item Traded: Cannabis (legal under band law)

Location: Climate-controlled dispensary

Purpose: Economic survival of the Sipekne'katik community

❓ The Legal Question

Does a contract that says "trade for livelihood" expire just because the type of goods changes?

Leo's Argument: "A Promise is a Promise. The Crown doesn't get to rewrite our contract because they don't like cannabis. If the government can pick and choose which items we're allowed to trade, then we never had a real right at all."

The Supreme Court Precedent

R. v. Marshall (1999) - The Leading Precedent

THE FACTS:

Donald Marshall Jr. caught 463 pounds of eels and sold them for $787 without a commercial fishing license. He was charged with violating federal law.

THE RULING:

The Supreme Court of Canada ruled in Marshall's favor. The 1760 Treaties grant the Mi'kmaq a constitutional right to earn a "Moderate Livelihood" through harvesting and selling natural resources.

CRITICAL TO LEO'S CASE: The Court rejected the Crown's argument that rights must be "frozen" in their pre-contact form. The Court said: "The right to trade evolves with the economy and society. Modern commerce is still commerce."

How This Helps Leo: If eels sold for cash in 1999 is protected by Treaty, why isn't cannabis sold for cash in 2025? Both are commodities. Both support the community. The Defense will use Marshall as their foundation.

⚠️ The Crown's Counter-Attack

The Crown will argue that eels are a traditional food source, while cannabis is a modern drug. They'll claim Marshall doesn't apply to items the Mi'kmaq never traditionally traded.

The Defense Response: "Our ancestors traded everything valuable—furs, food, medicines. Cannabis is a medicine. And besides, we're not bound by what our ancestors happened to have access to. We're bound by the right to trade itself."

📋 Exhibit C: The Legal Battleground - Frozen vs. Evolving Rights

This exhibit shows the battlefield where Leo's case will be won or lost. It's not about cannabis. It's about whether Indigenous rights are frozen in time or alive and evolving.

The Three Competing Legal Standards

⚖️

1999 R. v. Marshall

The Victory: Donald Marshall Jr. caught and sold 463 lbs of eels. Supreme Court ruled the 1760 Treaties grant a constitutional right to earn a "Moderate Livelihood."

WHY THIS MATTERS: Court allowed eels (not traditionally sold this way) to be protected. Rejected the idea that rights must be frozen in pre-contact form.

❄️

1996 R. v. Van der Peet

The "Frozen Rights" Test: To claim a protected practice, it must have existed before Europeans arrived (pre-contact).

THE TRAP: If this strict rule applies, cannabis sales are indefensible. Cannabis didn't exist in 1760.

✨
🧊

2023 R. v. Montour

The Thaw: Quebec ruling proposes abandoning "frozen rights." Instead, prove activities are protected by Indigenous legal systems and governance.

THE HOPE: If Leo can convince the Supreme Court, this becomes national law. Cannabis sales under Sipekne'katik governance would be protected.

The 2024 Nova Scotia Case: The Reality Check

⚠️ THE CURRENT LAW IN NOVA SCOTIA

In June 2024, a Nova Scotia judge rejected a Treaty defense for cannabis sales using the Van der Peet test. The judge said:

"Cannabis was not part of traditional Mi'kmaq commerce. The right to trade is limited to practices that predate European contact. Therefore, selling cannabis is not protected by Treaty."

This is the exact precedent Leo must overcome. If Van der Peet remains the law, Leo loses. If Montour replaces it, Leo has a fighting chance.
