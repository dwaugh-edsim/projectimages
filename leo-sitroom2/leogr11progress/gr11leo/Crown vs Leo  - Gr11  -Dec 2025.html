1. The Conflict

Canada legalized cannabis, but Sipekne'katik First Nation says they don't need Canada's permission to trade. They opened their own stores on their own land.

2. The Crackdown

The Province says these stores are "Illegal" and unsafe. They sent in police to raid the stores and seize the products.

3. The Resistance

Sipekne'katik fought back. They BANNED the Premier from their land. They argue: "We have a Treaty right to earn a living. You have no power here."

Exhibit A: The "Truckhouse" Clause

Historical Document

THE GHOST OF 1752

At the heart of this dispute lies a piece of parchment signed nearly 300 years ago. It promised the Mi'kmaq a "Truckhouse" to trade their goods. The building is long gone, but the promise remains.

The Question: Does this ancient clause create a modern constitutional right to sell cannabis, or was it merely a colonial rule for trading beaver pelts? The answer determines the fate of every dispensary in the province.

Exhibit B: Jurisdiction

Territorial Sovereignty

WHOSE LAW RULES THIS LAND?

Two governments claim authority over the same patch of earth. The Province cites the "Rule of Law"—arguing that health codes and speed limits stop at no border.

The Chiefs cite "Sovereignty"—arguing that on unceded territory, the King's law does not apply without consent.

Exhibit C: Evolution of Rights

Traditional Eel Weir

FROZEN IN TIME?

The Supreme Court created the "Frozen Rights" doctrine: to claim a right, you must prove your ancestors did it before Europeans arrived. The Crown says cannabis is new, so it's illegal.

But a new legal revolution is brewing (UNDRIP). The Defense argues rights are living things: We don't demand Indigenous people hunt with bows; why must they trade only in furs?

Exhibit D: Public Safety

Police Evidence Seizure

THE FENTANYL FACTOR

This is the Crown's nuclear option: Public Safety. Citing reports of fentanyl contamination, the Province argues that rights must yield to protect lives.

The Defense calls this a smear campaign. If the Band has its own safety laws, does the Province still have a right to intervene?

Exhibit E: Economics

Economic Impact

DOLLARS AND CENTS

The Crown sees tax evasion. The Defense sees "Economic Reconciliation"—revenue that funds housing and water.
