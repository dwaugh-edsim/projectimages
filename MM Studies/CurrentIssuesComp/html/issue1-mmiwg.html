Missing & Murdered Indigenous Women & Girls

Understanding the MMIWG crisis in Canada — its roots in colonialism, the activists fighting for justice, and the National Inquiry that called it genocide.

The Crisis in Context

Indigenous women and girls in Canada are disproportionately represented among missing and murdered persons. The National Inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls (2019) concluded that this constitutes a "deliberate race, identity and gender-based genocide."

Root Causes — The Cycle of Trauma

Residential Schools — For over a century, Indigenous children were forcibly removed from their families, severing cultural bonds and causing intergenerational trauma.
Child Welfare System — The "Sixties Scoop" and ongoing overrepresentation of Indigenous children in foster care perpetuated the cycle of family separation.
Homelessness & Exploitation — Youth aging out of care often face homelessness, substance abuse, and vulnerability to sexual exploitation and trafficking.
Systemic Failures — Inadequate police response, jurisdictional gaps, and institutional racism allowed cases to go unsolved and families to be dismissed.

Activists & Community Response

In the face of institutional inaction, Indigenous communities have led their own search and justice efforts:

Drag the Red — Community-led river searches in Winnipeg's Red River for missing women's remains, turning grief into action.
Bear Clan Patrol — Volunteer street patrol providing safety, food, and support to vulnerable community members, especially women and youth.
Midnight Medicine Walk — Nighttime walks through high-risk areas to provide safety, companionship, and spiritual healing.

The National Inquiry (2016–2019)

In 2016, Prime Minister Justin Trudeau launched the National Inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls. After hearing from over 2,300 people across Canada, the 2019 Final Report delivered 231 Calls for Justice.

"This genocide has been empowered by colonial structures evidenced notably by the Indian Act, the Sixties Scoop, residential schools, and breaches of human and Inuit and Métis rights, leading directly to the current increased rates of violence, death, and suicide in Indigenous populations."
— Reclaiming Power and Place: The Final Report of the National Inquiry into MMIWG, 2019

Key Findings

  • Indigenous women and girls are 12 times more likely to be murdered or missing than non-Indigenous women.
  • Police forces across Canada failed to adequately investigate cases involving Indigenous women.
  • The child welfare system was identified as a direct pipeline to exploitation and violence.
  • Colonial policies created the conditions for genocide — not individual acts, but systemic, structural violence.

Key Recommendations

  • Fund a National Action Plan with measurable goals and accountability mechanisms.
  • Establish a National Police Task Force to review and reopen cases.
  • Reform the child welfare system to prioritize Indigenous families and communities.
  • Address poverty, housing, and healthcare inequities that create vulnerability.
  • Ensure culturally safe policing and justice services.

Discussion Questions

  1. Explain how the residential school system created a cycle that has contributed to the crisis of Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls. What are the connections between each stage of the cycle?
  2. Why has the child welfare system been identified as a "pipeline" to exploitation? What specific factors make Indigenous youth in care vulnerable?
  3. Why have Indigenous communities had to create their own search and patrol efforts (Drag the Red, Bear Clan Patrol)? What does this reveal about the relationship between Indigenous peoples and Canadian institutions?
  4. The National Inquiry called the MMIWG crisis "deliberate race, identity and gender-based genocide." Do you agree with this classification? Why or why not?
  5. Which of the 231 Calls for Justice do you think is most urgent? Explain your reasoning.
