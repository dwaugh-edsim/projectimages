The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP)

Adopted by the UN General Assembly on September 13, 2007, UNDRIP establishes a framework of minimum standards for the survival, dignity, and well-being of Indigenous peoples worldwide. Canada officially endorsed it in 2016 and passed the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act in 2021.

UNDRIP is not a new set of rights — it articulates existing human rights as they apply to the specific situations of Indigenous peoples. It is a floor, not a ceiling.

Key Articles (46 total)

  • Art. 1: Right to full enjoyment of all human rights
  • Art. 3: Right to self-determination
  • Art. 4: Right to autonomy in internal affairs
  • Art. 5: Right to maintain distinct political, legal, economic, social, and cultural institutions
  • Art. 8: Right to not be subjected to forced assimilation or cultural destruction
  • Art. 10: Right to not be forcibly removed from lands/territories
  • Art. 11: Right to practise and revitalize cultural traditions
  • Art. 14: Right to establish and control educational systems in their own languages
  • Art. 15: Right to media free from discrimination
  • Art. 19: Free, prior, and informed consent before adopting legislative/administrative measures affecting them
  • Art. 26: Rights to lands, territories, and resources they have traditionally owned or used
  • Art. 28: Right to redress (restitution, compensation) for lands taken without consent
  • Art. 29: Right to conservation and protection of the environment
  • Art. 31: Right to maintain, control, protect, and develop cultural heritage and traditional knowledge
  • Art. 32: Free, prior, and informed consent before approving projects affecting their lands/resources
  • Art. 36: Right to maintain contacts across international borders
  • Art. 37: Right to have treaties recognized, observed, and enforced
  • Art. 40: Right to fair procedures for dispute resolution and remedies

How to Use UNDRIP to Analyze the News

When reading a news article about Indigenous issues, ask:

  1. What rights are at stake? Which articles of UNDRIP are relevant?
  2. Whose consent is needed? Was free, prior, and informed consent obtained (Art. 19, 32)?
  3. Is self-determination being respected? Are Indigenous peoples making their own decisions (Art. 3, 4)?
  4. Are treaties being honoured? Does this situation involve treaty rights (Art. 37)?
  5. What remedy is appropriate? What would justice look like under UNDRIP (Art. 28, 40)?
