The Real Pocahontas vs. Disney's Version

Disney's 1995 film Pocahontas is one of the most watched portrayals of Indigenous people in history. But the real story is almost unrecognizable compared to the animated version.

Disney's Version

  • A young woman who freely falls in love with John Smith
  • Smith is a heroic, sympathetic figure
  • A magical romance bridges two cultures
  • Both sides are equally at fault for conflict
  • Pocahontas chooses to stay with her people
  • A story of love, courage, and understanding

What Actually Happened

  • "Pocahontas" was a nickname meaning "the naughty one" — her real name was Matoaka
  • John Smith's "rescue" story was invented 17 years after the fact
  • At ~17, she was kidnapped by Captain Samuel Argall as a hostage
  • She was held captive for nearly a year
  • She married John Rolfe as a condition of her release
  • Rolfe commercialized tobacco, fueling the colonial economy
  • She was taken to England and died at age 21 in Gravesend

Disney's Pocahontas — Positives & Negatives

Positives

  • First Disney film to feature a Native American protagonist
  • Pocahontas is depicted as strong, independent, and wise — not a passive princess
  • Non-stereotypical portrayal (no teepees, no "Tonto speech")
  • Could serve as a starting point for classroom discussion about media representation

Negatives

  • Distorts real history beyond recognition
  • Endorses a "good Indian / bad Indian" binary
  • Portrays Native Americans as "savages" who need to be civilized
  • Twists the history of colonization into a happy love story
  • Makes colonizers and Natives "equal offenders" — erasing the power imbalance
  • Reinforces Western beauty standards through the "Indian princess" stereotype
The Powhatan Nation has formally objected to Disney's portrayal, stating that the film "rewrites history" and perpetuates harmful stereotypes about their ancestors and their nation's experience of colonization.

Reel Injun — 100 Years of Hollywood Stereotypes

The documentary Reel Injun (directed by Neil Diamond, a Cree filmmaker) traces how over a century of Hollywood cinema has shaped — and distorted — how the world sees Indigenous peoples.

Key Stereotypes Explored

  • The "Noble Savage": Romanticized, mystical figures living in harmony with nature — beautiful but ultimately primitive.
  • The "Hostile Indian": Faceless, violent enemies to be defeated by cowboys and soldiers — pure villain with no humanity.
  • The "Vanishing Indian": The idea that Indigenous peoples are disappearing, making their dispossession seem inevitable and natural.
  • Teepees & Horseback: Every Native American in film lives in a teepee and rides a horse — erasing the enormous diversity of Indigenous cultures (coastal, arctic, forest, plains, etc.).
  • "Tonto Speech": The broken English ("Me want peace") that became the default Hollywood "Indian" dialect — a language that never existed.

Consequences

These stereotypes have had profound real-world consequences: they have affected Indigenous children's self-esteem, influenced public policy, justified land theft, and made it difficult for Indigenous peoples to be seen as modern, complex human beings.

The documentary highlights Atanarjuat: The Fast Runner (2001), an Inuit film entirely in Inuktitut, as "the most Native movie ever made" — because it tells an Indigenous story from an Indigenous perspective, in an Indigenous language, for Indigenous audiences first.

The "Shooting a Dead Indian" Scene

One of the documentary's most powerful moments examines a John Wayne film where a settler shoots a dead Native American. The scene is analyzed for what it reveals about how Hollywood dehumanized Indigenous peoples — even in death, the Native body is treated as a prop, not a person.

Treaty Education — Understanding Our Shared History

Treaty Education is about understanding the relationship between Indigenous and non-Indigenous peoples in Canada — a relationship built on treaties that are constitutionally protected and still in effect today.

Key Topics Covered in Treaty Education

  • Doctrines of Colonialism: The Doctrine of Discovery, the Indian Act, centralization policies, residential schools, and the White Paper / Hawthorn Report.
  • Constitutional Framework: The BNA Act (1867), the Constitution Act (1982), Meech Lake, and the Charlottetown Accord — and how these documents affected Indigenous rights.
  • Aboriginal Rights: Section 35 of the Constitution, the Royal Proclamation of 1763, and the legal recognition of Indigenous rights.
  • Wabanaki Confederacy: The historic alliance of Mi'kmaq, Wolastoqiyik, Abenaki, Penobscot, and Passamaquoddy nations.
  • Mi'kmaq Treaties in the Maritimes: Peace and Friendship Treaties (1725–1779) and what they mean today.
  • Mi'kmaq Change Makers: Biographies of 40+ notable Mi'kmaq figures who have shaped history.

TRUE STORY — Indigenous Voices on Film

The documentary TRUE STORY centres Indigenous voices telling the history between Indigenous and settler peoples — a history that has been hidden, rewritten, and suppressed.

  • Turtle Island's creation story and its significance
  • The role of oral history in preserving knowledge across generations
  • The central role of women, Elders, Two-Spirit, and Indigiqueer people in Indigenous societies
  • How this history connects to the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation (September 30)

Discussion Questions — The Pocahontas Myth

  1. What are the Powhatan Nation's main objections to Disney's Pocahontas? Do you think their concerns are justified?
  2. What was Pocahontas's real name? Why is it significant that she is known by a nickname given to her by others?
  3. How was Pocahontas's marriage to John Rolfe different from the Disney version? What does the real story reveal about power dynamics?
  4. Why does Disney's version make both sides "equally at fault" for the conflict? What is lost when history is presented this way?

Discussion Questions — Reel Injun

  1. How has 100 years of Hollywood cinema distorted the image of Native Americans? Give specific examples.
  2. Why does the documentary highlight Atanarjuat: The Fast Runner as "the most Native movie ever made"?
  3. Have you seen any recent films or TV shows that challenge old stereotypes? What makes them different?

Discussion Questions — Treaty Education

  1. What is the Doctrine of Discovery? How has it been used to justify colonization? Is it still relevant today?
  2. What are the Peace and Friendship Treaties? How do they differ from "land surrender" treaties?
  3. Why is Section 35 of the Constitution Act (1982) important for Indigenous peoples?
  4. Choose one Mi'kmaw Change Maker from the Treaty Education resource and share their story. Why did you choose this person?
