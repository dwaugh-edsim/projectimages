## PART 01 — THE CANADIAN RESIDENTIAL SCHOOL SYSTEM

### What Were Residential Schools?
*   These were boarding schools funded by the Canadian government and run by churches.
*   **The Main Goal:** To force Indigenous children to give up their culture, language, and beliefs and adopt white Canadian culture (forced assimilation).
*   **The Mandate:** The government's policy goal was described as *"killing the Indian in the child."* This meant separating kids from their families, banning their native languages and traditions, and replacing them with English/French and Christianity.
*   Over **130 residential schools** operated across Canada, hurting family relationships and cultural traditions for generations.

### Shubenacadie Indian Residential School (Nova Scotia)
*   This was the **only residential school built in the Maritime Provinces**.
*   **Location:** Shubenacadie, Nova Scotia (part of the Sipekne'katik district).
*   **Years Active:** February 5, 1930 – June 22, 1967 (37 years).
*   **Conditions:** Very poor. The school suffered from underfunding, freezing rooms, leaky roofs, overcrowding, and bad food (malnutrition).

### How the Schools Were Started (Policy Origins)
*   **Bagot Commission (1842–1844):** A government report that recommended taking Indigenous children from their families to force them to assimilate. This became the blueprint for residential schools.
*   **1920 Mandatory Law:** The government changed the Indian Act to make attendance at residential schools **mandatory (compulsory)**. Children were forced to go; if parents refused, child welfare agents or police would threaten to take the children away anyway.

## PART 02 — THE 1940s CENTRALIZATION POLICY

### Failed Centralization Policy
This was a failed federal policy in Nova Scotia in the 1940s that forced Mi'kmaq families off their traditional scattered lands. The government relocated them from dozens of small, self-sustaining communities into just **two central reserves**: **Eskasoni** (on Cape Breton Island) and **Sipekne'katik** (Shubenacadie).

*   **Why the Government Did It:** To save money on services like schools and medical care, and to make it easier for government agents to watch and control the Mi'kmaq (forcing them to assimilate).
*   **What Actually Happened:** The government promised new houses, jobs, and farms, but they never showed up. Families arrived to find homelessness and starvation.
*   **Mi'kmaw Resistance:** Families strongly resisted the policy and the surveillance of agents, continuing to hunt, fish, and travel.
*   **End:** The government officially stopped the centralization policy in **1949**.
*   **Legacy:** The forced moves caused generational trauma and permanently disrupted Mi'kmaq land rights and traditional livelihoods.

## PART 03 — SUGAR FALLS & THE OUTSIDE CIRCLE

### Sugar Falls — Betty Ross's Story
*Sugar Falls* by David A. Robertson is a graphic novel based on the **true story of Betty Ross (Kokum)**, a residential school survivor.
*   **Structure:** Modern-day students visit their Kokum (grandmother Betty), who shares her story after a **smudging ceremony**.
*   **Key Themes:**
    1.  **Forced Separation:** By law, parents were forced to send their kids. Betty's father was emotionally devastated but powerless against "The Law."
    2.  **Intergenerational Trauma:** Betty's mother was a survivor herself who struggled to show love because of her own trauma—the "lessons" of the school created cycles of harm.
    3.  **Cultural Erasure:** Nuns and priests punished children for speaking their language. Hair, clothes, and names were stripped away.
    4.  **Barriers to Escape:** Children who tried to run away faced severe consequences—isolation, punishment, and the threat of freezing to death.
    5.  **Manual Labor:** Children performed manual labour (farm work, laundry, kitchen chores) instead of receiving proper school lessons.
    6.  **Resistance & Resilience:** Despite the system's efforts, Betty survived and reclaimed her identity—the smudging ceremony at the start of the story represents cultural reclamation.

### The Outside Circle (Hard Road to Walk)
*The Outside Circle* by Patti LaBoucane-Benson is a graphic novel about **Joey and Pete**, two Indigenous brothers caught in gang violence and prison cycles.
*   **Central Theme: Intergenerational Trauma:** How the legacy of residential schools, the Sixties Scoop, and systemic discrimination continues to hurt Indigenous families today.
*   **Key Terms from the Story:**
    *   **"Director of Child Welfare"** — connects to the Sixties Scoop and ongoing overrepresentation of Indigenous children in foster care.
    *   **"Child Welfare Act"** — the legal rules that let child welfare agencies take kids from their families.
    *   **"Historical Trauma"** — emotional and psychological wounds that build up and affect generations of a family.

## PART 04 — THE SIXTIES SCOOP & CLASSROOM SIMULATION

### What Was the Sixties Scoop?
As residential schools began closing in the 1960s, provincial child welfare agents became the new way the government took kids. Social workers entered reserves, judged families using middle-class white standards, and took children away to be adopted by non-Indigenous families.
*   **The Disproportionate Numbers:** 
    *   **1955:** Less than 1% of children in foster care in BC were Indigenous.
    *   **1964:** **34%** of children in care in BC were Indigenous.
    *   **2012:** **68%** of children in child welfare in Alberta were Indigenous.
    *   **The "Millennial Scoop"** — Indigenous children are still overrepresented in foster care today at rates even higher than during the residential school era.
*   **The Legal Tool (Section 88):** Added to the Indian Act in 1951, Section 88 allowed provincial laws (like child welfare codes) to apply to reserves, giving social workers the legal power to apprehend kids.
*   **Same Underlying Logic:** Separation of Indigenous children from their families and culture, leading to the loss of language, identity, and cultural belonging.

### Fictional Classroom Simulation: The Annie Francis Case File
*Note: The characters and family tree below are fictional examples created for our class assignment to show how these colonial policies affected families in real life.*

*   **The Fictional 1963 Welfare Form:** In our assignment, an 8-year-old fictional child named **Annie Francis** is apprehended and renamed **Anne Peters** in files. Social workers use detached, clinical language to label poverty as bad parenting:
    *   *Systemic Bias:* Terms like *"material poverty,"* *"lacks standard municipal infrastructure,"* and *"demonstrate lack of resource management"* judged reserve families by wealthy city standards, ignoring that reserve poverty was created by government reserve rules and land loss.
*   **The Box in the Closet:** In the simulation, Annie's family keeps a box hidden in a closet wrapped in a scarf. It holds a childhood photo of Annie laughing and a hand-knit sweetgrass sweater. This shows how trauma and silence work: the memory was too painful to talk about (silence), but too precious to throw away (memory).
*   **The Fictional Family Tree: Two Ways of Erasing Identity:**
    *   **Joseph & Lena Francis** (Simulation Grandparents):
        *   **Branch A: Annie Francis** (born 1955) -> Apprehended in 1963 -> renamed Anne Peters. **Erased physically** through child welfare removal (Sixties Scoop). Granddaughter is Maya Paul.
        *   **Branch B: Mary Francis Johnson** (born 1960) -> Married a non-status man -> Lost Indian Status. **Erased legally** on paper through Indian Act status rules (the second-generation cut-off). Granddaughter is Sarah Johnson.
    *   *Comparison:* Both represent the same colonial goal of family and identity erasure. One branch is erased by physical removal, the other by legal definition.

## PART 05 — THE INDIAN RESIDENTIAL SCHOOLS SETTLEMENT AGREEMENT (IRSSA)

### What Was It?
Signed in **2006** and enacted in **2007**, it is the **largest class-action settlement in Canadian history** ($1.9–5 billion) to address the legacy of residential schools.
*   **Five Pillars:**
    1.  **Common Experience Payment (CEP):** A direct money payment to all former students who stayed at a recognized residential school.
    2.  **Independent Assessment Process (IAP):** Compensation for specific claims of sexual or physical abuse, decided in individual hearings.
    3.  **Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC):** A five-year commission traveling Canada to record history and survivor testimonies.
    4.  **Commemoration:** A fund for memorial projects (like monuments or books) to ensure history is not forgotten.
    5.  **Health and Healing Services:** Funding for counseling and support groups (like the Aboriginal Healing Foundation).

### The TRC and the 94 Calls to Action
*   The TRC operated from **2008–2015**, concluding that the residential school system was **cultural genocide**.
*   It issued **94 Calls to Action** across six areas: (1) Child Welfare, (2) Education, (3) Language and Culture, (4) Health, (5) Justice, and (6) Reconciliation.
*   **CBC's "Beyond 94" Tracking:** In April 2025, reports showed that ten years after the TRC, only **15 of 94 Calls to Action** were complete (less than 20%).

## PART 06 — TERRITORIAL SOVEREIGNTY & RESOURCE CLASHES

### 1. The Oka Crisis (1990)
*   **What Was It?** A **1990** land dispute between Mohawk people and the town of Oka, Quebec, over plans to expand a golf course on a sacred Indigenous burial ground.
*   **Duration:** Lasted 78 days (July 11 – September 26) and resulted in two deaths.
*   **Why It Matters:** The first major, violent conflict between First Nations and provincial/federal governments in the late 20th century, highlighting unresolved land claims.

### 2. Marshall Decision & Moderate Livelihood Fishing Disputes
*   **1999 Marshall Decision:** The Supreme Court of Canada confirmed that Donald Marshall Jr. (and all Mi'kmaq) had treaty rights under the 1760-61 Treaties to hunt, fish, and gather to earn a **"moderate livelihood."**
*   **The Conflict:** The Supreme Court did not define exactly what a "moderate livelihood" was, nor did it clarify who had final regulation rights. This led to ongoing clashes between Mi'kmaw fishers (exercising their self-regulated treaty rights) and federal Department of Fisheries and Oceans (DFO) agents or non-Indigenous commercial fishers over who has jurisdiction to regulate, manage, and control resource harvesting on traditional waters.

### 3. The Leo Cannabis Case (Jurisdictional Disputes)
*   **The Issue:** A clash of legal jurisdiction over businesses operating on sovereign reserve lands.
*   **The Conflict:** Indigenous business owners operating cannabis dispensaries on reserves assert that their businesses are governed by community band laws and sovereign Indigenous jurisdiction. However, provincial and federal authorities argue that provincial licensing rules and state cannabis acts must apply, leading to disputes, raids, and legal battles over who has the final authority to regulate commerce on reserve land.

## PART 07 — JUSTICE SYSTEM & RESTORATIVE JUSTICE

### Fictional Classroom Simulation: Liam's Journey
*Note: "Two Paths: Liam's Journey" is a fictional graphic novel read in class to see how the justice system works.*
*   **The Scenario:** A fictional Mi'kmaw youth in Cape Breton named Liam is arrested for Break, Enter, and Theft at "Thomas's Corner Market" (owned by Mr. Thomas) after breaking a glass pane to escape freezing cold and find food (taking bread and a flannel shirt, leaving pocket change).

### Actuarial Risk Assessment Bias
*   **The Problem:** Checklists used by booking officers score Liam as "High Risk of Re-offending" because he is unemployed and has no permanent address (couch surfing).
*   **Systemic Bias:** These pre-trial checklists score poverty (lack of housing or jobs) as a risk factor. This means the system penalizes people for being poor, rather than checking if they have criminal intent.
*   **Incarceration Trends (Widening Gap):** In 2017 class notes, Indigenous men represented 25.2% of federal admissions, and women represented 36.1%. By 2025, these numbers rose: Indigenous adults represent **33%** of federal custody, and Indigenous women represent **50%** of federal custody, despite making up only 5% of Canada's population.
*   **TRC Call to Action #30:** Asks the government to commit to eliminating the overrepresentation of Indigenous people in custody.

### Real Precedents: Donald Marshall Jr. & Gladue Rights
*   **Donald Marshall Jr.:** A real Mi'kmaw teenager wrongfully convicted of murder in **1971** in Sydney, NS. He spent 11 years in prison.
*   **1989 Royal Commission:** Proved that systemic racism in the legal system caused Donald Marshall Jr.'s wrongful conviction, forcing the province to establish restorative justice groups like the **Mi'kmaw Legal Support Network (MLSN)**.
*   **Gladue Rights:** A legal right requiring judges to consider colonial history and seek alternatives to jail. It is a right to *substantive equality* (different accountability, not less).
*   **Gladue Funding Gap:** The lack of funding for reserve housing, mental health, and healing programs means these alternatives often do not exist, returning judges to incarceration.

### Comparing the Two Paths of Justice
*   **Western Adversarial Court (The Cold Path):**
    *   *Goal:* Punishment, isolation, deterrence (retributive justice).
    *   *Participants:* Lawyers, elevated judge in robes, police. The accused sits passively.
    *   *Focus:* What law was broken? Who did it? How much jail time is required?
    *   *Physical Structure:* Hierarchical (elevated judge's bench).
    *   *Outcome:* Criminal record, isolation, high rate of re-offending.
*   **Sentencing Circle (The Restorative Path):**
    *   *Goal:* Healing, repairing the harm, restoring relationships.
    *   *Participants:* Offender, victim, Elder, Judge, MLSN support, community.
    *   *Focus:* What harm was done? How do we repair it?
    *   *Physical Structure:* Non-hierarchical (chairs placed in circle at same height). Center holds a sweetgrass braid (harmony) and an eagle feather (speaking absolute truth).
    *   *Outcome:* Active repair plan (e.g. 40 hours of storefront repair, housing support). Reduces re-offending by **15–20%**.
*   **Bill C-5 (2022):** Removed mandatory minimum sentences for several offences, encouraging restorative outcomes.

### Msit No'kmaq (All My Relations)
*   **Meaning:** The Mi'kmaw worldview of interconnectedness between all living things, families, and history. Kinship is a circle that cannot be cut.
*   **Visual Contrast:** In *Two Paths*, shown through the contrast between the cold grey walls of court/jail (Chapter 5) and the warm golden sunset on the banks of the Shubenacadie River (Chapter 16).

## GLOSSARY — KEY TERMS

*   **Assimilation:** Forcing a minority group to give up their culture and adopt the culture of the dominant group.
*   **Indian Act:** 1876 federal legislation governing status, bands, and reserves; contains discriminatory provisions.
*   **Section 88:** 1951 amendment to the Indian Act that allowed provincial child welfare laws to apply on reserves.
*   **Sixties Scoop:** Mass removal of Indigenous children by child welfare agencies (1960s–1980s) into non-Indigenous families.
*   **IRSSA:** Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement (2006) — largest class-action settlement in Canadian history.
*   **TRC:** Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2008–2015) — documented residential school history and issued 94 Calls to Action.
*   **Intergenerational Trauma:** Trauma passed from survivors to their descendants through parenting, community disruption, and systemic factors.
*   **Centralization:** Failed 1940s Canadian federal policy forcing Mi'kmaq onto central reserves (Eskasoni and Sipekne'katik).
*   **Oka Crisis:** 1990 land dispute between Mohawk people and Oka, Quebec, over golf course expansion on burial grounds.
*   **Gladue Rights:** The constitutional right of Indigenous offenders to have their background factors considered, requiring judges to seek alternatives to jail.
*   **Substantive Equality:** Treating people differently to achieve equal outcomes, recognizing that treating everyone identically can reinforce inequalities.
*   **Sentencing Circle:** A community-led restorative sentencing process where the offender, victim, community members, and judge sit at equal level to agree on a healing and repair plan.
*   **Eagle Feather:** A sacred item in Mi'kmaw culture; holding it in a circle signifies speaking absolute truth directly to the Creator.
*   **Msit No'kmaq:** "All My Relations" — the Mi'kmaw worldview of interconnectedness between all living things, families, and history.
*   **Actuarial Risk Assessment:** Checklists used by courts that score systemic challenges (unemployment, lack of housing) as risk factors, penalizing poverty.
*   **MLSN:** Mi'kmaw Legal Support Network — established in NS following the Marshall Inquiry to support Indigenous people in the justice system.

## PRACTICE QUESTIONS — ANSWER KEYS

### Q1: In our Sixties Scoop simulation, why is a name change from Annie Francis to Anne Peters in government child welfare files such a significant and disturbing detail? What does it show about identity?
**Answer:** The name change represents total cultural erasure and forced assimilation. By changing a child's name in official files, the government physically and bureaucratically cuts their ties to their family and history. It makes the child "disappear" on paper, making it very hard for them to find their family or reclaim their status under the Indian Act later in life.

### Q2: In the class simulation, what does Annie's "box in the closet" wrapped in a scarf tell us about how trauma, memory, and silence work?
**Answer:** The hidden box represents intergenerational trauma and silence. The fact that the box was hidden shows the pain of child removal was too deep to speak about out loud (silence). But the fact that she kept the sweetgrass sweater and childhood photo for decades shows that preserving the memory of love and belonging was too important to throw away (resistance against erasure).

### Q3: Using our class simulation characters, explain how Sarah Johnson's legal status case and Annie Francis's child apprehension case show the same colonial goal.
**Answer:** Both cases represent the goal of erasing Indigenous identity using different tools. Mary's branch (Sarah's mother) represents **legal erasure** on paper (losing Indian status under discriminatory Indian Act rules). Annie's branch represents **physical erasure** (being taken from the reserve and placed in foster care). Both methods served to separate the family from their reserve land, kinship circles, and cultural rights.

### Q4: Why does a bail assessment checklist that labels an unemployed or couch-surfing person as "high risk" represent systemic bias? How does it penalize poverty?
**Answer:** Pre-trial checklists score people as "high risk" (meaning they shouldn't be released) if they lack a permanent address, are unemployed, or couch-surf. These factors are actually signs of poverty and systemic inequities, not proof of criminal intent. By treating poverty as a risk factor, the system penalizes people for not having money or stable housing, keeping them locked up before their trial has even started.

### Q5: Contrast the goals, physical setup, and role of participants in a Western court versus a Mi'kmaw Sentencing Circle.
**Answer:** 
*   **Western Court:** Focuses on punishment (jail time). It is hierarchical (judge sits high up on a bench) and adversarial (lawyers argue, the accused sits isolated and stays silent). It asks: *What law was broken and who did it?*
*   **Sentencing Circle:** Focuses on healing and fixing the harm. It is circular (everyone sits at the same height, showing equal value) and collaborative (victim, offender, Elder, judge, and community work together). It asks: *What harm was done and how do we repair it?*

### Q6: What is the "Gladue funding gap" and why does it stop the legal system from achieving substantive equality?
**Answer:** While Gladue Rights require judges to look for community-led alternatives to jail, a lack of government funding for Indigenous-led programs (like reserve housing, counseling, and healing centers) means these alternatives often do not exist. This is the **funding gap**: the court has a legal obligation to look for options, but has no actual programs to send people to, forcing judges to return to standard prison sentences. This prevents real substantive equality.

### Q7: Explain the ongoing tension between Indigenous self-determination (self-rule) and Canadian federal/provincial governments regarding territorial/resource control. What are some key examples of these clashes we covered in class?
**Answer:** The conflict revolves around who has the right to make decisions and govern Indigenous lands, resources, and communities. Indigenous peoples assert their inherent right to self-determination and self-governance, while federal and provincial authorities attempt to impose state-level jurisdiction and licensing. Key examples of these clashes covered in class include:
*   **The 1940s Centralization Policy:** Federal relocation of Mi'kmaq families to central reserves (Eskasoni and Sipekne'katik) to ease state control and surveillance.
*   **The Marshall Fishery Decision & Fishing Disputes:** Clashes between Mi'kmaw fishers (asserting self-regulated treaty rights to earn a moderate livelihood) and federal DFO agents or commercial fishers over who regulates the resource.
*   **The Leo Cannabis Case:** Clashes over reserve businesses where dispensary owners assert sovereignty and band laws to trade, while provincial police raid shops to enforce state licensing acts.
*   **The Oka Crisis (1990):** Mohawk resistance against a local municipality attempting to expand a golf course onto sacred burial grounds.
