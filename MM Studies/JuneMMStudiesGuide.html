Residential Schools & Centralization

The history of church boarding schools and the centralization reserve plans

June 8, 2026 Part 1 & 2

What Were Residential Schools?

Residential schools were boarding schools funded by the Canadian government and run by churches. The main goal was to force Indigenous children to give up their culture, language, and traditions and act like white Canadians (forced assimilation).

The Policy Mandate:

The policy goal behind these schools was explicitly described by politicians as "killing the Indian in the child." This meant separating kids from their parents, banning their native languages and spiritual practices, and replacing them with English or French instruction and the Christian religion.

Over 130 residential schools operated across Canada, causing deep, long-lasting damage to families and communities.

Shubenacadie Indian Residential School (Nova Scotia)

This was the only residential school in the Maritime Provinces. It operated in Shubenacadie (the Sipekne'katik district of Nova Scotia) from February 5, 1930, to June 22, 1967 (for 37 years). The school was notorious for terrible conditions: freezing temperatures, leaky roofs, severe overcrowding, chronic underfunding, and very poor food (malnutrition).

How the Schools Were Started (Policy Origins)

  • Bagot Commission (1842–1844): A government report that recommended taking Indigenous children away from their families to force them to assimilate. This became the blueprint for residential schools.
  • 1920 Mandatory Law: The government changed the Indian Act to make residential school attendance compulsory (mandatory). Parents were forced to send their kids; if they refused, child welfare agents or police would threaten to take the children away anyway.

Failed 1940s Centralization Policy

Centralization was a failed 1940s Canadian federal policy that forced Mi'kmaq families off their traditional scattered homelands across Nova Scotia. The government relocated families from dozens of self-sustaining communities into just two central reserves: Eskasoni and Sipekne'katik (Shubenacadie).

Why the Government Did It

• Save money on providing services like schools, housing, and healthcare by packing everyone into two spots.

• Make it easier for government agents to watch, supervise, and control the Mi'kmaq, helping force them to assimilate.

What Actually Happened

• Promises of housing, jobs, and farms never showed up. Families faced homelessness and starvation.

• The Mi'kmaq strongly resisted the policy and the surveillance of agents. The project was officially stopped in 1949, but it left a legacy of displaced land rights and trauma.

The Sixties Scoop & Simulation Case

The mass removal of children into foster care and our classroom simulation case

June 8, 2026 Part 3 & Case

What Was the Sixties Scoop?

As residential schools began closing in the 1960s, provincial child welfare agents became the new way the government took kids. The "Sixties Scoop" refers to the mass removal of Indigenous children from their families into foster care and adoption systems—usually putting them with non-Indigenous families who raised them away from their culture.

The Legal Tool (Section 88):

In 1951, the government added Section 88 to the Indian Act. This rule allowed provincial laws (like child welfare codes) to apply to reserve land, giving social workers the legal power to go onto reserves and apprehend children.

How the Numbers Grew (Disproportionate Representation)

Year / Context Region Percentage of Kids in Care Who Were Indigenous
1955 British Columbia Less than 1%
1964 British Columbia 34% (The peak of the Scoop)
2012 Alberta 68%
Modern Day Canada (National) The "Millennial Scoop" — Indigenous children are still overrepresented in foster care today at rates even higher than during the residential school era.

Classroom Simulation Case: The Apprehension of Annie Francis

Note: The case study and character tree below are fictional examples created for our classroom simulation to show how the Sixties Scoop and the Indian Act impacted families.

1. The Fictional 1963 Welfare Form

In our assignment, a fictional child named **Annie Francis** (age 8) is taken from her reserve and renamed **Anne Peters**. The form is stamped "APPREHENDED" and uses detached language to make poverty look like bad parenting:

• "Material Poverty": Criticized the family's simple lifestyle, ignoring that reserve poverty was caused by government land rules.

• "Lacks municipal infrastructure": Punished the family for not having running water, even though the government was responsible for building services on reserves.

• "Lack of resource management": Blamed parents for struggling to get by under systemic isolation.

2. The Box in the Closet (Silence vs. Memory)

In the simulation, Annie's family keeps a box hidden in a closet wrapped in a scarf. It holds a childhood photo of Annie laughing and a hand-knit sweetgrass sweater. This shows how trauma and silence work: the memory was too painful to talk about (silence), but too precious to throw away (memory).

Criminal Justice & Restorative Justice

Pre-trial checklists, real-world precedents, and comparing the two paths of justice

June 8, 2026 Part 4 & Case

Classroom Case Study: "Two Paths" (Liam's Journey)

Note: "Two Paths: Liam's Journey" is a fictional graphic novel read in class. It tells the story of Liam, a Mi'kmaw youth in Cape Breton who breaks into a store ("Thomas's Corner Market") simply to get warm and find food on a freezing winter night. We use his case to study how the actual Canadian justice system compares to restorative justice.

Pre-Trial Risk Checklist Bias

In the story, when police book Liam, a checklist scores him as "High Risk of Re-offending" because he is unemployed and couch-surfing. In real courts, these check-box tools score poverty (not having a job or house) as a risk factor. This means the system penalizes people for being poor, rather than checking if they have criminal intent.

Overrepresentation Statistics: In 2017 class records, Indigenous men made up 25.2% of federal inmates and women made up 36.1%. Today, Indigenous adults make up 33% of federal inmates, and Indigenous women make up 50% of federal custody admissions, even though Indigenous people are only 5% of Canada's population. TRC Call to Action #30 asks the government to commit to ending this overrepresentation.

Real Precedents: Donald Marshall Jr. & Gladue Rights

Donald Marshall Jr. was a real Mi'kmaw teenager who was wrongfully convicted of murder in 1971 in Sydney, NS. He spent 11 years in prison for a crime he did not commit. A 1989 Royal Commission proved that systemic racism caused his wrongful conviction, forcing Nova Scotia to establish restorative justice groups like the Mi'kmaw Legal Support Network (MLSN).

Gladue Rights & Funding Gap: A constitutional right requiring judges to look at an Indigenous person's background (like trauma and colonialism) and look for alternatives to jail. It aims for substantive equality (different accountability, not less). But because there is a funding gap (a lack of money for reserve housing, counseling, and healing programs), these alternatives are often unavailable, forcing judges to send people to jail anyway.

Conventional Court vs. Sentencing Circle

Below is a breakdown of the two paths of justice compared in our class activities.

Conventional Court (The Cold Path)
Primary Goal

Punishment, isolation, and deterring others (retributive justice).

Key Participants

Attorneys, an elevated judge in robes, and police. The accused person sits passively and is represented by others.

Focus of Inquiry

What law was broken? Who did it? How much jail time or punishment does the code require?

Physical Structure

Hierarchical. The judge sits high up on a bench to represent state authority over the people.

Real-World Outcome

A criminal record, isolation, and a high rate of returning to prison (recidivism) because the root problems are ignored.

Sentencing Circle (The Restorative Path)
Primary Goal

Healing, repairing the harm, restoring relationships, and reintegrating the offender.

Key Participants

The offender, the victim, community Elders, support workers (like MLSN), and the judge sitting at the same level.

Focus of Inquiry

What harm was done? How do we fix it? What root problems caused the behavior?

Physical Structure

Circular. Everyone sits at the same height to show equal value. A sweetgrass braid (harmony) and an eagle feather (speaking absolute truth) sit in the center.

Real-World Outcome

An active plan to repair the damage. Research shows that community-led restorative justice reduces re-offending rates by 15–20%.

Msit No'kmaq (All My Relations):

The Mi'kmaw worldview that everything in the universe is connected—families, land, history, and nature. Kinship is a circle that cannot be cut. In our study of "Two Paths," this is shown visually by comparing the cold, grey walls of the prison conveyor belt on Page 5 with the warm golden sunset on the Shubenacadie River on Page 16.

Historical Chronology Timeline

Real historical dates and events from our curriculum units

June 8, 2026 Chronology

This timeline lists real historical dates from Canadian history. Our fictional class simulation cases are set in 1963 (Annie's Scoop case) and 2027 (Liam's restorative circle case).

1749

Scalp Bounties: Governor Edward Cornwallis issues bounties offering money for Mi'kmaq scalps, showing early violent colonial tactics.

1842–1844

Bagot Commission: A government report recommends separating Indigenous children from their families to force assimilation, creating the blueprint for residential schools.

1876

Indian Act enacted: Canada passes the Indian Act, the main law used to govern reserves, status, and bands. It contains many discriminatory rules.

1920

Mandatory Attendance: The government makes attendance at residential schools mandatory (compulsory) by law for all Indigenous children.

1930

Shubenacadie Opens: The Shubenacadie Indian Residential School opens in Nova Scotia. It is the only residential school built in the Maritimes.

1942–1949

Centralization Policy: The federal government forces Nova Scotia's Mi'kmaq off their traditional scattered lands onto central reserves in Eskasoni and Sipekne'katik. Abandoned in 1949 due to severe starvation and resistance.

1951

Section 88 Added: An amendment to the Indian Act allows provincial child welfare laws to apply on reserves, opening the door for the Sixties Scoop.

1967

Shubenacadie Closes: The Shubenacadie Indian Residential School officially closes on June 22.

1960s–1980s

The Sixties Scoop Peaks: Tens of thousands of Indigenous children are taken from their parents by child welfare agencies and placed with non-Indigenous families.

1971

Donald Marshall Jr. Case: Mi'kmaw teenager Donald Marshall Jr. is wrongfully convicted of murder in Sydney, Cape Breton, and spends 11 years in prison for a crime he didn't commit.

1985

Bill C-31 Status Reform: The government amends the Indian Act to stop stripping women of status when they marry non-status men, but adds new cut-off categories (like the second-generation cut-off).

1989

Marshall Royal Commission: An official report proves that systemic racism in the legal system caused Donald Marshall Jr.'s wrongful conviction, leading to the creation of restorative justice networks in Nova Scotia.

1990

Oka Crisis begins (July 11): A 78-day standoff between Mohawk people and Oka, Quebec, over expanding a golf course on a sacred burial ground.

1996

Last Residential School Closes: The last government-run residential school in Canada closes down in Saskatchewan.

2006

IRSSA Signed: The Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement is signed. It is the largest class-action settlement in Canadian history ($5+ billion).

2008–2015

Truth and Reconciliation Commission: The TRC travels across Canada to collect testimonies. Its final report calls the residential school system "cultural genocide" and issues 94 Calls to Action.

2022

Bill C-5 Passed: Canada removes mandatory minimum sentences for several crimes, encouraging judges to look for community-led restorative justice options instead of automatic prison time.

2025

Beyond 94 Tracking: An update shows that nearly ten years after the TRC, less than 20% (only 15 of 94) of the Calls to Action are fully complete.

Vocabulary Database

Core terms, definitions, and plain language explanations

June 8, 2026 Glossary

Assimilation

Forcing a minority group to give up their culture, language, and beliefs and adopt the culture of the dominant group.

Indian Act

1876 federal legislation that governs status, bands, and reserves; contains discriminatory provisions used by the Canadian government to control and assimilate Indigenous peoples.

Section 88

A rule added to the Indian Act in 1951 that let provincial laws (like child welfare codes) apply on reserves, enabling the Sixties Scoop.

Sixties Scoop

Mass removal of Indigenous children by child welfare agencies (1960s–1980s) who were placed into non-Indigenous adoptive and foster homes away from their culture.

IRSSA

Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement (2006) — the largest class-action settlement in Canadian history ($5+ billion) to help survivors and set up the TRC.

TRC

Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2008–2015) which traveled Canada to listen to survivors, document the history of residential schools, and issue 94 Calls to Action.

Intergenerational Trauma

Trauma passed down through generations. Children of survivors grow up dealing with the emotional damage, loss of language, and family disruptions their parents went through.

Centralization

Failed 1940s Canadian federal policy that forced Nova Scotia's Mi'kmaq off their scattered lands into just two central reserves (Eskasoni and Sipekne'katik) to save money and increase control.

Oka Crisis

1990 land dispute between Mohawk people and Oka, Quebec, over a golf course expansion on a sacred burial ground; lasted 78 days with two deaths.

Gladue Rights

The legal right of Indigenous people in court to have their background factors (colonialism, family trauma) considered by the judge, who must look for alternatives to jail.

Substantive Equality

Recognizing that treating everyone exactly the same (formal equality) doesn't work if they start from unequal places. It means treating people differently to make sure outcomes are fair (like using Gladue factors).

Sentencing Circle

A community-led court meeting where the offender, victim, community Elders, and judge sit in a circle at the same level to agree on a plan to repair the damage and heal.

Eagle Feather

A sacred symbol in Mi'kmaw culture. In a circle, holding the Eagle Feather means the speaker is speaking their absolute, honest truth directly to the Creator.

Msit No'kmaq

"All My Relations" — the Mi'kmaw belief that all things (people, history, land, nature) are connected in a circle of kinship that cannot be cut.

Actuarial Risk Assessment

A checklist used by booking officers to decide if someone can go home before trial. These lists score being poor (no job, no house) as "high risk," which unfairly penalizes poverty.

MLSN

Mi'kmaw Legal Support Network — a group created in Nova Scotia after the Donald Marshall Jr. inquiry to help Indigenous people get fair treatment in the legal system.

Exam Prep Quiz & Practice Analysis

Key inquiry questions and revealable answer keys

June 8, 2026 Exam Prep
Q1: In our Sixties Scoop simulation, why is a name change from Annie Francis to Anne Peters in government child welfare files such a significant and disturbing detail? What does it show about identity?

Answer Key (Fictional Example Analysis): In our class assignment, this name change represents cultural erasure. By changing a child's name in official files, the government physically and bureaucratically cuts their ties to their family and history. It makes the child "disappear" on paper, making it very hard for them to find their family or reclaim their status under the Indian Act later in life.

Q2: In the class simulation, what does Annie's "box in the closet" wrapped in a scarf tell us about how trauma, memory, and silence work?

Answer Key (Fictional Example Analysis): The hidden box represents intergenerational trauma and silence. The fact that the box was hidden shows the pain of child removal was too deep to speak about out loud (silence). But the fact that she kept the sweetgrass sweater and childhood photo for decades shows that preserving the memory of love and belonging was too important to throw away (resistance against erasure).

Q3: Using our class simulation characters, explain how Sarah Johnson's legal status case and Annie Francis's child apprehension case show the same colonial goal.

Answer Key (Fictional Example Analysis): Both cases represent the goal of erasing Indigenous identity using different tools. Mary's branch (Sarah's mother) represents **legal erasure** on paper (losing Indian status under discriminatory Indian Act rules). Annie's branch represents **physical erasure** (being taken from the reserve and placed in foster care). Both methods served to separate the family from their reserve land, kinship circles, and cultural rights.

Q4: Why does a bail assessment checklist that labels an unemployed or couch-surfing person as "high risk" represent systemic bias? How does it penalize poverty?

Answer Key: Pre-trial checklists score people as "high risk" (meaning they shouldn't be released) if they lack a permanent address, are unemployed, or couch-surf. These factors are actually signs of poverty and systemic inequities, not proof of criminal intent. By treating poverty as a risk factor, the system penalizes people for not having money or stable housing, keeping them locked up before their trial has even started.

Q5: Contrast the goals, physical setup, and role of participants in a Western court versus a Mi'kmaw Sentencing Circle.

Answer Key:

  • Western Court: Focuses on punishment (jail time). It is hierarchical (judge sits high up on a bench) and adversarial (lawyers argue, the accused sits isolated and stays silent). It asks: *What law was broken and who did it?*
  • Sentencing Circle: Focuses on healing and fixing the harm. It is circular (everyone sits at the same height, showing equal value) and collaborative (victim, offender, Elder, judge, and community work together). It asks: *What harm was done and how do we repair it?*

Q6: What is the "Gladue funding gap" and why does it stop the legal system from achieving substantive equality?

Answer Key: While Gladue Rights require judges to look for community-led alternatives to jail, a lack of government funding for Indigenous-led programs (like reserve housing, counseling, and healing centers) means these alternatives often do not exist. This is the **funding gap**: the court has a legal obligation to look for options, but has no actual programs to send people to, forcing judges to return to standard prison sentences. This prevents real substantive equality.

Mi'kmaw Sovereignty

Before child welfare systems, residential schools, or centralization, Mi'kma'ki was an organized homeland with political relationships and strong family networks. The traditional Grand Council (Sante' Mawio'mi) guided the nation, maintaining peace and stewardship of resources based on treaty principles.
