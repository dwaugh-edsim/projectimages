3Abigail (ABIS)(not specified)The porcupine is a delicate and defensive creature who walks their land. To the Mi’Kmaq people the porcupine represents strength and self protection because the porcupine is a kind, harmless animal equipped with a shield of quills for protection. Quill drop earrings aren’t just a way to show off indigenous culture, identity, and story telling but it connects the person wearing them to the ground they live on and the strength and worth they hold inside of themself.

Traditionally Mi’Kmaq quill drop earrings were made from birch bark and porcupine quills that have been gathered after porcupine shed. They would start by cleaning the quills to remove all dirt and oils then the quills would have been boiled with roots, flowers or other types of organic dies. From there they would be flattened between finger nails or teeth, making them flexible so they could thread the end into tiny holes already made in the birch bark, fold the ends on the other side so when the bark completely dried they are secured and will not fall out.

As traditional clothing evolved, so did the jewelry and earrings. Quill drop earrings began to involve bright colored seed beads making the earrings easier to stitch and more desirable to practice. This change of medium made the materials more excessable, the process less labor intensive, much easier and faster and more detailed and intricate designs could be achieved with these beads. This allowed Mi’Kmaq people to express their culture and stories in a much more effective and enjoyable way but that does not mean the traditional process is still not used today.

5BAASHIQ (NINE)(not specified)On an August morning in 1993, Donald Marshall Jr. and a companion launched a small outboard motorboat into the coastal waters of Pomquet Harbour, Nova Scotia, to fish for eels. They landed 463 pounds of eel, which they sold for $787.10. Marshall was arrested after being charged under the federal Fisheries Act and the Maritime Provinces Fishery Regulations for fishing without a licence, selling eels without a licence, and using an illegal net during the closed season.
Marshall argued that he had a treaty right to fish which exempted him from compliance with the regulations. He was convicted by Nova Scotia's Provincial Court in 1996, with his conviction upheld by the Court of Appeal in 1997. He then appealed to the Supreme Court of Canada. Justice Binnie, writing for the majority, overturned the convictions, holding that the Mi'kmaq did have treaty rights based on the 1760–61 treaties to catch and sell fish, including eels.
The seven panel comic directly connects to this incident, leading the viewer through each phase of Marshall’s story, from the quiet dawn on Pomquet Harbour in Panel 1, to the bucket of eels and humble sale in Panel 2, to the arrest and courtroom defeats in Panels 3 and 4, to the Supreme Court victory and lasting legacy in Panels 5 through 7. Each panel is not simply an illustration it is a chapter of a story about Mi'kmaw power, resilience, and the right of a people to live from their ancestral waters. The colours shift deliberately from the warm ocean blues of freedom to the cold institutional reds of colonial law, and back again to the golden greens of victory mirroring Marshall's own journey from the harbour, through injustice, and into history.

12Kyle Parsons (PUCK)(not specified)The Mi’kmaw Treaties of 1778 and 1779 were important agreements between the Mi’kmaq people and the British government during the American Revolutionary War. These treaties were created to keep peace between the Mi’kmaq and the British and to make sure the Mi’kmaq would not join the American rebels in fighting against Britain.
The Treaty of 1778 was written and signed at Fort Howe, which is now part of Saint John, New Brunswick. The treaty was organized by Michael Francklin, the British Superintendent of Indian Affairs in Nova Scotia. He met with Mi’kmaq leaders from several communities, including Richibucto, Miramichi, and Chignecto. The Mi’kmaq chiefs signed the treaty on behalf of their people, while British officials signed it for the Crown.
A second treaty was signed in 1779. This agreement renewed the promises made in 1778 and included Mi’kmaq leaders from other communities in the region. Like the earlier treaty, it was arranged by British officials and signed by both Mi’kmaq representatives and British authorities.
The main purpose of these treaties was to maintain peace and friendship. The Mi’kmaq agreed not to support the American rebels, and the British promised to continue peaceful relations with the Mi’kmaq. The treaties also recognized that the Mi’kmaq could continue hunting, fishing, and using natural resources to support their way of life.
One of the most important facts about the 1778 and 1779 treaties is that the Mi’kmaq did not give up their land. Unlike many later treaties in Canada, these agreements focused on peace, cooperation, and trade rather than land surrender.
Today, the treaties remain important because they are still recognized by Canadian law. They help protect Mi’kmaw treaty rights and are an important part of the history of Atlantic Canada.

In my project i am going to draw about where the treaty was signed and the people signing it, a close replica of the actual treaty, and what they treated did for the Mi’kmaw people.

14Sam (WAVE)(not specified)The Mikmaw canoe was one of the most important tools used by the Mikmaw people for transportation, fishing, hunting and trading. It was carefully designed to be able to travel through rivers, lakes and coastal waters. Traditional Mikmaw canoes were usually made from birch bark stretched over a light wooden frame. The bark was sewn together with spruce roots to keep the canoe waterproof. Because the materials were lightweight, the canoe could easily be carried between waterways during long journeys on land because of its very narrow body and lightweight. The shape of the Mikmaw canoe was different from many other Indigenous canoes. It had high curved ends and a narrow body that allowed it to move quickly and smoothly through the water, and when the other cultures' canoes or boats were built differently they wouldn't be able to fit through the narrow rivers or while carrying them on land that would cause the canoes to break over time. The canoe helped connect Mikmaw communities and supported trade with neighbouring nations. Building a canoe required skill, patience and teamwork. Knowledge was passed down through generations of building their traditional way around in a canoe. Canoes reflected the Mikmaw relationship with nature because builders used natural resources respectfully and carefully using all they could with what they had. Today the Mikmaw canoe remains as an important symbol of Indigenous culture, survival and their tradition. Many communities still continue to teach canoe building techniques to preserve this important part of Mikmaw heritage.

17Zaid (ZAID)(not specified)A dream catcher is a traditional indigenous protective talisman consisting of a handmade hoop woven with a web-like pattern and decorated with items such as feathers and beads. Good dreams pass through the center hole and slide down the feather to the sleeper. Bad dreams become tangled in the web and evaporate at sunrise. People place dream catchers in their houses, cars, hallways, or above their beds to filter out bad dreams and protect those sleeping. Dream catchers are also displayed to express indigenous heritage and to show that the Mi’kmaw were the first humans on the land.
The dream catcher, also called “pewitekemkewey,” originated with the Ojibwe. It holds deep modern and cultural significance to the Mi’kmaq today. Dream catchers represent healing, cultural reclamation, community resilience, and the lasting presence of indigenous people's ancestral territory in Mi’kma’ki. According to traditional Ojibwe teachings, the universe is watched over by Asibikaashi, the Spider Woman. She was the fierce protector and custodian of all the people, especially the infants and the small children.When the Ojibwe nation spread across North America, women found it difficult to care for their children alone. To ensure their children’s protection, mothers and grandmothers began weaving magical webs using willow hoops and sinew to catch harm and negative dreams.materials needed to make the dream catcher are a circular hoop,a circular hoop (like a metal ring or embroidery hoop), leather or suede cord, string, craft glue, feathers, and beads.
