Q1 — Actuarial Bias & Prison DemographicsIndigenous men increase from 25.2% to 33%, while Indigenous women increase from 36.1% to 50%. Both groups are overrepresented, but Indigenous women have widened faster and are now disproportionately more overrepresented than men. This is often because women in the justice system are more likely to be criminalized for survival-related issues linked to poverty, housing instability, and trauma (such as survival offences or substance-related charges). Actuarial risk scoring is biased because it treats poverty, unstable housing, and unemployment as “risk factors,” even when they are the result of colonial policies, not criminal intent. This means the system punishes social conditions rather than actual dangerous behaviour.
Q2 — TRC Call to Action #30TRC Call to Action #30 demands that the federal government eliminate the overrepresentation of Indigenous people in custody by addressing the causes, including systemic discrimination, and implementing alternatives to imprisonment grounded in Indigenous approaches. This connects to residential school trauma (as seen in Sugar Falls) because survivors experienced disrupted parenting, loss of language, and intergenerational trauma. Those effects contributed to cycles of poverty and justice involvement, meaning the justice system is responding to harms that were originally created by earlier government policies.
Q3 — Donald Marshall Jr.Donald Marshall Jr. was a Mi’kmaw man from Nova Scotia who was wrongfully convicted of murder in 1971 in Sydney, Nova Scotia. He spent 11 years in prison before being exonerated. The Royal Commission concluded that the justice system failed him due to police misconduct, systemic racism, and confirmation bias, meaning investigators ignored evidence that could have proven his innocence. His case revealed deep structural racism in Canadian courts and led to major justice reforms.
Q4 — Gladue Rights & Funding GapSubstantive equality means that courts must treat Indigenous offenders in a way that recognizes historical and systemic disadvantages, not just apply the same punishment standards as everyone else. Gladue Rights require judges to consider background factors like residential school history, foster care, and poverty. The “Gladue funding gap” refers to the lack of funding for community-based alternatives like healing programs, counselling, and restorative justice options. Without these resources, judges often have no practical alternatives to jail, even when they want to apply Gladue principles.
Q5 — Sentencing Circle DesignA sentencing circle removes hierarchy: everyone sits in a circle at equal height without robes or elevated benches, unlike a Western courtroom where the judge sits above others and lawyers are separated. The circle represents equality and shared responsibility. The Eagle Feather is passed around and only the person holding it may speak. It symbolizes truth, respect, and honesty, and is considered sacred in Mi’kmaw culture, meaning speakers are expected to speak sincerely.
Q6 — Conventional vs. Restorative JusticeA conventional court focuses on punishment, legal guilt, and law enforcement, with the state acting as the main authority. A sentencing circle focuses on healing, accountability, and repairing harm, involving the victim, offender, and community. In a circle, the victim has a voice in the process and the community participates in creating a healing plan, rather than being passive observers while the state decides punishment.
Q7 — Bill C-5 & RecidivismIndigenous-led restorative justice programs reduce recidivism by approximately 30–50% compared to incarceration (depending on the program). However, changing laws like Bill C-5 is not enough because deeper issues, such as poverty, lack of housing, addiction services, education gaps, and intergenerational trauma, are still present. Without addressing these root causes, people may still end up back in the justice system even after alternative sentencing.
Q8 — Msit No'kmaq & Two PathsMsit No’kmaq means “All My Relations”, referring to the interconnectedness of all people, animals, land, and community. The sweetgrass braid represents this connection, as it symbolizes unity and balance among mind, body, and spirit. The contrast between the cold grey walls (earlier chapters) and the warm golden sunset (final chapter) represents two paths of justice: one based on punishment, isolation, and control, and the other based on healing, community, and relational responsibility.
Q9 — Maya Paul ConnectionLiam’s housing insecurity and status issues connect back to policies that restricted Indigenous land access and imposed status rules. From Maya Paul’s case, reserve confinement and Indian Act status codes limited economic opportunity and housing development, creating long-term poverty. These conditions later show up in Liam’s case as “risk factors,” even though they were historically created by government systems.
Q10 — Leo 1752 Treaty ConnectionThe override of Mi’kmaw authority in Liam’s case reflects the same jurisdictional conflict seen in Leo’s 1752 Treaty case. Despite treaty rights recognizing Mi’kmaw self-governance, Canadian courts still apply provincial and federal legal authority over Indigenous communities. This limits community-based sentencing options and continues the historical pattern of the state overriding Indigenous legal systems and governance structures.
