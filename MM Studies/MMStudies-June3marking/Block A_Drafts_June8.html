9MATEO (YOLO)(not specified)The portrayal of Indigenous people in the media has changed over time, but many challenges still remain. historically, Indigenous peoples were often represented through stereotypes that misrepresented their cultures, traditions, and ways of life. movies, television shows, newspapers, and advertisements frequently depicted Indigenous people as either aggressive warriors, helpless victims, or mysterious figures connected only to the past. These portrayals ignored the diversity and complexity of Indigenous communities and contributed to misunderstandings among the public.
One major issue with media representation is the lack of Indigenous voices in the creation of content. for many years, stories about Indigenous peoples were told by non-Indigenous writers, producers, and journalists. As a result, Indigenous perspectives were often excluded or distorted. This led to inaccurate depictions that reinforced harmful stereotypes and prejudice. Such representations can influence public opinion and affect how Indigenous people are treated in society.
In recent years, there has been growing awareness of the importance of authentic representation. More Indigenous filmmakers, actors, writers, and journalists are sharing their own stories and perspectives. This has helped create more accurate and respectful portrayals of Indigenous peoples in the media. Modern films, television series, and documentaries increasingly highlight Indigenous cultures, achievements, and contemporary issues, allowing audiences to gain a deeper understanding of Indigenous communities.
Despite this progress, stereotypes and underrepresentation still exist. News coverage often focuses on negative topics such as poverty, crime, or conflict, while positive stories and achievements may receive less attention. This can reinforce harmful misconceptions and fail to reflect the full reality of Indigenous lives.
10Michael (MIKE)(not specified)This scale model represents a Clovis point spear, one of the earliest hunting tools used by Indigenous peoples in North America more than 13,000 years ago. While this model is not an exact replica, it has meny of the main features and is as accurately as possible. Some changes were made, such as shortening the wooden shaft to about 3–4 feet instead of the original 6–10 feet, making it more practical for this project.

The most important part of the spear is the Clovis point, a carefully shaped stone tip known for its distinctive fluted design. However, these points were extremely hard to make and were extremely time consuming, These points were attached to wooden shafts and were highly effective hunting tools. Creating them required great skill, showing the advanced knowledge and craftsmanship of the people who made them. People would sometimes spend days just making one spear without stopping

Clovis point spears were mainly used for hunting large animals during the Ice Age, including mammoths, mastodons, and bison. Successful hunts provided food, clothing and tools making these spears essential for survival. Because of their effectiveness, Clovis points are considered one of the most significant technological developments of the Mi'kmaq people.

Although this model is a simplified version of the original, it reflects the importance of the Clovis spear and the people who used it. By recreating this tool, we can better understand how early peoples adapted to their environment and used innovation to survive

The central image represents Rita Joe, the renowned Mi’kmaw poet whose work advocated for Indigenous identity, cultural survival, and social awareness. Surrounding and forming her portrait are images connected to Indigenous women whose lives were lost, creating a visual connection between remembrance, resilience, and advocacy. The layered composition draws directly on themes studied while learning about the Mi’kmaw, including the effects of colonialism, systemic discrimination, and the importance of preserving Indigenous stories. By merging individual faces into a larger figure, the work emphasizes how personal experiences become part of a collective history. The portrait functions as both a memorial and a symbol of resistance, highlighting the voices that continue to speak through art, literature, and community memory. The glass also suggests preservation, protecting memories that should not be forgotten. Together, these elements invite viewers to consider the relationship between remembrance and responsibility. The work honors Indigenous women, acknowledges the realities of loss and injustice, and reflects the ongoing effort to ensure that their stories, identities, and contributions remain visible for future generations.
