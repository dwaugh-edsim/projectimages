## Lena (FOOD)

### Slide 1

The formal implementation of the Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement (IRSSA) in 2007 was a critical turning point because it moved Canada beyond simple litigation toward a comprehensive, state-sanctioned framework for reconciliation. By establishing five central pillars, including the creation of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, it shifted the national focus from individual legal battles to a collective effort to document historical harms and provide healing for survivors. Furthermore, the $1.9 billion commitment represented a historic official acknowledgement of the state's role in the systematic attempt to erase Indigenous cultures, finally validating the decades-long struggle of survivors for justice.

### Slide 2

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) is the most critical pillar for long-term reconciliation because it addresses the fundamental need for public education and the creation of an official historical record. While financial compensation like the Common Experience Payment provides immediate relief, the TRC’s work in documenting survivor testimonies ensures that the systemic impacts of residential schools are never forgotten by future generations. By fostering a national dialogue and establishing the 94 Calls to Action, this pillar creates a sustainable framework for systemic change and mutual respect between Indigenous and non-Indigenous peoples. Ultimately, long-term healing is only possible when a society collectively acknowledges the truth of its past to prevent such tragedies from ever being repeated.

### Slide 3

The Shubenacadie Indian Residential School was operational for 37 years, from 1930 to 1967. As indicated in the image, the Roman Catholic Church (specifically the Sisters of Charity of Saint Vincent de Paul and the Missionary Oblates of Mary Immaculate) was responsible for its administration.

### Slide 4

it definitely would have provided Betty Ross with a lump-sum financial acknowledgement for the time she spent residing at a residential school, recognizing the general loss of childhood and culture. While the Independent Assessment Process (IAP) addressed the specific, severe abuses she witnessed and suffered, the TRC would have offered her a platform for 'Truth-Telling,' allowing her to share her story publicly to foster reconciliation and record the true history of the system. Together, these pillars of the IRSSA would have transitioned her journey from one of silent endurance to one of formal recognition and communal healing.

### Slide 5

According to the Beyond 94 tracker, projects in the "Proposed" or "Underway" phases often stall due to persistent structural barriers within government systems, including paternalism and insufficient resources. These projects frequently encounter delays when legislative and institutional frameworks lack the flexibility or commitment needed to move beyond performative measures toward concrete action. Furthermore, a lack of continuity in leadership and shifts in political priorities can cause momentum to stall, forcing initiatives to restart or proceed at a much slower pace.

### Slide 6

The most significant challenge for reconciliation in the Halifax region is the lack of meaningful progress toward Economic Reconciliation, specifically regarding the "moderate livelihood" rights promised in the 1752 Treaty. While the IRSSA and Beyond 94 tracker highlight the need for systemic change, the struggles of figures like Leo Francis demonstrate that Mi'kmaw treaty rights are often still sidelined by legal and commercial interests. To move forward, our region must bridge the gap between acknowledging historical treaties and actively supporting the Indigenous-led economic self-determination that those agreements were intended to protect. Achieving this would represent a shift from symbolic apologies to the lived reality of "Peace and Friendship" as envisioned centuries ago in Kjipuktuk.

### Slide 1

The formal implementation of the Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement (IRSSA) in 2007 was a major turning point for Canada because it marked the first official recognition of the harm caused by the residential school system. After decades of survivors fighting for justice, the agreement acknowledged that Indigenous children had been taken from their families and subjected to abuse, cultural loss, and forced assimilation. The IRSSA provided compensation, support for healing programs, and created the Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada, which allowed survivors to share their stories publicly. This helped educate Canadians about the true history and lasting effects of residential schools, while beginning the process of accountability, healing, and reconciliation between Indigenous peoples and the Canadian government.

### Slide 2

I believe the most critical pillar for long-term reconciliation was the Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada (TRC). While compensation and healing services were extremely important for survivors, the TRC helped educate the entire country about the truth of residential schools and their lasting effects on Indigenous communities. By collecting survivor testimonies and sharing them publicly, the TRC gave survivors a voice and helped many Canadians understand a history that had often been ignored or hidden. Long-term reconciliation requires more than financial compensation—it requires awareness, accountability, and a commitment to change. The TRC encouraged conversations about Indigenous rights, racism, and reconciliation that continue to influence schools, governments, and communities across Canada today.
