1. Actuarial Risk Scoring vs. Systemic Poverty

Actuarial tools score individuals as "high risk of re-offending" based on systemic factors like unemployment, lack of fixed address, or housing instability. Students often view these scores as neutral measures of criminal intent rather than systemic penalties for poverty.

"The scoring system is fair because it judges people based on their risks, but if they have no home or job they are just more likely to do crimes again."

Student Submission Quote

Why it's a Misconception: This perspective incorrectly treats risk scores as objective, neutral measures of an individual's criminality. In reality, actuarial tools penalize systemic symptoms of poverty (such as housing instability or unemployment) resulting from historical colonial legacies. By treating structural disadvantages as personal risk factors, the system pathologizes poverty and reproduces high recidivism scores instead of assessing actual criminal intent.

Class Discussion Points

  • How do actuarial risk assessment models create feedback loops that perpetuate the overrepresentation of Indigenous people in prisons?
  • What is the difference between individual criminal intent and systemic vulnerability?
Restorative Justice (Liam's Journey) Slide 3 of 6

2. Sentencing Circles vs. "Soft" Justice

Students frequently misunderstand restorative justice options like sentencing circles as informal, non-binding meetings or "getting off easy," rather than intensive processes of community-based accountability.

"Sentencing circles are good for talking and healing, but they aren't real courts so the offender isn't actually getting a real punishment or sentence."

Student Submission Quote

Why it's a Misconception: This is a misconception because it falsely assumes that "real" justice can only be punitive (jail/isolation) and that circles are informal or optional. In reality, sentencing circles are legally binding processes integrated into the Canadian Criminal Code. They often demand *greater* personal accountability, forcing the offender to sit in a circle, look their victim and community Elders in the eye, and take active, structured responsibility to repair the harm.

Class Discussion Points

  • Why might facing a victim and community Elder in a circle require *more* personal accountability than sitting silently in a retributive courtroom?
  • How does the concept of *Msit No'kmaq* (All My Relations) guide restorative justice outcomes?

3. Shubenacadie Administration: Facts Matter

Factual accuracy is critical when discussing institutional accountability, class actions, and church apologies. A recurring error was misattributing the historical administration of Shubenacadie Residential School.

"The Shubenacadie Indian Residential School in Nova Scotia operated from 1923 to 1967 under the Anglican Church of Canada..."

Student Submission Quote

Why it's a Misconception: This statement is factually incorrect because Shubenacadie Residential School was administered by the Roman Catholic Church, not the Anglican Church. While the Anglican Church did run other schools, historical precision is crucial here because legal settlements, formal church apologies, and institutional accountability are legally tied to the specific religious denomination that operated each site.

Class Discussion Points

  • Why is historical precision critical when discussing legal accountability, class actions, and church apologies?
  • How does shifting administrative blame impact local community healing and reconciliation efforts?

4. TRC Calls to Action vs. Legislative Bills

Students often confuse the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's 94 Calls to Action with parliamentary bills, assuming they are "stuck in the House or Senate" rather than policy recommendations for multiple levels of society.

"A lot of the calls that are getting stalled are because they get approved in the house of Commons and move on to the Senate where they are getting stalled."

Student Submission Quote

Why it's a Misconception: This is a misconception because it confuses commission recommendations with actual legislative bills. The 94 Calls to Action are non-binding requests directed at various levels of government, churches, and corporations. They are not 'stuck' in a legislative chamber; rather, delays are caused by political inaction, funding gaps, and a lack of coordination between federal and provincial bodies.

Class Discussion Points

  • What is the difference between a legislative bill and a commission's report/recommendations (Calls to Action)?
  • If Calls to Action are non-binding, who holds the responsibility to implement them?

5. Common Experience Payment (CEP) vs. TRC

Many students confuse the direct financial recognition of the Common Experience Payment (CEP) with the truth-telling and public record mandate of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC).

"She would be compensated a lot for the abuse she endured in residential schools by the TRC so she can tell her story..."

Student Submission Quote

Why it's a Misconception: This is a misconception because it conflates truth-sharing with financial compensation. The TRC did not have the authority or mandate to distribute money; its purpose was strictly to gather testimonies and establish a national public archive. Compensation payouts like the CEP were separate, government-administered components of the broader Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement (IRSSA).

Class Discussion Points

  • What is the difference between the role of financial redress (CEP) and cultural/historical documentation (TRC)?
  • Why is a public record of truth considered essential for healing, independent of financial compensation?
