The Fight for Status

Domestic Activism, International Pressure, and the 1985 "Compromise"

For over a century, the Indian Act enforced a patrilineal system that discriminated against Indigenous women. By the mid-1980s, a convergence of grassroots activism, international humiliation at the United Nations, and new constitutional laws forced the Canadian government to act.

1. The Discriminatory Law (Section 12(1)(b))

Until 1985, the Indian Act contained a provision known as Section 12(1)(b). It stipulated that:

  • "Marrying Out": A woman with Indian status who married a non-status man lost her status, her treaty rights, and her right to live on the reserve. She could not pass status to her children.
  • Male Privilege: An Indian man who married a non-status woman kept his status, and his wife gained status automatically.

2. Domestic Pressure: Indian Rights for Indian Women

In 1967, Indigenous women mobilized to form Indian Rights for Indian Women (IRIW). Led by figures like Mary Two-Axe Earley, Nellie Carlson, and Kathleen Steinhauer, the group fought against significant opposition from both the federal government and male-dominated Band Councils.

They faced intense challenges, including lack of funding and community ostracization, yet they succeeded in bringing the issue of gender discrimination to the national stage.

3. International Pressure: The Lovelace Case

When Canadian courts failed to protect Indigenous women (most notably in the 1973 Lavell and Bédard Supreme Court decisions), activists took their fight to the international stage.

Sandra Lovelace v. Canada (1981)

Sandra Lovelace Nicholas, a Maliseet woman from the Tobique First Nation, lost her status after marrying a non-Indigenous man. When the marriage ended, she and her children were denied housing and education on her reserve.

She brought her case to the United Nations Human Rights Committee. In 1981, the UN ruled that Canada was in violation of Article 27 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. The ruling declared that by denying Lovelace the right to live in her community, Canada was denying her the right to enjoy her culture.

This ruling was a major embarrassment for Canada on the world stage, labeling the country as a violator of human rights and adding urgency to the demand for legislative change.

4. Constitutional Pressure: The Charter (1982)

The final blow to the old Indian Act came with the repatriation of the Constitution in 1982. Section 15 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms guaranteed equality before and under the law, without discrimination based on sex.

The government was given a three-year grace period to bring laws into compliance. As the April 1985 deadline approached, the government had no choice but to amend the act to avoid having it struck down by the courts.

5. The "Compromise": Bill C-31 (1985)

Passed in June 1985, Bill C-31 was the government's response. It was framed as a "compromise" between three competing interests: Women's Rights, First Nations Self-Determination, and Government Fiscal Restraint.

The Changes

  • Reinstatement: Women who had lost status for "marrying out" were reinstated (along with their children).
  • Abolition: The "marrying out" rule was abolished; marriage no longer affected status.
  • Band Control: Bands were given control over their own membership lists (distinct from federal status).

The Compromise (The Flaws)

To limit the number of new Status Indians (and thus federal costs), the government created the "Second Generation Cut-Off."

  • Hierarchy of Status: Created two classes of citizens:
    • 6(1): Full status (can pass it on).
    • 6(2): Partial status (cannot pass it on if they marry a non-status person).
  • Residual Discrimination: Women reinstated were often placed in 6(1)(c) categories that limited their ability to pass status compared to their brothers.

Why was it a compromise?

The government argued that simply granting full status to everyone would overwhelm First Nations communities that were already underfunded. By separating "Status" (federal benefits) from "Membership" (band rights), the government attempted to satisfy women's groups by returning their legal identity, while appeasing Band Councils by allowing them to deny reinstated women entry into the community to protect limited housing and resources.
