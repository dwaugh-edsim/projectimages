Indian Status & The Indian Act

From the "Marry-Out" rule to Bill C-31 and the Second-Generation Cut-off.

The Law Before 1985: Gender Discrimination

Before 1985, the Indian Act operated almost exclusively through the male line. Whether you were legally recognized as an "Indian" depended entirely on the Status of your husband or father.

The Punishment: "Marry-Out"

If an Indigenous woman married a man who did not have Status (a non-Status man), she automatically lost her own Indian Status. Her children also lost their Status. She was effectively exiled from her community.

The Privilege: "Marry-In"

If an Indigenous man married a non-Indigenous woman (e.g., a white woman), that woman automatically gained Indian Status. Their children were guaranteed Status.

Context: When the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms was introduced in 1982, it guaranteed equality rights. The government realized the "marry-out" rule was now unconstitutional and had to change.

The Law After 1985: Bill C-31

In 1985, Bill C-31 passed to end sex discrimination. Women who lost Status got it back. However, the government introduced a complex Two-Tier System to limit the number of people who could claim status in the future.

Section 6(1) Status (The Stronger Status)

Usually someone with two parents who have Status. They can pass status to their child regardless of who they marry.

Section 6(2) Status (The Limited Status)

Someone with only one parent who has Status. They can hold Status themselves, but they have limited ability to pass it on.

The "Second-Generation Cut-Off"

The government created a rule to slowly reduce the number of Status Indians over time:

If a person registered under Section 6(2) has a child with a Non-Status person, that child is NOT eligible for Status. The line ends there.
