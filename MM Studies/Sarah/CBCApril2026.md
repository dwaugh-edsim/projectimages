How a Senate bill could end the Indian Act's 2nd-generation cut-off
Bill S-2 amendment addresses loss of status after 2 generations of 'marrying out'

For 150 years, who is considered a "Status Indian" in Canadian law has been determined by the federal government through the Indian Act.

As the Indian Act marks 150 years this month, a bill before the House of Commons could end an amendment that prevents the transfer of Indian status after two generations of "marrying out."

Bill S-2 was proposed to return status to about 3,500 people who lost it through previous entitlement clauses, to deal with issues of band registration for women who were transferred to their husband's First Nations instead of their own and to replace offensive and outdated language in the act.


However, at the urging of First Nations people who spoke before Senate committees, an amendment was added to end the second-generation cut-off and allow a "one-parent" clause to take its place, meaning status would be transferred to children as long as one of their parents had status. 

When the Indian Act became law in 1876, it defined who was an "Indian" and carried status and how one could lose their status through enfranchisement, voluntarily or involuntarily.

Women lost their status if they married non-Indigenous men. Men who married non-Indigenous women did not lose their status and their wives gained status.

Amendments In 1985 ended that, but created what is referred to as the "second-generation cut-off," where if someone has only one parent with status, they cannot pass on status to their children.

Dawn Lavell-Harvard, former president of the Native Women's Association of Canada, told The House host Catherine Cullen that Bill S-2 would have major impacts on her family.

"There's this profound gut-wrenching sadness when you realize that my grandchildren won't have the same opportunities. They won't have the same rights. They won't have the same ability to be members in our community," she said.

Lavell-Harvard said ending the second-generation cut-off is part of ensuring First Nations people have the same rights as other Canadians when it comes to citizenship.

"If we don't create a one-parent rule which means we have the right to pass on our status, our citizenship, in effect, the same way Canadian citizens do, to our children and grandchildren with each line then it means there will be eventual extinction of our First Nations," she said.

"And for many First Nations, the smaller ones, it's coming sooner rather than later."

Concerns about new registrations
But Chief Jean-Guy Whiteduck of Kitigan Zibi Anishinabeg said membership should be decided by the nation, not the government, and there are concerns about new members registering in the band without any recent connections to the community.

"People in the community have a feeling these people are coming with no knowledge of their culture and often they don't have the language and all that and it is a problem," Whiteduck said.

Nick Ottawa runs the registration office at Kitigan Zibi. He said previous legal challenges have resulted in about 1,000 new applications for band membership in recent years, and he's concerned passing S-2 could increase that number drastically.

"Sometimes it takes a lot of research to verify [community connection]," Ottawa said.

Ottawa said it's hard to explain this process to community members and to his own daughter, Hayley, who is impacted by the second-generation cut-off.

"She gets upset," Ottawa added. "If she has a child with a non-Native person, then the act says status stops there, but she goes, 'Dad, I'm Anishinabe.'"

Indigenous Services Minister Mandy Gull-Masty has said the government will approach eliminating the second-generation cut-off by increasing consultation with First Nations. (Justin Tang/The Canadian Press)
Indigenous Services Minister Mandy Gull-Masty has advocated for a slower approach to removing the second-generation cut-off and has said the federal government will need to consult with First Nations across the country.

"The question is not about whether or not we will lift second-generation [cut-off]. The question is simply about how," Gull-Masty said last month in Toronto.

"I want to respect that communities have the right to decide for themselves. If you feel that a one-parent rule supports your community, that is your path to take. I also want to be clear that there are other communities who want more than that, who want a different option."

Niigaan Sinclair, a professor of Indigenous Studies at the University of Manitoba, told Cullen he doesn't think S-2 is going to pass anytime soon.

"I don't know why … the federal government's suddenly now stuck on the issue of consultation, and is holding everything up because they're worried about all opinions of First Nations," Sinclair said.

Sinclair said the change could bring in between 100,000 and 250,000 people who have lost their status due to the Indian Act, which he said "is a Canadian problem, not a First Nations problem."

The bill is currently set to be presented in committee in the House of Commons.
