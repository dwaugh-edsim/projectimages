Core Concepts

Aboriginal people of Canada (including the Mi'kmaq) have faced problems within the justice system as a result of systemic social injustice.

Justice

The process or result of using laws to fairly punish crimes and criminals.

Social Justice

Justice in terms of the distribution of wealth, opportunities, and privileges within a society.

Injustice

Absence of justice, the violation of the rights of another, or general unfairness.

Incarceration Gaps Grow (September 2017)

This 2017 report highlights the widening divide between Indigenous and non-Indigenous inmates in federal prisons.

Gaps between Indigenous and non-Indigenous inmates growing, latest statistics show
Published: September 15, 2017 by Alison Crawford (CBC News) // Original Link

There are more Indigenous offenders in Canada's prisons and they serve more time before being released on parole than other prisoners, according to statistics from the federal Department of Public Safety.

Indigenous people are also more likely to be held in a maximum security facility, according to the Corrections and Conditional Release Statistical Overview.

In federal penitentiaries, the Indigenous population has increased by 25 percent over the past five years and almost 40 per cent since 2007.

Indigenous men represent 25.2 percent of all in-custody males, while Indigenous women represent 36.1% of all females behind bars. According to Statistics Canada, only 4.9% of Canadians are Indigenous.

And while 82.4 percent of all Indigenous offenders served their complete sentence before being released, just 65.2 percent of non-Indigenous offenders are held until their statutory release date.

In the same statement, the Justice Minister expressed hopes for an ongoing review of the criminal justice system: "This involves addressing gaps in services to Indigenous people, including increasing the use of restorative justice processes and other initiatives to reduce the rate of incarceration of Indigenous persons."

Restorative Justice Principles

Restorative justice is a response to crime that focuses on restoring the losses suffered by victims, holding offenders accountable for the harm they have caused, and building peace within communities. It is a different way of thinking about crime.

Key Functions

  • It focuses on the harm caused by crime: repairing the harm done to victims and reducing recidivism by preventing crime.
  • It is achieved through a cooperative effort by both offenders and the communities.
  • It requires offenders to take responsibility for their actions and for the crime that they have caused.

Four Core Values

  1. Encounter: Create opportunities for victims, offenders, and community members who want to do so to meet to discuss the crime and its consequences.
  2. Amends: Expect offenders to take steps to repair the harm they have caused.
  3. Reintegration: Seek to restore victims and offenders as whole, contributing members of society.
  4. Inclusion: Provide opportunities for parties involved in a specific crime to participate in its resolution.

Benefits of Restorative Justice

  • It views criminal acts more holistically: rather than defining crime only as lawbreaking, it recognizes that offenders harm victims.
  • It involves more parties: rather than giving key roles only to the government and offender, it involves the victim and community.
  • It measures success differently: rather than measuring how much punishment has been inflicted, it measures how much harm has been repaired or prevented.
  • It recognizes the importance of community participation and initiative rather than leaving the problem of crime alone to the government.

Globe & Mail Case Study Inquiry (2017–2018)

Based on the archival Globe and Mail article on Indigenous Incarceration rates and proposed reforms:

Currently, what percentage of female prisoners in Canada are Indigenous women?
2017/2018 Status: 50% of female prisoners in Canada are Indigenous, while they only make up 4.9% of the Canadian female population.
What criticism has the government faced regarding this issue?
Critics point out that there have not been enough concrete government policies or resources to respond to the problem beyond public statements and legal commissions.
What bill is the government proposing to address the issue? What would that bill do?
Bill C-5: This bill proposes undoing aspects of the previous "tough on crime" agenda. It would repeal 20 different mandatory minimum sentences (especially for non-violent drug offenses and firearm charges) and re-establish conditional sentences like house arrest so that judges can consider individual circumstances.
The government has placed blame on previous policy decisions. What measures did they take?
Between 2007 and 2012, the federal government introduced a series of rigid mandatory minimum sentences. This occurred despite warnings from lawyers and civil rights groups that they violate the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and disproportionately affect Indigenous and Black people, leading to numerous constitutional challenges.
What are mandatory minimum sentences? How do they affect incarceration rates?
Mandatory minimum sentences are legislated penalties that predetermine the least severe punishment a judge can give for a specific crime. Because they restrict judicial discretion, they keep incarceration rates high, preventing judges from considering historical and personal contexts.
Why are some Indigenous activists unsatisfied with the proposed bill?
They argue that removing mandatory minimums is only a legal Band-Aid. True change requires addressing systemic root causes like guaranteed livable income, housing security, access to healthcare, clean drinking water, and historical intergenerational trauma. Without funding these community resources, courts have no real alternatives to jail.
