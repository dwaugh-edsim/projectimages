Reference Data Sheet

Indigenous Peoples and Canadian Justice Statistics

Published: May 2026 Course Context: Mi'kmaw Studies 11

This resource compiles verified, modern statistical data regarding the overrepresentation of Indigenous peoples (including the Mi'kmaq) within the Canadian criminal justice system. It is designed to replace outdated 2011/2016 figures in older classroom notes.

1. Incarceration Disparity & General Custody

Data released by Statistics Canada in 2026 shows that the representation of Indigenous adults in custody has continued to increase, representing a critical systemic gap.

10x
Incarceration rate of Indigenous adults compared to non-Indigenous adults nationally.
33.2%
Proportion of provincial custodial inmates who identify as Indigenous (despite being only 4.3% of the population in those provinces).

The Provincial Disparity (Nova Scotia Context)

Across key surveyed provinces, the average daily incarceration rate was 89 per 10,000 for Indigenous adults, compared to just 8 per 10,000 for non-Indigenous adults. The highest provincial disparity was reported in Saskatchewan (where Indigenous adults are incarcerated at a rate 19.4 times higher), but Atlantic Canada and Nova Scotia continue to show widening margins between Indigenous and non-Indigenous custodial admissions.

2. Federal Prisons & Gender Breakdown

The overrepresentation is most severe in federal penitentiaries (for individuals serving sentences of two years or more), particularly among Indigenous women.

50.0%
Of all federally incarcerated women identify as Indigenous (up from 36.1% in 2017).
27.0%
Of federally incarcerated men identify as Indigenous (up from 25.2% in 2017).

This means that although Indigenous women make up less than 5% of the total female population in Canada, they now represent exactly half of all federally incarcerated women. In some individual institutions, such as the Edmonton Institution for Women, this proportion has exceeded 70%.

3. Legislative Status: Bill C-5

In older classroom files, Bill C-5 was discussed as a "proposal." Here is the updated historical and operational timeline as of 2026:

  • Enacted: Bill C-5 was formally passed by Parliament and received Royal Assent on November 17, 2022.
  • Core Changes: The bill repealed mandatory minimum sentences (MMS) for 14 offences in the Criminal Code (specifically simple drug possession and certain firearm offences with no injury/threat). It also expanded the use of conditional sentences (such as house arrest) to allow judges to consider individual background factors (like Gladue factors) during sentencing.
  • Current Evaluation (2026): While Bill C-5 restored judicial discretion, the overall incarceration rate for Indigenous people has not significantly declined. Justice advocates (such as the Canadian Association of Elizabeth Fry Societies) point out that reforming sentencing laws alone cannot resolve overrepresentation without addressing the social determinants of health, housing, and structural poverty.
