# Shubenacadie Residential School and the Life of Mi’kmaw Activist Nora Bernard

## Executive Overview

The Shubenacadie Indian Residential School, located in Sipekne’katik district of Mi’kma’ki near Shubenacadie, Nova Scotia, was the only federally run Indian residential school in the Maritime provinces and operated from 1930 to 1967. It was central to the Canadian government’s policy of forcibly assimilating Indigenous children, particularly Mi’kmaq and Wolastoqiyik, by suppressing their languages and cultures and separating them from their families. Mi’kmaw activist and Shubenacadie survivor Nora Bernard transformed her own experience of harm at this school into a landmark legal and political struggle that resulted in the largest class‑action settlement in Canadian history for residential school Survivors.[1][2][3][4][5]

This report provides (1) a concise history of the Shubenacadie Indian Residential School, including its operation, abuses, and later recognition as a National Historic Site, and (2) a biography of Nora Bernard, with emphasis on her childhood, activism, and the circumstances of her death. It is intended as a classroom‑ready background document that centres Mi’kmaw experiences and the ongoing legacy of residential schools.

## Shubenacadie Indian Residential School: Origins and Purpose

The former Shubenacadie Indian Residential School was built in 1928–29 on a hill between Highway 2 and the Shubenacadie River, approximately seven kilometres from Sipekne’katik First Nation (Indian Brook) in Nova Scotia. It opened to students in 1930 and remained in operation until 1967, making it the only residential school in the Maritimes.[6][3][1]

The school was funded and overseen by the federal government, initially managed by the Roman Catholic Archdiocese of Halifax and later by the Missionary Oblates of Mary Immaculate, with daily operations staffed by the Sisters of Charity of St. Vincent de Paul of Halifax. It formed part of a national network of church‑run, state‑funded institutions designed to assimilate Indigenous children into Euro‑Canadian society by separating them from their families, prohibiting Indigenous languages and spiritual practices, and replacing them with Christian religion and English or French instruction.[7][3][5][1]

## Students, Communities, and Daily Regime

Mi’kmaq and Wolastoqiyik (Maliseet) children from Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island, New Brunswick, and parts of Quebec were sent to Shubenacadie; some sources note that children from other Indigenous communities may also have attended. Many children were compelled to attend through threats that child welfare authorities would otherwise remove them from their families, an experience reported in individual survivor accounts including that of Nora Bernard.[2][3][4][1]

Daily life at Shubenacadie combined limited classroom instruction with heavy labour, including chores, farm work, and maintenance of the school property. Survivors describe being punished for speaking their own languages, being forbidden to practice their cultures, and being required to conform to strict schedules and religious observances.[3][5][7]

## Abuse, Neglect, and Deaths of Children

Historical research and survivor testimony document widespread abuse at Shubenacadie, including harsh discipline, beatings, emotional and psychological abuse, sexual violence, medical experimentation, and neglect. Accounts also describe chronic malnutrition, starvation rations, inadequate clothing, and poor healthcare, which exposed children to illness and contributed to deaths at the school.[5][1][3]

The National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation’s commemoration page for Shubenacadie lists children known to have died while associated with the school, including Mi’kmaw children such as Colin Bernard (died 1943‑10‑19) and many others, illustrating the human cost of the institution. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) concluded in 2015 that Canada’s residential school system amounted to cultural genocide; many Shubenacadie Survivors and descendants refer to the policy, and their specific experiences at the school, as genocide.[8][3][5]

## Resistance, Survivors, and Community Responses

Despite the school’s intent to sever children from their languages and cultures, Mi’kmaw and Wolastoqiyik students resisted in multiple ways, including secretly speaking their languages, maintaining kin relationships inside the school, and running away. Families and community leaders protested the school and attempted to protect children, though their efforts were constrained by colonial law and the power of church and state.[7][3][5]

Survivors’ testimonies, including those later gathered in collaboration with the Mi’kmawey Debert Cultural Centre, document both the violence of the school and the resiliency of the children who endured it. These stories have become central to public understanding in Mi’kma’ki and across Canada of the harms of residential schools and the need for truth, justice, and reparations.[9][10][5]

## Closure, Demolition, and National Historic Site Designation

Shubenacadie Indian Residential School closed in 1967, after which the building was abandoned and left unused for nearly two decades. In 1986, a fire destroyed the deteriorated structure; a factory was subsequently built on the footprint of the former school.[1][6][3]

The site gained growing recognition as a place of mourning and remembrance for Survivors and families. In response to Calls to Action from the TRC, Parks Canada worked with the National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation (NCTR), Mi’kmaw organizations, and Survivors to recognize residential schools as sites of national historic significance. On 1 September 2020, the former Shubenacadie Indian Residential School was designated a National Historic Site of Canada, acknowledging both the abuses that took place there and the resilience and activism of Survivors.[11][10][9][5][1]

The nomination for this designation was advanced by the co‑chair of the Tripartite Culture and Heritage Working Committee of the Mi’kmaq‑Nova Scotia‑Canada Tripartite Forum on behalf of Survivors and their families. The designation text explicitly notes that the school was part of a colonial policy of forced assimilation, that children experienced abuse, malnutrition, harsh punishment, and child labour, and that the site stands as a witness to children who died there and to those who continue to fight for restitution and justice.[5][7]

## Biography of Nora Bernard: Early Life and Residential School Experience

Mi’kmaw activist Nora Bernard ONS was born on 22 September 1935 in the Mi’kmaq First Nation community of Millbrook, near Truro, Nova Scotia. She was the second oldest in a family of six children and took on caregiving responsibilities for her siblings from an early age, especially after her father left the family.[4][12][2]

In 1945, when she was nine, local officials informed her mother that if she did not sign consent forms to send her children to a residential school, child welfare authorities would seize them into “protective custody.” Under this coercive threat, Nora and her siblings were sent to Shubenacadie Indian Residential School, where she remained for approximately five years. Her younger sister later recalled that Nora frequently stood up to nuns who handled the children roughly and would find ways to get extra food for her youngest brother in the school kitchen, even as she herself endured severe beatings.[13][12][2][4]

## Loss and Reinstatement of Legal Status

In 1955, at about age 20, Nora married a non‑Indigenous man, which under the Indian Act at the time caused her to lose her legal status as an “Indian” and her associated band membership. This meant she could no longer reside as a member of her own community, despite living only a short distance from where she was born, a situation that has been highlighted as an example of the gendered discrimination embedded in the Act.[14][2][13]

The discriminatory section of the Indian Act was repealed in 1985 through Bill C‑31, allowing many women and their children to apply for the restoration of status. However, reinstatement of band membership was not automatic; Nora had to fight for many years to be recognized again as a member of Millbrook First Nation, and she was only voted back into the band in 2006–2007, shortly before her death.[2][13][14]

## Organizing Shubenacadie Survivors and Launching Legal Action

In the late 1980s, Nora began actively searching for other Shubenacadie Survivors and inviting them to meet in her home, creating an informal support network that eventually grew into an organization representing former students of the school. In 1995 she formally established an association for Shubenacadie Survivors with the goal of seeking justice and compensation for the abuses they had suffered.[4][14][2]

Nora approached several lawyers about taking on a residential school case and was refused, but she persisted until Halifax lawyer John McKiggan agreed to represent the group. With his assistance, she filed a class‑action lawsuit on behalf of Shubenacadie Survivors against the Government of Canada, seeking compensation for the harms caused by residential schooling.[12][13][2][4]

## From Shubenacadie Lawsuit to National Settlement

News of the Shubenacadie class action encouraged Survivor groups from other residential schools and provinces to launch their own lawsuits, creating a patchwork of legal actions across Canada. These cases were eventually joined and negotiated into the Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement (IRSSA), which has been described as the largest class‑action settlement in Canadian history, representing approximately 70,000–79,000 Survivors and providing more than 5 billion Canadian dollars in compensation.[13][2][4]

McKiggan and other commentators have emphasized that without Nora’s leadership, persistence, and organizing work, the national settlement might never have taken place. Her efforts helped to shift national understandings of residential schools from a relatively obscure or minimized chapter of history to a central issue in discussions of truth, reconciliation, and Indigenous rights in Canada.[14][12][4]

## Public Advocacy and Testimony

Beyond her courtroom efforts, Nora was a prominent public advocate for Survivors. In 2005 she testified before the House of Commons of Canada, describing not only physical abuse in residential schools but also the emotional, psychological, and cultural harms experienced by children who were taken from their families and communities.[2][13][4]

She consistently framed residential schooling as a system that inflicted intergenerational trauma and called for recognition, apology, and compensation from the federal government. Her testimony contributed to the political momentum that led to the IRSSA and later the federal government’s formal apology to residential school Survivors in 2008.[13][14][2]

## Death of Nora Bernard and Legacy

Just as compensation payments under the residential schools settlement were beginning to reach Survivors, Nora was killed in her home in Millbrook First Nation on 26 or 27 December 2007; she was 72 years old. Contemporary reporting notes that she was found dead on 27 December 2007, shortly after Christmas, and that unread Christmas cards from Survivors were still in her mailbox.[12][2][13]

Her grandson was later charged and convicted in connection with her death, which was widely described as a profound loss for Survivors and for Indigenous activism in Canada. Family members and community leaders emphasized her lifelong role as a protector and advocate for her siblings, her children, and other residential school Survivors, highlighting that she “never stopped” caring for others and fighting for justice.[12][13]

Nora has been posthumously honoured for her contributions, including recognition in discussions of Indigenous women’s leadership and, in some accounts, the use of the honorary ONS (Order of Nova Scotia) designation with her name. Commentators describe her as a key figure whose grassroots organizing in Mi’kma’ki altered the course of national history by making the IRSSA possible and advancing broader conversations about truth and reconciliation.[4][14][2][12]

## Intersections of Place, Memory, and Justice

The stories of Shubenacadie Residential School and of Nora Bernard are deeply intertwined. Nora’s childhood experience at the school shaped her later determination to bring Survivors together and seek legal redress, while her activism in turn helped to ensure that institutions like Shubenacadie would be publicly acknowledged as sites of systemic harm rather than forgotten local histories.[14][2][4]

The designation of the former Shubenacadie school as a National Historic Site in 2020, made in consultation with Mi’kmaw Survivors and organizations, can be understood as part of the broader legacy of Survivors’ activism, including Nora’s work. It formalizes the school’s significance in the story of Canada while explicitly naming the colonial policies and abuses that occurred there, offering a focal point for education, remembrance, and ongoing calls for justice.[9][1][7][5]

For communities in Mi’kma’ki and across the country, remembering Shubenacadie and honouring figures like Nora Bernard remains central to understanding both the history of residential schools and the continuing work of truth, reconciliation, and decolonization.[10][9][14]
