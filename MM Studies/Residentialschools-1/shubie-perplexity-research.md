# "I am designing a lesson on Nora Bernard and the Shubenacadie Residential School for high school students in Nova Scotia. Please find the following specific resources and information:

Primary Source Excerpts: Find 2-3 short, impactful quotes from Nora Bernard regarding her time at Shubenacadie or her motivation for the 1995 class-action lawsuit.

Survivor Data: Find the specific number of students who attended Shubenacadie or the number of survivors Nora initially organized in her Dartmouth home to add 'hard data' to the dossier."

Here are classroom‑ready pieces you can drop straight into a lesson or slide deck.

## Primary source excerpts (short quotes)

1. On types of abuse at residential school (House of Commons testimony, 2005):

> “Sexual and physical abuse was not the only abuse that the survivors experienced in these institutions. Abuses included such things as being incarcerated through no fault of their own; the introduction of child labour; the withholding of proper food, clothing, and proper education; the loss of language and culture; and no proper medical attention.”[^1]

2. On her motivation for the class‑action:

> “Justice. I just wanted justice for my First Nations people that attended residential schools – not only down here – throughout Canada.”[^2][^3]

3. On what Shubenacadie felt like as a child:

> “It was no place for a child… Once you entered those big doors in the front and they slammed behind you, it was just like going into prison.”[^4][^2]

All three are short enough to use as lesson starters, exit tickets, or close‑reading exercises.

## Renaming Cornwallis Street to Nora Bernard Street

You can use this as your “economic/social reconciliation” bridge into contemporary Halifax.

- Halifax Regional Council voted in 2022 to rename Cornwallis Street in the North End to Nora Bernard Street, following recommendations from the Task Force on the Commemoration of Edward Cornwallis and the Recognition and Commemoration of Indigenous History.[^21][^22]
- Edward Cornwallis, the original namesake, issued bounties for Mi’kmaq scalps in 1749; repeated community debates framed his commemoration as incompatible with reconciliation.[^23][^24]
- The municipality ran a multi‑stage public consultation: thousands of submissions generated a shortlist of 15 names, and “Nora Bernard Street” received about 2,400–2,417 public votes, the highest total.[^24][^22][^23]
- Media coverage and city communications emphasize that Nora was selected because she was a Millbrook First Nation residential school Survivor whose 15‑year legal battle helped secure a C\$5+ billion settlement for an estimated 79,000 Survivors and that she received the Order of Nova Scotia.[^23][^24][^21]
- At the official renaming ceremony in October 2023, family members, Millbrook Chief Bob Gloade, and Councillor Lindell Smith spoke; the event included smudging and Indigenous drumming, and her daughter described the choice as “overwhelming” and “about time that they recognize…our matriarch…our trailblazer.”[^25][^26][^24]

Key classroom angle: this is a concrete example of how naming, public memory, and consultation processes are part of economic/social reconciliation in a specific Nova Scotia neighbourhood.

## Survivor numbers and organizing data

You asked for specific “hard data” you can drop into the dossier.

### How many children attended Shubenacadie?

- An APTN report on the National Historic Site designation quotes a researcher stating that **more than 1,000 Mi’kmaw and Wolastoqiyik students attended Shubenacadie between 1930 and 1967**.[^28]
- CBC reporting around the ground‑search work notes that the school **“affected the lives of thousands of Indigenous children”** from across the Maritimes who were forced to attend.[^29]
- The National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation lists **the names of 16 children known to have died while at the institution**, which you can show students directly through the NCTR site.[^13][^9][^29]

You can frame this as: *“Over a thousand Mi’kmaw and Wolastoqiyik children were sent to Shubenacadie, and researchers say the school affected thousands more Indigenous children and families across the Maritimes.”*[^28][^29]

### How many Survivors did Nora organize?

- A detailed Daily News retrospective on her work (archived at Algoma University) notes that her organizing **began in 1987 in her Truro kitchen, where she interviewed Survivors of Shubenacadie Indian Residential School.**[^2]
- The same article states that by **1998 the association had more than 900 registered members** in Canada and the United States, despite having no formal funding.[^2]
- Other profiles (e.g., SeeDifferent and JFK Law) confirm that these kitchen meetings grew into a formal Survivor organization and that her class‑action work ultimately connected to **about 70,000–79,000 Survivors nationwide** through the national settlement.[^30][^31][^32]

So while sources do not give an exact count of “the first group in her home,” you can accurately tell students:

- “Nora started by gathering Survivors around her own kitchen table in Truro in 1987.”[^2]
- “Within about a decade, her association had grown to over 900 registered members from Canada and the U.S., and her class‑action helped open the door for a national settlement that reached roughly 70,000–79,000 residential school Survivors.”[^31][^30][^2]
