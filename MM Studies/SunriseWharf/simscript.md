## Unit 01: Sovereign Claim

**Long Title:** EXHIBIT 01: The Sovereign Right to Trade

**Interaction Prompt:** Should the Committee acknowledge the inherent Treaty right to trade as the foundation of wharf use in the Accord?

**Options:** ACKNOWLEDGE RIGHT / REGULATE ACCESS

### Overview
This unit examines the fundamental legal claim made by the Sunrise Collective. You will compare the oral traditions and 1760 Treaty texts against modern legal interpretations of "Moderate Livelihood" and community-led management plans.

The goal is to determine if the wharf is a municipal utility or a Treaty site. You will analyze how the 21-year standstill since the Marshall decision led to the current flashpoint at Sunrise Harbour.

Finally, we will look at the "Full Box" theory of Indigenous rights. This previews the evidence coming in the Field Data and Legal tabs.

### Field Data: THE SUNRISE STANDSTILL
The morning mist at Sunrise Harbour wharf often hides the underlying tension, but the facts remain clear. Since September 2020, the Sipekne'katik First Nation has operated a self-regulated fishery that bypasses federal seasonal windows. [cite: 1] This move is not a whim, but a calculated implementation of a community management plan built over years of internal consultation. [cite: 23]

"We are not looking for a fight. We are looking for a livelihood that was promised to us in 1760," says one harvester. For many in the community, the DFO (Department of Fisheries and Oceans) trap tags are symbols of colonial oversight that has no place in a modern Treaty relationship. [cite: 24] They argue that their right to trade is inherent and sovereign, predating the very existence of the DFO itself.

The stand-off has turned the wharf into a constitutional battlefield. While commercial fishers see "unauthorized gear," the Sunrise Collective sees the exercise of a supreme law. [cite: 40] The municipality now faces the difficult task of managing a shared utility where two different legal systems are operating simultaneously on the same dock.

### Legal Docs: THE MARSHALL PRECEDENT
In *R. v. Marshall* (1999), the Supreme Court of Canada affirmed a constitutionally protected right [cite: 21] to a "moderate livelihood." This landmark ruling acknowledged that the 1760/61 Treaties were not historical relics but living obligations. [cite: 111] The court found that the right to fish and trade is protected by Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982, which recognizes and affirms existing Aboriginal and Treaty rights.

Legal analysts point to the **"Full Box"** theory as the core of the dispute. This theory suggests that Indigenous rights were never "given" by the Crown. Instead, they were already full and merely ignored for centuries. [cite: 123] If the "box" of rights is full, then any municipal attempt to restrict wharf access is an unconstitutional infringement.

However, the DFO points to *Marshall II* (1999), which clarified that Treaty rights can be regulated for conservation purposes. [cite: 112] This creates a legal tension. The Committee must decide if the Accord begins by acknowledging inherent sovereignty or by establishing regulatory frameworks.

## Unit 02: Conflict of Laws

**Long Title:** EXHIBIT 02: Conflict of Laws

**Interaction Prompt:** Given the 2025 Biological Review's warning on management vacuums, how should the Committee balance conservation and Treaty rights in the Accord?

**Options:** CONSERVATION FIRST / JOINT MONITORING

### Overview
Unit 02 tackles the jurisdictional friction between municipal bylaws and constitutional rights at Sunrise Harbour. We look at the "Sparrow Test" for justification and analyze DFO enforcement data versus Mi'kmaw claims of jurisdictional autonomy.

You will evaluate whether the Harbor Authority has the power to override a Treaty right in the name of safety. This chapter previews the conflict between "seasonal windows" and "inherent access."

The goal is to find a middle ground that respects both the Sparrow Test and the local Harbor Master's logs.

### Field Data: BYLAW §4.2: THE SEASONAL WINDOW
Municipal Bylaw §4.2 is the primary tool for managing wharf traffic, but it was never designed for Treaty implementation. The bylaw mandates that all commercial landings occur strictly within DFO-approved windows. [cite: 4] When gear is landed outside these dates, the DFO seizes it, citing a need for regional fairness and stock protection. [cite: 31]

"The rules have to apply to everyone if we want a sustainable harbor," says the Harbor Master. From his perspective, an uncoordinated landing is a safety hazard and a management nightmare. [cite: 37] He points to recent seizures as proof that the current system is under intense pressure.

Mi'kmaw harvesters, however, argue that these "windows" are a unilateral imposition. [cite: 40] They contend that a municipal bylaw cannot legally override a constitutionally protected Treaty right. To them, the DFO's enforcement actions are not about conservation, but about maintaining colonial control over Indigenous trade.

**Image:** images/chapter2.png | **Credit:** Atlantic Fisherman: DFO enforcement focus (2023)

### Legal Docs: THE SPARROW TEST FOR JUSTIFICATION
In *R. v. Sparrow* (1990), the Supreme Court of Canada established a strict test for any government regulation that infringes upon an Aboriginal or Treaty right. [cite: 21] For a bylaw like §4.2 to be valid, it must serve a "valid legislative objective," usually conservation or public safety. Even then, the infringement must be "minimally impairing," meaning the government must use the least restrictive means possible.

Legal experts are questioning if a total ban on out-of-season landings is truly "minimally impairing." [cite: 119] Could the municipality instead offer a "Treaty Slip" system that manages traffic without banning the right? [cite: 48] This would allow for safe landings while respecting the constitutional priority of the harvester.

The Honour of the Crown is also at stake. [cite: 18] If the municipality enforces a bylaw that effectively cancels a Treaty right, it may be acting dishonorably. The Committee must decide if the Accord should follow the letter of the bylaw or the spirit of the Sparrow Test.

**Interaction Prompt:** Given the history of arson and mob violence at Middle West Pubnico, what must be the Committee's priority for physical security in the Accord?

### Overview

### Field Data: THE MIDDLE WEST PUBNICO ARSON
The 2020 dispute was not merely a legal disagreement. It was a period of sustained physical violence. In October 2020, a lobster pound in Middle West Pubnico used by Mi'kmaw fishers was set ablaze in a suspicious fire that destroyed the facility. [cite: 63] This arson followed days of vandalism where a mob of hundreds surrounded the facility, destroying thousands of pounds of lobster. [cite: 64]

At the Sunrise Harbour wharf, Mi'kmaw harvesters were trapped inside a facility as a crowd outside grew increasingly aggressive. [cite: 68] One harvester, Jason Marr, was forced to live-stream the incident as a van was torched in the parking lot. [cite: 69] These were not isolated protests. They were targeted acts of intimidation designed to stop Treaty-based fishing through force.

"They were not there to talk. They were there to burn us out," recalled one harvester who witnessed the fire. [cite: 72] The violence at Sunrise Harbour and Pubnico proved that without a robust security framework, the wharf can quickly descend into lawlessness. The municipality must now account for this history of violence when drafting the new Accord.

### Legal Docs: THE FAILURE OF THE CROWN TO PROTECT
The violence of 2020 led to a significant legal challenge regarding the RCMP's role in the dispute. Sipekne'katik First Nation filed a lawsuit alleging that the RCMP and the DFO failed in their duty to protect Treaty-holders from mob violence. [cite: 98] The suit claims that officers stood by as facilities were vandalized and harvesters were intimidated.

Legal analysts argue that the "Honour of the Crown" extends to the provision of public safety. [cite: 18] If the state fails to protect Indigenous people while they are exercising a constitutionally protected right, it is a profound failure of the rule of law. The 2020 crisis has forced a re-evaluation of how police respond to conflicts between civilian fishers and Treaty fishers.

The courts have previously ruled that the state has a duty to act with integrity. [cite: 90] In the context of the wharf, this means the police cannot remain neutral when one group is committing arson against another. The Accord must define a new "Enforcement Protocol" that ensures the safety of all harvesters, regardless of the political climate.

## Unit 04: Role of Elders

**Long Title:** EXHIBIT 04: The Role of Elders

**Interaction Prompt:** Should the Committee mandate Elder guidance for all wharf disputes in the Accord?

**Options:** JOINT WATCH / ELDER GUIDANCE

### Overview
Unit 04 introduces the cultural governance of the Mi'kmaq. You will explore how Elder guidance and "Netukulimk" principles of sustainability can be integrated into a joint municipal framework to move beyond police enforcement.

We will examine how Treaties are viewed as "covenants" rather than just contracts. This chapter previews the "Shared Values" section of the Accord.

The goal is to determine if a culturally responsive dispute resolution model can succeed where the current model has failed.

### Field Data: THE WISDOM OF THE NATION
In Mi'kmaw culture, **Elders** are the living archives of law and protocol. They teach that Treaties are not merely legal contracts between states, but **covenants**, which are sacred promises made between nations. [cite: 3] This perspective shifts the focus from winning a dock to sharing a resource.

The principle of **Netukulimk** is central to this worldview. It means using natural resources for the benefit of the community without jeopardizing the stock for future generations. [cite: 141] Many believe that if Netukulimk were the guiding law of the wharf, the conflict between commercial and Treaty fishers would vanish.

"We are not just fishers. We are stewards," says one Elder. They argue that the current municipal model is too focused on traps and windows and ignores the "Brother to Brother" relationship established in the original Treaties. The Committee must decide if an Elder-led council should have the final say in wharf disputes.

**Image:** images/chapter4.png | **Credit:** APTN: Netukulimk - Our Way Forward

### Legal Docs: THE HONOUR OF THE CROWN
The "Honour of the Crown" is a legal principle that requires the government to act with integrity in all its dealings with Indigenous peoples. [cite: 18] It is not just a moral suggestion. It is a constitutional requirement that limits how municipalities can exercise power. [cite: 86]

When the municipality ignores Elder guidance, it may be violating this principle. Meaningful consultation requires more than just listening. It requires the government to be willing to **change its policies** in response to what is heard. [cite: 91] If an Elder says a bylaw is harming the community's way of life, the municipality has a legal duty to accommodate that concern.

This creates a tension between "Standardized Rules" and "Culturally Responsive Management." The legal evidence suggests that a "One-Size-Fits-All" approach to the wharf may actually be unconstitutional. The Accord represents an opportunity to align municipal operations with the Honour of the Crown.

## Unit 05: Living Treaty

**Long Title:** EXHIBIT 05: Living Agreements

**Interaction Prompt:** Should the Accord treat the 1760 Treaty as a "living agreement" that overrides municipal wharf rules?

**Options:** LIVING LAW / MUNICIPAL LAW

### Overview
This unit focuses on the bridge between history and the modern day. We analyze the 1760 "Truckhouse" clause and determine if historical trading posts were the precursors to the modern wharf infrastructure we see today.

You will evaluate the Supreme Court's mandate for "large and liberal" interpretations of Treaties. This chapter previews the "Historical Context" section of the Accord.

The goal is to determine if a 260-year-old promise still holds jurisdictional power in 2026.

### Field Data: THE EVOLUTION OF THE TRUCKHOUSE
The 1760/61 Treaties contained a specific clause that granted the Mi'kmaq the right to bring their goods to sell at **Truckhouses**. [cite: 2] While the British Crown originally intended these truckhouses to control and monitor Indigenous trade, the Mi'kmaq understood the clause as a guarantee of their economic sovereignty.

Today, historians and legal scholars argue that the modern wharf is the functional descendant of the Treaty Truckhouse. [cite: 149] If the Treaty promised a place to trade, then the infrastructure of that trade, including the docks, the off-loading zones, and the markets, must be protected by that same promise. This is a core pillar of **Economic Reconciliation**.

"The wharf is our office. The 1760 Treaty is our lease," says one local archivist. This perspective reframes the wharf not as a municipal project, but as a "Treaty Site" where Indigenous commerce has a constitutionally protected right to exist. The Committee must decide if the Accord will officially recognize this historical evolution.

**Image:** images/chapter5.png | **Credit:** NS Archives: 1760 Treaty Text

### Legal Docs: THE LARGE AND LIBERAL DOCTRINE
The Supreme Court of Canada has mandated that all Treaties must be interpreted in a "large, liberal, and generous manner." [cite: 21] This legal doctrine means that courts must look at the intent of the agreement, not just the literal 18th-century wording. [cite: 115]

Under this doctrine, a "Truckhouse" is not just a wooden building from 1760. It is whatever modern infrastructure is needed for a "moderate livelihood" in 2026. [cite: 111] This means that the right to trade inherently includes the right to use a modern harbor with all its amenities.

Legal evidence suggests that when a municipality treats a Treaty as a "dead history project," it is in violation of Supreme Court precedent. The Treaties are **Sovereign Laws** that remain in full effect today. The Accord must reflect this reality by treating the 1760/61 agreements as the supreme law of the wharf.

### Field Data: THE PRINCIPLE OF SELF-DETERMINATION
The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP) affirms the right of Indigenous peoples to self-determination and to maintain their own institutions. [cite: 160] In Canada, UNDRIP became federal law through the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act (2021).

Co-management models have succeeded in other Canadian contexts. The Nunavut Wildlife Management Board and the Mackenzie Valley Resource Management Board are examples of shared governance structures that balance Indigenous rights with provincial and federal oversight. [cite: 165] These models show that when all parties sit at the table as equals, sustainable solutions are possible.

At Sunrise Harbour, the principle of self-determination means that Mi'kmaw harvesters have a right to participate in the management of the wharf, not just as users but as decision-makers. [cite: 170] The question for the Committee is how to structure this participation in a way that respects both Treaty rights and municipal authority.

**Image:** images/chapter6.png | **Credit:** UNDRIP Implementation Framework

### Legal Docs: DRAFTING THE ACCORD
The 2026 Wharf Accord must address three core elements:

1. **Acknowledgment of Rights**: The Accord must recognize the constitutionally protected Treaty right to fish and trade for a moderate livelihood, as affirmed in *R. v. Marshall* and protected by Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982.

2. **Safety and Conservation**: The Accord must establish clear safety standards and conservation protocols that apply to all users of the wharf, consistent with the Sparrow Test and the Honour of the Crown.

3. **Consultation and Governance**: The Accord must create a joint governance structure that includes Mi'kmaw representation, municipal oversight, and commercial fisher input, ensuring that all voices are heard in wharf management decisions.

Legal scholars note that the Accord does not need to resolve every legal question. [cite: 180] What it needs to do is create a framework for ongoing dialogue and shared management. The goal is not perfection. The goal is progress.
