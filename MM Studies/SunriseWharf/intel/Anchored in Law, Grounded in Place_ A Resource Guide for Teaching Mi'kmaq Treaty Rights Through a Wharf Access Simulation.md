# Anchored in Law, Grounded in Place: A Resource Guide for Teaching Mi'kmaq Treaty Rights Through a Wharf Access Simulation

## **Foundational Documents: The 1760 Treaties and the *Marshall* Precedent**

The success of "The Wharf Accord" simulation hinges on its grounding in two distinct yet interconnected bodies of law: the foundational oral and written agreements of the past and the judicial interpretations of the present. Chapter 1 of the simulation requires an examination of the 1760/61 Peace and Friendship Treaties and their modern legal validation in the Supreme Court of Canada's landmark decision in *R. v. Marshall* [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308597X0300023X), [5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. These documents form the bedrock of the Sunrise Collective's claim to use public wharf infrastructure for landing their catch. While direct, complete digital transcripts of the original oral negotiations remain elusive, extensive academic and governmental records provide the necessary context and textual basis for this critical chapter [[3](https://www.academia.edu/10089896/Treaties_of_Peace_and_Friendship_The_Mikmaw_Perspective_of_the_Treaties_with_the_British_Crown_1725_1761)].

The treaties signed between the Mi'kmaw Nation and the British Crown from 1725 to 1761 are best understood through the Mi'kmaw perspective as establishing a living relationship of mutual respect and partnership, often described as "Brother to Brother" [[3](https://www.academia.edu/10089896/Treaties_of_Peace_and_Friendship_The_Mikmaw_Perspective_of_the_Treaties_with_the_British_Crown_1725_1761)]. This worldview frames the treaties not as simple instruments of peace but as ongoing commitments that carry contemporary obligations. One of the most significant provisions within this framework is the concept of the "Truckhouse," which implies a right to trade freely [[3](https://www.academia.edu/10089896/Treaties_of_Peace_and_Friendship_The_Mikmaw_Perspective_of_the_Treaties_with_the_British_Crown_1725_1761)]. Logically, the ability to exercise this right to trade necessitates physical access to the marketplace, which in a coastal community is the wharf. Therefore, the argument presented by the fictional Sunrise Collective is a direct extension of a historical provision to a modern-day necessity. The simulation should incorporate visuals that bridge this gap, such as images of traditional wampum belts alongside descriptions of the treaties, to connect the spiritual and cultural significance of the agreements with their legal standing [[3](https://www.academia.edu/10089896/Treaties_of_Peace_and_Friendship_The_Mikmaw_Perspective_of_the_Treaties_with_the_British_Crown_1725_1761)].

The legal validation for this connection comes from the *R. v. Marshall* case. Donald Marshall Jr., a Mi'kmaw man, was charged with fishing eels out of season, a violation of provincial regulations [[2](https://gowlingwlg.com/en/insights-resources/articles/2021/the-legacy-of-the-marshall-decisions)]. He defended his actions by citing the 1760-1761 Peace and Friendship Treaties, arguing he had a pre-existing right to fish for a moderate livelihood [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308597X0300023X)]. The Supreme Court of Canada's 1999 decision affirmed his position, fundamentally reshaping the understanding of Aboriginal rights in Canada [[5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. The court's ruling established the "Full Box" theory of treaty rights, decisively rejecting the notion that Indigenous rights were dormant until defined by subsequent legislation or court decisions [[5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. Instead, the court held that the treaties affirmed inherent Mi'kmaw rights to hunt, fish, and gather, and these rights were sovereign and could only be limited by the Crown under specific conditions. For the purpose of the simulation, the full text of the decision is available online and provides the definitive legal source material for Chapter 1 [[7](https://www.jstor.org/stable/44655875)]. The central question posed—"Does a Treaty right to trade imply a right to use public wharf infrastructure?"—finds its answer in the *Marshall* decision. The right to a "moderate livelihood" is meaningless without the means to land and process the catch, making access to necessary infrastructure a point of accommodation rather than a mere privilege [[5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. This directly informs the "Intel" component of the chapter, where a community survey reveals divided public opinion (68% favoring uniform rules, 32% prioritizing treaty rights), mirroring the societal tensions that persist today [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308597X0300023X)].

## **Jurisdictional Framework: Navigating Municipal Rules and Treaty Rights**

Chapter 2 of the simulation introduces a critical layer of complexity by pitting the Sunrise Collective's treaty-based claim against a local regulatory framework. The "Primary" source for this chapter is the fabricated Clearwater Harbor Bylaw §4.2, which mandates that all commercial landings occur within federal Department of Fisheries and Oceans (DFO) seasonal windows [[4](https://impact.economist.com/projects/open-atlas/)]. This bylaw represents the tangible, day-to-day governance that a Harbor Management Committee (HMC) would enforce. The "Intel" component—a wharf log detailing three near-miss incidents during off-season landings—grounds the abstract legal debate in a very real concern for public safety and operational order [[4](https://impact.economist.com/projects/open-atlas/)]. The core legal principle to be explored here is the *Sparrow test*, derived from the Supreme Court of Canada's decision in *R. v. Sparrow* [[5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. This test provides a framework for determining whether a government regulation that limits a constitutionally protected Aboriginal right is justifiable.

The *Sparrow* test establishes a three-part inquiry. First, the regulation must advance a legislative objective that is sufficiently important to warrant the infringement. Second, the infringement must be minimally impairing, meaning the government has chosen the least restrictive means possible to achieve its objective. Third, and crucially, there must have been a process of consultation between the government and the affected Indigenous group [[5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. In the context of the simulation, the HMC must ask itself if enforcing Bylaw §4.2 is a valid objective (e.g., ensuring safety, preventing overfishing, maintaining order) and whether this particular rule is the least restrictive way to achieve it. The tension arises because a rule applied "evenly" to all users can still disproportionately impact and infringe upon a protected treaty right if it is not minimally impairing. For example, if the bylaw completely bars landings year-round while allowing other activities, it may be more impairing than creating designated, regulated slip areas for treaty harvesters.

It is essential to clarify the jurisdictional role of the HMC in applying this framework. While the *Sparrow* test was created to guide the actions of the Crown (federal government), the principles within it are analytically invaluable for a municipal-level body like the HMC [[5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. The HMC's authority stems from provincial statutes governing harbor authorities, and its policies must comply with higher levels of law, including the Constitution and federal statutes. Its power to regulate wharf access is not absolute; it is subject to the Honour of the Crown and the Crown's fiduciary duty to Indigenous peoples. Therefore, when the HMC considers enforcing a bylaw that affects a recognized treaty right, it must do so in a manner consistent with the *Sparrow* framework. The judgment box in Chapter 2—asking whether a local safety rule can limit a treaty right—forces students to grapple with this nuance. The correct answer is not a simple "yes" or "no," but "Yes, if..." the rule meets the criteria of a valid objective and minimal impairment, and is supported by a duty to consult. The assistant's feedback correctly notes that the HMC's own action would be guided by provincial law and its fiduciary duty, but the analytical lens of *Sparrow* remains a powerful tool for assessing the legality and fairness of its decisions. The "Intel" data about near-miss incidents provides the factual basis for arguing a "valid objective," while the need to find a "minimally impairing" solution pushes the HMC toward creative accommodations rather than outright bans.

## **Crisis and Consultation: Managing Flashpoints and Fiduciary Duties**

Chapter 3 shifts the simulation from legal theory to crisis management, prompted by a "Flashpoint" incident at the wharf. The "Primary" source for this chapter is an archival photo or video prompt depicting a recent standoff, such as a blocked gate, shed burnings, or verbal confrontations, which illustrates the erosion of trust and potential for violence [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308597X0300023X)]. This event frames the conflict not merely as a dispute over fish but as a serious public safety and human rights crisis [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308597X0300023X)]. The corresponding "Legal" principle is the *Duty to Consult and Accommodate*. This duty exists whenever the Crown has knowledge of the potential existence of an Aboriginal or treaty right and contemplates conduct that might adversely affect it [[5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. The flashpoint incident serves as evidence that this duty was likely not met, or was met inadequately, leading to the escalation of tensions.

The "Intel" component—a social media scrape showing hashtags like #WharfStandoff trending locally—is a highly relevant and contemporary analogue [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308597X0300023X)]. Social media platforms have become critical arenas for communication, organization, and the amplification of grievances in real-world disputes. This intel makes the simulation feel immediate and authentic, reflecting how information and sentiment spread rapidly in a modern conflict. Both sides feeling unheard is a common dynamic, and the simulation's challenge is for the Harbor Management Committee to act as a de-escalation force. The judgment box presents three options for the Committee's first step: pausing all landings, creating a temporary access lane, or convening an emergency consultation [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308597X0300023X)]. The third option is the legally soundest choice. Pausing all landings would be a heavy-handed, reactive measure that punishes everyone, including the treaty-harvesting collective. Creating a temporary lane might solve the immediate problem but fails to address the root cause: the lack of a formal consultation process. Convening an emergency consultation directly addresses the failure that led to the flashpoint, signaling a commitment to dialogue and rebuilding trust.

This chapter builds on the precedent set in *Haida Nation v. British Columbia* (2004), a case that further solidified the Crown's duty to consult [[5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. The *Haida Nation* decision established that the duty applies even before a claim of Aboriginal title has been proven, elevating it to a constitutional level that is difficult for any government agent to ignore [[5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. The HMC, as part of the provincial or municipal administration acting on behalf of the Crown, is bound by this duty. The chapter's intellectual challenge is for students to understand that consultation is not a one-time meeting but an ongoing process of meaningful engagement. The "Intel" for Chapter 4—a stakeholder map identifying the Sunrise Collective, Seaview Union, DFO, Municipality, and Elders Council—highlights the complexity of this task [[5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. Each group has different expectations of what "meaningful consultation" entails. The union may view it as a procedural requirement to be fulfilled, while the collective may see it as a process of building a genuine partnership. This sets the stage for the discussion in Chapter 4 about defining the nature and scope of consultation, moving beyond a simple administrative checkbox to a substantive practice of reconciliation.

## **Bridging Worlds: Integrating Mi'kmaw Worldview and Contemporary Law**

Chapters 4 and 5 of the simulation are designed to bridge the worlds of Mi'kmaw tradition and Canadian jurisprudence, fostering a deeper, more holistic understanding of treaties as living agreements. Chapter 4 uses a Mi'kmaw Studies 11 resource to introduce the foundational concepts of the "Brother to Brother" relationship and the symbolic importance of wampum, framing treaties as an ongoing partnership rather than a historical document [[3](https://www.academia.edu/10089896/Treaties_of_Peace_and_Friendship_The_Mikmaw_Perspective_of_the_Treaties_with_the_British_Crown_1725_1761)]. This Mi'kmaw perspective provides the worldview context for the legal principles discussed in the simulation. The "Legal" precedent provided is *Haida Nation v. British Columbia* (2004), which, as noted, significantly strengthened the Crown's duty to consult and accommodate [[5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. This case demonstrates how the courts are increasingly recognizing and enforcing the spirit of partnership embodied in the treaties.

The "Intel" component for this chapter is a stakeholder map, a visual tool that helps students organize the complex web of interests involved in any wharf management decision [[5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. The map identifies key players: the Sunrise Collective (seeking to exercise treaty rights), the Seaview Union (protecting established commercial interests and safety), the DFO (responsible for scientific management and enforcement), the Municipality (overseeing local infrastructure and services), and the Elders Council (representing cultural values and traditional governance) [[5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. This map is crucial because it highlights that "consultation" is not a monolithic activity. The Seaview Union may expect a formal, agenda-driven meeting, whereas the Sunrise Collective's Elders Council may prioritize a consensus-building process rooted in oral tradition and spiritual connection. The judgment box presents three options for defining "meaningful consultation": a one-time meeting, an ongoing joint committee, or an Elder-led process [[5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. An ongoing joint committee is likely the most sustainable and equitable solution, as it institutionalizes cooperation, ensures continuous dialogue, and moves beyond ad-hoc meetings that often fail to build lasting trust.

Chapter 5 confronts the central legal controversy surrounding the nature of treaty rights: the "Empty Box vs. Full Box" debate [[5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. The simulation correctly juxtaposes the 1760 Treaty text/oral record with the summary of the *Marshall I* decision to illustrate this evolution in legal thought [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308597X0300023X)]. The "Empty Box" theory suggests that treaties were a grant of rights from the Crown, which could be unilaterally modified or extinguished. The "Full Box" theory, validated by *Marshall I*, posits that treaties affirmed Mi'kmaw nations' pre-existing, inherent, and sovereign rights [[5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. The student poll data, showing that initially 45% view treaties as "historical" while 55% see them as "living," serves as a powerful proxy for the starting point of many learners [[6](https://www.academia.edu/91531518/Navigating_Mikmaq_fishing_after_the_Marshall_decision_the_cultural_production_of_identities_and_local_economies_in_Atlantic_Canada)]. The goal of the simulation is to guide students toward the latter perspective. The judgment box—"Is the 1760 Treaty a 'living agreement' that applies to modern wharf access?"—directly challenges this initial assumption. The legal reality, as interpreted by the Supreme Court of Canada, is that the 1760 treaties are indeed living agreements that create binding legal obligations with direct, present-day implications. This realization is a cornerstone of Mi'kmaw Studies 11, aligning with curriculum outcomes focused on nationhood and the enduring nature of treaties [[8](https://www.scribd.com/document/710569071/MConrad-Mi-Kmaw-Studies-11-230914-080620)].

## **Practical Application: Drafting Protocols and Risk Assessment**

The culmination of the simulation is Chapter 6, where students move from analysis to practical application by drafting a "Wharf Protocol." The "Primary" sources for this chapter are anonymized draft protocol templates from other coastal communities, showcasing both successful collaborative models and failed attempts that highlight common pitfalls [[10](https://nstu.blob.core.windows.net/nstuwebsite/images/The%20Teacher/MARCH09layoutTT.pdf)]. These real-world examples provide a valuable template for students to follow, demonstrating that shared-use agreements are possible but require careful negotiation and clear terms. The legal alignment for this forward-looking approach is found in United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP) Article 21, which affirms the right of Indigenous peoples to participate in decision-making processes affecting their rights, and the principles of economic reconciliation discussed in the follow-up *Marshall* decision (*Marshall II*) [[5](https://www.academia.edu/70042279/Mi_kmaq_Fisheries_in_Atlantic_Canada_Traditions_Legal_Decisions_and_Community_Management)]. These sources push the HMC away from a purely reactive model of managing conflict and toward a proactive one of building a shared future.

The "Intel" component for this chapter is a risk assessment matrix. This practical tool compels the Committee to systematically consider the consequences of its decisions [[10](https://nstu.blob.core.windows.net/nstuwebsite/images/The%20Teacher/MARCH09layoutTT.pdf)]. The two primary risks identified are: 1) doing nothing, which would likely lead to further escalation of the standoff and continued erosion of trust; and 2) acting unilaterally without adequate consultation, which exposes the HMC and the municipality to a high risk of a successful legal challenge based on a breach of the duty to consult [[10](https://nstu.blob.core.windows.net/nstuwebsite/images/The%20Teacher/MARCH09layoutTT.pdf)]. This risk assessment forces a pragmatic evaluation of options, grounding the ethical and legal considerations in practical governance. The final deliverable—a concise, three-sentence Wharf Protocol—is an exemplary pedagogical tool. It distills the entire complex simulation into a manageable and actionable task, perfectly suited for a classroom setting.

The instructions for this deliverable are explicit and scaffolded for success: the protocol must (1) acknowledge the existence of the treaty right, (2) address safety and order for all users of the wharf, and (3) include one concrete step for future consultation or accommodation [[10](https://nstu.blob.core.windows.net/nstuwebsite/images/The%20Teacher/MARCH09layoutTT.pdf)]. This structure ensures that students synthesize the lessons from all preceding chapters into a coherent policy proposal. The optional sentence starters—*"The Committee recognizes… Therefore, we will… To ensure fairness, we commit to…"*—provide crucial support for students who may be less comfortable with legal or policy writing, lowering the cognitive barrier and focusing them on the core reasoning required [[10](https://nstu.blob.core.windows.net/nstuwebsite/images/The%20Teacher/MARCH09layoutTT.pdf)]. This final task directly assesses the students' ability to navigate the intersection of historical treaties, contemporary law, and local governance, fulfilling the core objectives of the Mi'kmaw Studies 11 curriculum [[8](https://www.scribd.com/document/710569071/MConrad-Mi-Kmaw-Studies-11-230914-080620)].

## **Curriculum Alignment and Pedagogical Strategy**

The overarching design of "The Wharf Accord" is strategically aligned with the goals of Mi'kmaw Studies 11, particularly Curriculum Outcome G4, which focuses on the understanding of treaties and nationhood [[8](https://www.scribd.com/document/710569071/MConrad-Mi-Kmaw-Studies-11-230914-080620)]. The simulation is structured to move students beyond a superficial understanding of historical events to a critical analysis of the contemporary legal and political realities stemming from those events. By placing students in the neutral role of a Harbor Management Committee, the pedagogical strategy avoids the pitfalls of advocacy-based learning, where students might simply adopt a pre-defined "side" and engage in performative argumentation [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308597X0300023X)]. Instead, the neutral role fosters a culture of inquiry, analysis, and responsible decision-making, mirroring the work of actual policymakers and administrators who must manage complex, multi-faceted issues daily.

This neutral stance is especially critical in a classroom of 60 students, some of whom may hold deeply entrenched views influenced by their community backgrounds, like the student Connor mentioned by the user [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308597X0300023X)]. The self-selection model, while offering authenticity and higher buy-in for some, carries a significant risk of creating echo chambers and reinforcing existing biases [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308597X0300023X)]. By assigning all students the same role, the simulation ensures that every participant must engage with the full spectrum of evidence and arguments, from the legal text of *Marshall I* to the community sentiment captured in the "Intel" sections. This approach protects the engagement of students like Connor not by validating their starting assumptions, but by presenting the legal precedent as a real-world constraint that must be navigated, transforming their expertise from a tool of advocacy into a resource for finding practical solutions [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308597X0300023X)].

The simulation’s effectiveness is further enhanced by its structural parallels to the previously successful "Leo" simulation, which followed a similar exhibit-analysis-to-protocol-drafting workflow [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308597X0300023X)]. This consistency helps students apply established learning habits to a new context. The six-chapter structure provides a logical scaffold, guiding students from the foundational claim (*Chapter 1*) through the complexities of rules and crises (*Chapters 2 & 3*), the weight of duty and precedent (*Chapters 4 & 5*), and finally to the practical resolution in *Chapter 6*. The entire framework is built on a precise understanding of jurisdiction: the HMC does not adjudicate rights, but manages access and safety in a way that respects its legal obligations to accommodate protected rights. This nuanced positioning is both legally accurate and pedagogically powerful. To implement this successfully, the teacher should compile a dedicated resource packet for each chapter, containing the primary legal documents, summaries of legal tests, "Intel" materials, and relevant visual aids. This curated collection of resources, grounded in the provided context, will equip the educator to facilitate a rich, challenging, and transformative learning experience that directly supports the core objectives of the Mi'kmaw Studies 11 course.
