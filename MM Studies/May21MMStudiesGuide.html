PART 01 — THE RESIDENTIAL SCHOOL SYSTEM

What Were Residential Schools?

Federally funded, church-run boarding schools designed to assimilate Indigenous children into Euro-Canadian society.

Core Mandate: "kill the Indian in the child" — separate children from families, prohibit Indigenous languages and spiritual practices, replace with Christian religion and English/French instruction.

Over 130 residential schools operated across Canada.

Shubenacadie Indian Residential School (Nova Scotia)

Only residential school in the Maritime Provinces

  • Location: Shubenacadie, Nova Scotia (Sipekne'katik district of Mi'kma'ki)
  • Operated: February 5, 1930 – June 22, 1967 (37 years)
  • Conditions: Chronic underfunding, freezing temperatures, structural leaks, severe overcrowding, malnutrition

Key Policy Origins

  • Bagot Commission (1842–1844): Recommended the forced removal of Indigenous children from their families in order to assimilate them — the intellectual foundation for residential schools
  • 1920: Amendment to the Indian Act made residential school attendance mandatory — the government tried to "solve the Indian problem"
  • Children were compelled to attend through threats that child welfare authorities would otherwise seize them from their families
PART 02 — SUGAR FALLS — BETTY ROSS'S STORY

The Graphic Novel

Sugar Falls by David A. Robertson is based on the true story of Betty Ross (Kokum), a residential school survivor.

Structure: Modern-day students visit their Kokum (grandmother Betty), who shares her story through a smudging ceremony before beginning.

Key Themes in the Narrative

  1. Forced Separation: By law, Aboriginal parents were forced to send their children to residential schools. Betty's father was emotionally devastated but powerless against "The Law"
  2. Intergenerational Trauma: Betty's mother was herself a residential school survivor who struggled to show love because of her own trauma — the "lessons" of the school created cycles of harm that impacted Betty's own family years later
  3. Cultural Erasure: The priest and nuns punished children for speaking their language. Hair, clothes, and names were stripped as symbols of identity
  4. Barriers to Escape: Children who tried to run away faced severe consequences — isolation, punishment, and the threat of never seeing family again
  5. Vocational Training over Education: Children performed manual labour (farm work, laundry, kitchen duties) instead of receiving proper academic education
  6. Resistance & Resilience: Despite the system's efforts, Betty survived and reclaimed her identity — the smudging ceremony at the story's beginning represents cultural reclamation

Connection to Textbook (Aboriginal Peoples: Building for the Future)

  • Page 48–49: Language suppression, vocational training, harsh conditions
  • Page 70: Targeting language and ceremonies so children would "lose their sense of identity"; contrast with the Chehalis Community School (teaching language and traditional beadwork) as an example of Aboriginal control of Aboriginal education
PART 04 — THE INDIAN RESIDENTIAL SCHOOLS SETTLEMENT AGREEMENT (IRSSA)

What Was It?

2006/2007: The largest class-action settlement in Canadian history

$1.9–5 billion commitment to address the legacy of residential schools

Built on five pillars:

Pillar Purpose
Common Experience Payment (CEP) Direct financial payment to all former students who resided at a recognized residential school
Independent Assessment Process (IAP) Compensation for specific claims of sexual or serious physical abuse, requiring individual hearings
Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) Five-year commission traveling Canada to document history and lasting impacts through survivor testimonies
Commemoration Fund for local and national memorial projects to ensure the history is never forgotten
Health and Healing Services Mental health supports and Aboriginal Healing Foundation for survivors' recovery

The TRC and the 94 Calls to Action

  • The TRC operated from 2008–2015
  • Concluded that the residential school system amounted to cultural genocide
  • Issued 94 Calls to Action across six categories:
    1. Child Welfare
    2. Education
    3. Language and Culture
    4. Health
    5. Justice
    6. Reconciliation

Beyond 94 — Tracking Progress (CBC)

  • CBC News launched Beyond 94 in March 2018 to monitor progress on every Call to Action
  • As of the most recent update (April 2025):
    • 17 Not Started
    • 28 Projects Proposed
    • 34 Projects Underway
    • 15 Complete
  • Less than 20% of the 94 Calls to Action are marked as "Complete" nearly a decade after they were issued
CENTRALIZATION

Failed 1940s Policy

Centralization was a failed 1940s Canadian federal policy that forced Nova Scotia's Mi'kmaq people off their traditional lands. The government relocated them from dozens of scattered communities into just two central reserves: Eskasoni and Sipekne'katik (Shubenacadie).

Key Facts

Goal: The government sought to cut administrative costs and accelerate Indigenous assimilation.

Conditions: Promised housing, jobs, and farms never materialized, leading to starvation and homelessness.

Resistance: Mi'kmaq families strongly resisted the policy and the surveillance of government agents.

End: The federal government officially abandoned the failed project in 1949.

Legacy: The forced moves caused generational trauma and permanently altered Mi'kmaq land rights.

PART 05 — THE SIXTIES SCOOP

What Was It?

As residential schools began closing in the 1960s, child welfare authorities became the new mechanism of removal.

Mass removal of Indigenous children from their families into foster care and adoption systems — predominantly with non-Indigenous families.

Called the "Sixties Scoop" because of the scale and speed of the removals.

Scale & Impact

  • 1955: Less than 1% of children in care in BC were Indigenous
  • 1964: 34% of children in care in BC were Indigenous
  • 2012: 68% of children involved in child welfare in Alberta were Aboriginal
  • The "Millennial Scoop" — Indigenous children remain overrepresented in child welfare today at rates exceeding the residential school era

Connection to Residential Schools

  • Same underlying logic: separation of Indigenous children from their families and culture
  • Same outcome: loss of language, identity, cultural belonging
  • Same intergenerational trauma: children removed grew up disconnected from their communities
PART 07 — THE OUTSIDE CIRCLE (HARD ROAD TO WALK)

The Graphic Novel

The Outside Circle by Patti LaBoucane-Benson tells the story of Joey and Pete, two Indigenous brothers caught in cycles of gang violence and incarceration.

Central theme: intergenerational trauma — how the legacy of residential schools, the Sixties Scoop, and systemic discrimination continues to impact Indigenous families today.

Key Quotes & Themes (from the "Connecting the Dots" investigation)

  • "Director of Child Welfare" — connects to the Sixties Scoop and ongoing overrepresentation of Indigenous children in care
  • "Child Welfare Act" — the legal mechanism that enabled mass removal of Indigenous children
  • "Historical Trauma" — the cumulative emotional and psychological wounding across generations
PART 08 — THE OKA CRISIS

What Was It?

The Oka Crisis (French: Crise d'Oka), also known as the Mohawk Crisis or Kanehsatà꞉ke Resistance (French: Résistance de Kanehsatà꞉ke), was a land dispute between a group of Mohawk people and the town of Oka, Quebec, Canada.

The dispute was over plans to build a golf course on land known as "The Pines" which included an Indigenous burial ground. The crisis began on July 11, 1990, and lasted 78 days until September 26, 1990. Two fatalities resulted from the conflict. The dispute was the first well-publicized violent conflict between First Nations and provincial governments in the late 20th century.

Key Facts

  • The Mohawk community of Kanehsatà꞉ke established barricades to block access to the proposed golf course expansion
  • The Quebec provincial police (Sûreté du Québec) attempted to dismantle the barricade on July 11, leading to a shootout that killed Corporal Marcel Lemay
  • After 11 days, the Canadian Army was deployed to enforce the barricade removal
  • The crisis ended on September 26, 1990, when the barricades were dismantled and the Mohawk warriors surrendered
  • The golf course expansion was ultimately cancelled
PART 09 — CROSS-CUTTING THEMES

1. Colonial Legacy

From residential schools to the Sixties Scoop to ongoing child welfare overrepresentation — the same policy logic persists: separation of Indigenous children from families and culture.

The Indian Act remains the foundational piece of discriminatory legislation.

2. Resistance & Resilience

  • Betty Ross (Kokum) — survival and cultural transmission through storytelling
  • Survivor oral histories — shared publicly since the 1970s, bypassing mainstream media to preserve truth

4. Intergenerational Trauma

Residential school survivors struggled to parent because they were never parented themselves.

The Sixties Scoop continued the cycle.

Current child welfare statistics show the pattern persists.

6. Self-Determination & Education

Aboriginal control of Aboriginal education — the antidote to residential schools.
