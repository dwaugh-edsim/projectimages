The Shubenacadie Indian Residential School: An Exhaustive Historical, Legacy, and Pedagogical Guide for Mi'kmaw Studies 11
Historical Origins and the Systemic Mandate of the Shubenacadie Institution
The Shubenacadie Indian Residential School (SIRS), known colloquially by students and community members as "St. Anne's Convent," "Indian College," "The Resi," "The Ressi," or simply "Shubie," operated in Shubenacadie, Nova Scotia, from February 5, 1930, until its formal closure on June 22, 1967.1 Positioned in the Sipekne'katik district of Mi'kma'ki, the facility was the only residential school established in the Maritime Provinces, serving as a regional hub for the forced assimilation of Mi'kmaw and Wolastoqiyik children from Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island, New Brunswick, and Quebec.1  
Historically, the establishment of the Shubenacadie facility represented a "second wave" of residential school expansion in Canada.4 This institutional phase emerged after the First World War, decades after Peter Henderson Bryce’s damning 1907 report on the catastrophic health conditions in Western residential schools.4 During this interwar period, the federal Department of Indian Affairs (DIA) faced growing, organized, rights-centered activism from Indigenous organizations, which the state viewed as a direct threat to its colonial and assimilatory mandate.4 To counter this political mobilization, the federal government sought to expand the residential school model into the Atlantic region to systematic target younger generations.4  
Construction began in 1928 on agricultural land purchased from George Gay, culminating in an imposing Classical Moderne three-storey red brick building designed in a symmetrical E-shaped plan.1 This structural design featured expansive dormitories on each lateral wing, a chapel in the rear wing, and a surrounding property containing barns, staff residences, cultivated fields, and pastures.2 While the school’s initial advertised mandate was to provide basic education and refuge for orphaned or neglected children on Maritime reserves, Duncan Campbell Scott, then the Deputy Superintendent General of Indian Affairs, expanded the school's directive immediately prior to its opening.1 Scott positioned the institution as a coercive alternative to the existing on-reserve day schools, transforming it into a vehicle for mandatory cultural erasure.1  
Over its 37 years of operation, an estimated 1,000 children were placed within the institution, representing approximately 10% of the entire Mi'kmaw child population in the Maritimes during its operation.1 The school operated under Roman Catholic management, staffed by the Sisters of Charity of Saint Vincent de Paul (headquartered at Mount Saint Vincent College in Halifax) from 1929 until 1956.1 In 1956, operations were transferred to the Missionary Oblates of Mary Immaculate, who administered the facility until its closure.1 Following its decommissioning in 1967, the school sat abandoned for nearly two decades until a fire destroyed the derelict building in 1986, leading to its subsequent demolition and the construction of a commercial factory on the property in 1988.1

Institutional Realities: Underfunding, Deprivation, and Daily Life
For the first two decades of the institution's existence, the federal government severely underfunded the facility, effectively reproducing and worsening the impoverished conditions of the reserves.1 This systemic underfunding manifested in a structurally compromised physical plant that was drafty, damp, and frequently unheated during Atlantic winters.1  
To minimize operating costs, the administration underpaid its teaching staff; prior to 1950, educators at the school received less than half the salary of their provincial counterparts.1 Although salaries doubled in 1950 to match provincial public school averages, the school struggled with chronic educator instability.1 The average teacher tenure during the 1950s and 1960s was only two to three years, with a teacher turnover rate reaching 30% by 1965.1  
This systemic neglect created an environment of structural deprivation, malnutrition, and medical precarity.1 Independent nutritional evaluations of the student body in 1945 described student dietaries as "overwhelmingly poor," and a follow-up assessment in 1946 labeled the administration's disregard for student hunger as "utterly disgusting".1 Food program deficiencies caused chronic hunger, forcing children to eat low-quality food scraps, while administrators and religious staff consumed high-quality meals.5 On major holidays like Easter Sunday, children were given only a single boiled egg as their daily sustenance.5  
This physical and psychological deprivation led to widespread disease.1 Anthropologist Scott Hamilton of Lakehead University has compared the medical realities of these institutions—characterized by high numbers of sick children, limited diagnostic and medical capacity, and severe overcrowding—to the tragic conditions of modern long-term care facilities during public health crises.1 Outbreaks of contagious illnesses such as tuberculosis, jaundice, chickenpox, measles, and diphtheria swept through the dormitories because the administration did not isolate infectious students.5  
Medical emergencies were routinely dismissed by supervising staff.5 Alan Knockwood fell ill while carrying dining room dishes and collapsed from a burst appendix, which required last rites, because the school nurse dismissed his excruciating pain as "lollygagging".5 Roger Lewis entered the institution in late 1949 as a three-year-old child, demonstrating that even infants were subjected to these harsh conditions despite being deemed too young for formal education.3  
Historical records document a tragic toll of student deaths within the school due to illnesses and untreated medical conditions.5 These records provide a stark database for students analyzing the physical costs of the residential school system.6

The daily routine was structured around forced assimilation and child labor.5 Upon arrival, children’s cultural belongings and clothing were confiscated, their hair was cut off, and they were scrubbed with tar soap and doused with DDT or de-lousing powder.5 Traditional languages were strictly forbidden; children who spoke Mi'kmaq or Maliseet were beaten.5  
Physical discipline was severe and erratic.5 Staff used wooden straps, rulers, and crop whips to enforce obedience.5 Bedwetting children were publicly humiliated, forced to march through the communal dining hall during meals with their wet sheets placed over their heads.5 In addition to academic and religious instruction, the school functioned as a site of forced labor, requiring children to work in the institution’s agricultural fields, laundries, and coal-fired boiler rooms.2  
These experiences left deep psychological scars.5 The trauma was compounded by jurisdictional gray zones, where the federal government and religious entities evaded accountability for the abuse.5 For example, a survivor named Debbie was taken out of the country to the United States by a supervising nun, Sister Gilberta Eleanor Keller, during her time at the school.8 Years later, when she sought legal compensation, she faced administrative hurdles because the settlement agreements were specific to harm caused directly by the federal government, leaving her in a jurisdictional void regarding church-administered abuses.8
Indigenous Agency, Mobilization, and the Legal Fight for Restitution
Despite the system's efforts to erase Indigenous identity, survivors launched legal, political, and academic campaigns to expose the truth of the residential school system and secure compensation.5
Nora Bernard and the Genesis of the National Class Action
Nora Bernard of the Millbrook First Nation was a central figure in the fight for legal restitution.7 Born on September 22, 1935, Bernard was forced into the Shubenacadie school in 1944 at the age of nine when an Indian Agent threatened her mother with child welfare intervention if she did not sign the consent forms.7 She spent five years at the school, where she routinely stood up for her siblings and peers against abusive staff.7  
In 1955, Bernard married a non-Indigenous man, which legally stripped her of her status and band membership under the discriminatory provisions of the federal Indian Act.7 Legally prohibited from living in her home community, she was segregated from her kin.10 Although the 1985 passage of Bill C-31 amended the Indian Act to allow women to reclaim their status, it introduced new discriminatory categories of identity—such as Section 6(1) and 6(2)—which continued to complicate her family's legal status.10 It was not until 2007 that she was formally voted back into the Millbrook First Nation.7  
In 1987, Bernard began searching for other survivors, hosting initial meetings in her Dartmouth home to share testimonies of institutional abuse.9 These meetings led to the founding of the Shubenacadie Indian Residential School Association (SIRSA).12 After several law firms refused to take the case, Bernard convinced Halifax lawyer John McKiggan in 1995 to represent the group in a class-action lawsuit against the federal government.9  
This lawsuit was the first of its kind in Canada.9 As news of the Shubenacadie suit spread, other survivor groups across the country launched similar legal actions, which were eventually consolidated into a single national class-action lawsuit.9 Bernard spoke directly to the House of Commons in 2005, testifying about the physical abuse, forced labor, malnutrition, loss of language, and medical neglect at the school.7  
Her efforts led directly to the 2006 Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement, valued at over $5 billion.7 Sadly, Bernard was murdered in December 2007 by her grandson James Douglas Gloade during a drug-related dispute, just before the first common experience payments were distributed to survivors.9 She was posthumously awarded the Order of Nova Scotia in 2008, and the City of Halifax renamed Cornwallis Street to "Nora Bernard Street" in December 2022 to honor her legacy.7

Survivor Oral Histories and Regional Documentation
SIRS survivors also worked to document their histories.4 In the 1970s, survivors began sharing their accounts publicly.4 In June 1978, the Mi'kmaw newspaper Micmac News issued a call for former students to share their testimonies, bypassing mainstream media to preserve these oral histories.4  
In 1992, Isabelle Knockwood published Out of the Depths: The Experiences of Mi'kmaw Children and the Indian Residential School at Shubenacadie, Nova Scotia.4 This work was the first book-length, first-voice account of the institution, weaving together Knockwood’s personal stories of her eleven years at the school with testimonies from other survivors and federal documents.4  
Knockwood's book challenged the institutional silence of the Sisters of Charity, who claimed that their records were destroyed in a 1951 fire.4 Her documentation proved that survivors could reconstruct these historical truths.4 In recognition of her work, Saint Mary's University awarded Knockwood an Honorary Doctorate in Civil Law in 2013.5  
These legal and literary campaigns led to academic recognition, with SIRS featured in J.R. Miller’s Shingwauk’s Vision (1997) and John Milloy’s A National Crime (1999).4 Today, the Mi'kmawey Debert Cultural Centre’s IRS Legacy Project, coordinated by Dorene Bernard, preserves these oral histories to ensure that the experiences of survivors are shared with future generations.5
The Poetics of Reclamation: Rita Joe and the Legacy of the Gentle Warrior
The literary works of Elder Rita Joe are central to cultural reclamation and national reconciliation in Canada.13
Biography of Rita Joe
Rita Joe (née Rita Bernard) was born on March 15, 1932, in Whycocomagh on Cape Breton Island, Nova Scotia.14 After her mother died when Rita was five years old, she lived with foster families and was orphaned by the age of ten.14 At twelve, she left Cape Breton to attend the Shubenacadie Indian Residential School, where she remained for four years.14  
During her time at the school, staff constantly told her that she was "no good".14 This systematic degradation damaged her self-esteem, but it also inspired her to write.14 She later recalled that she became a writer precisely because she had been forbidden from writing or speaking her truth as a child.13  
After returning to Cape Breton to live on the Eskasoni First Nations Reserve, where she and her husband raised ten children, she began writing to challenge the negative depictions of Indigenous people she encountered in her children's schoolbooks.14 In her memoir, she expressed her hope: "My greatest wish is that there will be more writing from my people, and that our children will read it".14  
Joe published several poetry collections, including Poems of Rita Joe (1978), Song of Eskasoni (1989), and We Are the Dreamers (1999).15 Known as the "Gentle Warrior" for her use of poetry to fight injustice without anger, she was honored as the 2023 Nova Scotia Heritage Day honouree, cementing her legacy in Canadian history.15
Stanza-by-Stanza Literary Analysis of "I Lost My Talk"
Rita Joe's most famous poem, "I Lost My Talk," is a core text for understanding the trauma of cultural erasure and the path to reconciliation.13
Stanza 1: I lost my talk / The talk you took away. / When I was a little girl / At Shubenacadie school.
Analysis: The "talk" represents her native language, cultural identity, and traditional worldview.14 Taking it away reflects the core objective of the residential school system: to separate children from their families and assimilate them into Eurocentric culture.14 The reference to "Shubenacadie school" grounds the poem in her personal experience.14
Stanza 2: You snatched it away: / I speak like you / I think like you / I create like you / The scrambled ballad, about my world.
Analysis: The violent verb "snatched" emphasizes how her language was taken.14 Under threat of punishment, she was conditioned to speak, think, and create in English.14 This shifted her thinking, creating a "scrambled ballad" where her original worldview was buried but not completely lost.14
Stanza 3: Two ways I talk / Both ways I say, / Your way is more powerful.
Analysis: This stanza addresses her reality of knowing two distinct cultures and languages.14 However, she acknowledges that due to colonial policies, such as the Indian Act, the English language and Eurocentric worldviews hold significantly more societal power and dominance.14
Stanza 4: So gently I offer my hand and ask, / Let me find my talk / So I can teach you about me.
Analysis: Instead of responding with anger, she gently offers her hand, asking for space to reclaim her language and identity.14 This reclamation is not for isolation, but to teach the settler community about her culture, transforming the colonial relationship into one based on harmony and justice.14
Contemporary Adaptations and Artistic Influence
Rita Joe’s poetry has inspired numerous artistic and musical adaptations.13 Her poem "I Lost My Talk" was adapted into a major multi-media orchestral work by Canadian composer John Estacio.13 Estacio’s composition is divided into four continuous movements that mirror the four stanzas of the poem.13 The piece begins with a peaceful flute solo representing the traditional Indigenous world, which is then disrupted by harsh, dissonant strings that evoke the trauma of the residential school, before transitioning into a themes of reconciliation.13  
Joe's legacy also continues through contemporary Mi'kmaw writers, artists, and activists.17 For example, Rebecca Thomas, a former Poet Laureate of Halifax, wrote the award-winning children's book I'm Finding My Talk as a direct response to Joe's poem.15 Thomas, the child of a residential school survivor, explores the journey of reclaiming language and identity in a contemporary context, demonstrating how Joe's "gentle warrior" approach continues to inspire new generations of writers.15
Pedagogical Framework: Integrating Shubenacadie into Mi'kmaw Studies 11
The history and legacy of the Shubenacadie school serve as a critical foundation for students in the Mi'kmaw Studies 11 curriculum in Nova Scotia.18 The course is designed around an inquiry-based learning (IBL) model, encouraging students to explore how they are connected to the First Peoples of Nova Scotia.18
Curriculum Outcomes and Guidelines
The Mi'kmaw Studies 11 curriculum focuses on developing an understanding of governance, culture, education, spirituality, and social justice.18 The study of the Shubenacadie school directly aligns with two primary curriculum outcomes 20:
Outcome G5: Evaluate the adverse effects of discriminatory policies, legislation, and social injustices on First Nations in Canada.20
Outcome E2: Investigate the origins, goals, and impacts of the Indian Residential School System.20
To meet these outcomes, teachers are guided to structure units around the three parts of the social studies inquiry framework 20:
Inquiring: The teacher introduces the historical or contemporary issue, modeling how to pose questions and identify reliable sources.20
Finding Evidence: Students work in partners or small groups to analyze primary testimonies, historical documents, and secondary sources, compiling evidence using graphic organizers.20
Communicating: Students select an appropriate format—such as presentations, short videos, digital story maps, or formal proposals—to share their research findings.20
Trauma-Informed Safety Guidelines
Because the history of residential schools involves systemic violence and intergenerational trauma, curriculum guidelines require teachers to prioritize student safety.18 The Nova Scotia Department of Education provides specific guidelines for navigating these difficult histories 18:
Classroom Agreements: Before exploring this history, teachers and students should co-construct a "classroom agreement" to establish expectations for respectful, reflective, and empathetic dialogue.20
Trauma-Informed Support: Teachers must acknowledge that this content can be distressing, particularly for Indigenous students who are descendants of survivors.2 Students must be made aware of available support resources, including the National Indian Residential School Crisis Line (1-866-925-4419).2
Resource Selection: Materials must be pre-evaluated to ensure they support human rights, social justice, and healthy relationships, avoiding graphic or sensationalized accounts that could cause secondary trauma.20
First Voice Representation: Focus on "first voice" accounts, such as the testimonies of Shubenacadie survivors, to ensure that the strength, resilience, and agency of Indigenous communities remain central to the learning experience.20
Lesson Plan: Analyzing "I Lost My Talk"
This lesson plan integrates the work of Rita Joe into the Mi'kmaw Studies 11 curriculum, focusing on how poetry can serve as an act of resilience and advocacy.16
Curricular Connection: Speaking and Listening, Critical Response, and Personal Response.16
Core Inquiry Question: How can poetry create change, and how does Rita Joe use language to respond to historical injustices? 16
Materials: Text of "I Lost My Talk" 14, Pauline Young's illustrations 22, and John Estacio’s musical adaptation.13
Instructional Sequence
1. Inquiring (Modeling)
The teacher introduces the poem "I Lost My Talk," sharing historical context about Shubenacadie and the systemic suppression of the Mi'kmaw language.14 The teacher projects the poem and models how to analyze Joe's choice of words, such as "snatched" and "scrambled ballad," highlighting how these metaphors represent the trauma of language loss.14 The teacher explains how historical terms like "Indian" in Joe's other poetry must be understood within their specific historical context.23
2. Finding Evidence (Collaborative Work)
Students work in pairs to analyze the structure and imagery of the poem.22 To explore the physical and emotional impact of language loss, students participate in one of two pathways 24:
Translation Pathway: Students fluent in Mi'kmaq or another language translate a stanza of the poem, reflecting on how translation alters the poem's expression of identity and culture.24
Reflective Writing Pathway: Students who only speak English imagine what it would feel like to have their language taken away.24 They write a short narrative describing how they would perform everyday tasks—such as ordering food, explaining a medical emergency to a doctor, or sharing a family story—without their primary language.24
3. Communicating (Recitation and Artistic Extension)
Students present their reflections through recitation or multi-media projects.22 For recitations, students are guided to perform the poem using three distinct emotional lenses 24:
Anger: Reciting the poem from a place of anger, positioning the "you" as a hostile adversary.24
Fear: Reciting the poem from a place of fear, representing the "you" as an overwhelming, threatening force.24
Reconciliation: Reciting the poem from a place of reconciliation, emphasizing the gentle offering of her hand in the final stanza.24
Students then discuss how incorporating all three emotions changes the power of the message.24 Alternatively, students can combine Joe's poem with another medium—such as adding a musical track, creating an illustration, or producing a short video—to extend its meaning, mirroring how Pauline Young's illustrations and John Estacio’s music built upon the original work.13
Inquiry Guide: Environmental Racism in Mi'kma'ki
This inquiry framework guides students through a regional study of environmental racism, connecting historical policies of displacement to contemporary environmental injustices.20
Curricular Connection: Outcome G5 ( discriminatory policies and social injustices).20
Core Inquiry Question: How has environmental racism affected the physical and mental health of Mi'kmaw communities, and how have people taken action to raise awareness about these conditions? 20
Inquiry Implementation Steps
1. Case Study Modeling (Pictou Landing and Boat Harbour)
The teacher models the inquiry using the history of the Pictou Landing First Nation.20 The teacher shares resources detailing how the construction of an upstream pulp mill polluted Boat Harbour, a traditional fishing and gathering area, causing severe health impacts and cancers for over 50 years.5  
The teacher models how to extract key evidence from historical records, mapping how the community sued the mill and the provincial government for environmental damage.5 The teacher demonstrates how to evaluate the reliability of sources, such as government reports, corporate statements, and community testimonies.20
2. Student Investigations
Working in small groups, students select another community in Mi'kma'ki or Canada affected by environmental racism.20 Using curated, reliable sources, students compile evidence to answer the following research questions 20:
Where: Where is the community located geographically, and who lives there? 20
What: What are the specific causes of environmental degradation (e.g., landfills, industrial runoff, toxic waste sites)? 20
When: When did the degradation begin, and what was the historical policy context? 20
Why: Why is this community facing this issue (e.g., zoning laws, lack of political representation, discriminatory policies)? 20
How: How has this environmental contamination affected the physical, mental, and cultural health of community members? 20
3. Comparative Analysis and Presentation
Students present their research using digital formats, such as ArcGIS Story Maps, short documentary videos, or formal reports.20 Through class discussions, students compare their findings to identify systemic commonalities across different communities, analyzing how environmental racism is connected to historical colonial policies of displacement and resource exploitation.20
Inquiry Guide: Decolonizing Regional Landmark Names
This inquiry guides students to analyze how renaming controversial public landmarks can serve as an act of decolonization and reconciliation.20
Curricular Connection: Outcomes G5 (social injustices) and E2 (residential schools legacy).20
Core Inquiry Question: How does the names of public places reflect colonial history, and how does reclaiming traditional place-names foster equity? 7
Inquiry Implementation Steps
1. Historical Context and Case Study
The teacher introduces the renaming of "Cornwallis Street" to "Nora Bernard Street" in Halifax.7 Students analyze who Edward Cornwallis was, why his legacy is controversial, and how renaming the street honors a champion of residential school survivors.7  
The teacher guides students through a discussion of how public landmark names reflect historical power dynamics, analyzing how keeping colonial names can perpetuate systemic trauma for Indigenous peoples.20
2. Investigating Rename Procedures
Working in partners, students identify a landmark or street in their local municipality or province named after a controversial historical figure associated with colonial violence.20 Students research the specific procedures required to change a place name at the municipal, provincial, or federal level, gathering evidence on 20:
The bureaucratic steps and required documentation for submission.20
The barriers and political resistance communities typically encounter.20
The consultation processes used to engage local Indigenous communities and the general public.20
3. Renaming Proposal Portfolio
Students collaborate to draft a formal proposal to rename their chosen landmark, utilizing a graphic organizer to compare the history of the original figure with a suggested Indigenous leader or traditional Mi'kmaw place-name.20 The proposal must include 20:
Historical Rationale: An evidence-based argument explaining why the current name is controversial and how renaming the landmark supports decolonization.20
Advocacy Plan: A detailed strategy outlining how to raise public awareness, organize community consultations, and lobby political representatives.20
Curricular Presentation: Partners present their proposal portfolios to the class, defending their name choices and advocacy plans in a simulated municipal council hearing.20 This experience allows students to apply their historical knowledge to real-world civic processes, embodying the active, informed citizenship at the core of the Mi'kmaw Studies 11 curriculum.18
