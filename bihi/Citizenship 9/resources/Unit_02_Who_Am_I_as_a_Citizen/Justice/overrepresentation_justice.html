Overrepresentation & Systems Inquest

Key background readings, definitions, and sliding notes covering how historical colonialism, systemic biases, and cultural divides contribute to Indigenous overrepresentation in the Canadian justice system.

DOJ Report: Overrepresentation of Indigenous People

This section summarizes findings from the Department of Justice Canada's official study on the roots of overrepresentation (Source: justice.gc.ca).

"As a result of Canada’s colonial history, Indigenous peoples have been subjected to assimilation policies and practices that have created collective and individual intergenerational trauma resulting in negative impacts on social determinants of health for many. Their experiences, compounded by inadequate housing as well as limited education and employment opportunities, have been identified in the literature as contributing to Indigenous people being in contact with the criminal justice system more often and for longer periods than non-Indigenous people."

Core Contributors to Overrepresentation

  • Colonialism: Forced assimilation policies (such as the Indian Act, Sixties Scoop, and Residential School system) stripped away community governance structures, broke family bonds, and suppressed cultural practices.
  • Trauma & Social Determinants: Historical trauma drives downstream social vulnerabilities: housing instability, lack of employment, and limited educational support in rural reserves and urban centers.
  • The Incarceration Loop: Rather than addressing these structural disadvantages, traditional courts respond by incarcerating individuals, which separates them from community support networks and traps them in a cycle of return.

Negative Impacts of Colonialism (Key Examples)

The legacy of assimilation has created severe challenges for communities:

  1. Disruption of traditional governance and legal structures.
  2. Loss of language, values, and community support systems.
  3. Intergenerational trauma resulting from Residential Schools (e.g., Shubenacadie).
  4. Poverty and lack of socioeconomic development on reserves.
  5. High rates of housing insecurity and forced migration to urban centers.
  6. Loss of Indian status for women under gender-discriminatory sections of the Indian Act.
  7. Chronic health disparities and lack of culturally relevant mental healthcare.
  8. High rates of child welfare intervention (the "Millennium Scoop").

The Five Pillars of Systemic Inequity

To audit the justice system, we look at five critical concepts explaining why Indigenous individuals face high contact rates with the courts:

1. Cultural Alienation

The disconnection of individuals from their traditional culture, language, and values as a result of forced assimilation, leaving youth without a firm sense of identity.

Relevance: Explains why Liam's grandfather could not teach him the word for 'home.'
2. Territorial Dispossession

The forced removal of Indigenous people from their traditional lands and resources onto small, isolated reserves, crippling their economic self-sufficiency.

Relevance: Directly connects to reserve housing limits and migration to towns.
3. Intergenerational Trauma

Trauma passed down through generations. Children of Residential School survivors experience the effects of their parents' abuse, loss of language, and parental isolation.

Relevance: Connects Liam's family history directly to his housing and job instability.
4. Systemic Discrimination

Institutional policies and practices that result in unfair treatment of minority groups, even if the rules seem neutral on their face (e.g., risk assessment tools).

Relevance: Scores poor people higher simply because they lack resources.
5. Socioeconomic Marginalization

The exclusion of a group from social and economic opportunities, resulting in higher rates of unemployment, poverty, and unsafe housing conditions.

Relevance: Forces individuals into survival crimes like breaking and entering.

Cultural Gaps & The Risk Assessment Trap

Western and Native systems view accountability and truth in fundamentally different ways. This divide often worsens trauma in conventional courts.

Western Legal System

  • Adversarial: Focuses on state vs. accused. The defense lawyer tries to win or get the charges dropped, often advising silence.
  • Retributive: The primary goal is punishment (jail time) to deter others.
  • Hierarchical: The judge sits elevated in black robes. The accused sits at a separate desk, isolated and passive.
  • Focus: What law was broken and how much punishment is required.

Indigenous Justice Circle

  • Restorative: Focuses on healing relations. The victim, offender, and community collaborate.
  • Reintegration: The goal is active restitution and restoring the offender's role in the community.
  • Non-hierarchical: Everyone sits in an equal circle at the same height, symbolizing shared responsibility.
  • Focus: What harm was done, how we got here, and how we repair it together.

Why Indigenous People are Assessed as "High Risk"

Standard bail and sentencing checklists determine if an offender is 'high risk' using criteria that penalize poverty and history:

  • Employment & Housing Status: Scoring points for being unemployed or having no permanent address (which are socioeconomic inequalities, not crime indicators).
  • Family Instability: Checking off points for a history of family separation or trauma (penalizing the legacies of the Sixties Scoop and residential schools).
  • Lack of Local Support: Factoring in a lack of institutional references or local credit (ignoring traditional family care systems).
  • Prior Convictions: Ignoring that prior arrests were often the result of profiling, leading to a compounding scoring trap.

Slide Reflection (First Nations in the Justice System)

These reflection questions represent the key classroom prompts from the slides. Use them to frame your answers in Part 3 of the assignment.

Key Inquest Discussion Prompts

  • Link with Systemic Discrimination:

    How do rules that apply to everyone equally (like having a credit record or fixed address for bail) end up treating Indigenous people unequally because of historical land theft and status exclusions?

  • Limitation of Opportunities:

    In what ways have discriminatory policies (the Indian Act's reserve pass system, lack of school funding, status loss) limited the job, housing, and social options for youth like Liam?

  • Addressing the Inequity:

    Why does the government need to support community-led programs (like the Mi'kmaw Legal Support Network) instead of just funding bigger prisons and courts?
