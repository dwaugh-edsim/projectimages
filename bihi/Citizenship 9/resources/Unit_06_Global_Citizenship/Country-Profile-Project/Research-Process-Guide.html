Research Process Guide

How to research and build your Country Profile Project

Start Here

Don't try to research everything at once. Follow these steps in order - each one builds on the last.

What Are References and Why Do We Use Them?

Referencing means giving credit to the sources you used for information in your project. It's not optional - it's a required part of all academic work, from high school through university and beyond.

Why Do We Reference?

  • Give credit - The people who did the research deserve recognition for their work
  • Show your work - Proves you didn't just make things up - you actually researched
  • Help others - Lets readers find the same information you found if they want to learn more
  • Avoid plagiarism - Using someone's work without credit is academic dishonesty and can have serious consequences
  • Build trust - Shows you've done proper research with real, credible sources

Why High School and University Require This

In high school, referencing teaches you academic integrity - the habit of being honest about where your information comes from. In university, this becomes even more critical. Universities have strict plagiarism policies, and failing to cite sources can result in failing grades, academic probation, or even expulsion.

The good news? The habits you build now - citing sources, using citation generators, checking your work - will serve you well throughout your academic career. It's a skill you'll use in every course from now on.

What Does a Reference Look Like?

For this project, we use APA style. A basic reference includes:

  • Author or Organization - Who created the information?
  • Year - When was it published?
  • Title - What is the name of the article/page?
  • URL - Where can it be found online?
Government of Canada. (2022). Canada's National Adaptation Strategy. https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/climate-change/adaptation/national-strategy.html
UNFCCC. (2023). Tuvalu's Updated NDC. https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/NDC/2023-02/Tuvalu.pdf

Using a Citation Generator (Recommended)

You don't need to memorize APA formatting rules. Use a free citation generator to create your references:

BibGuru

bibguru.com - Fast, supports many source types

MyBib

How to Use a Citation Generator:

  1. Copy the URL of your source
  2. Paste it into the citation generator
  3. Select "APA 7th edition" as the style
  4. Copy the generated citation
  5. Paste it at the bottom of your slide

Important Notes

Every slide with information from a source needs a citation at the bottom. Images also need credit if they're not your own. Wikipedia is a starting point - cite the actual sources Wikipedia uses, not Wikipedia itself. Always check that the generated citation looks correct before adding it to your slides.

1 First: Get Oriented

Before diving into deep research, build a basic understanding of your country:

  1. Find your country on a map. Know which continent it's on, what its neighbors are, and what ocean might affect it.
  2. Read the Wikipedia intro. Seriously - the first paragraph of Wikipedia tells you the most important basics. Read it twice.
  3. 3 facts you must know:
    • Where is it?
    • What language do people speak there?
    • What is the government type?

You don't need to memorize everything

You need enough background to write 3-4 sentences about your country. Save the detailed research for later.

2 Understand Your Global Issue

Your topic has two parts: a country AND a global issue. Make sure you can explain the issue clearly.

Common Mistake

"Climate change" is NOT specific enough. Climate change is the global issue. Your country's RESPONSE to climate change is what your project is about. Same with "poverty" - poverty is the global issue. What is YOUR country doing about it?

Ask yourself:

  • What exactly is the problem? (e.g., sea level rise, air pollution, human rights violations)
  • Why does this problem affect the whole world, not just one country?
  • How does this problem specifically affect THIS country?

What is NOT a global issue

A country's internal disputes that don't cross borders, or local problems with no international connection. Your issue should impact multiple countries.

3 Research Your Country's Response

This is the CORE of your project. You need to answer: What has the government DONE?

Where to search:

Government Websites

Search "[country name] government [issue] policy" or "[country name] ministry"

Wikipedia

Look for your country's page on the specific issue, not just general Wikipedia

News Search

Try "[country name] [issue] policy 2023" or "[country name] [issue] response"

UN/NGO Reports

International organizations often monitor country responses

Search tips:

  • Most countries have an NDC (climate pledge to the UN) - search "[country] NDC"
  • Human rights = try "[country] human rights report" or "[country] OHCHR"
  • Climate = try "[country] climate adaptation plan"
  • Conflict/refugees = try "[country] migration policy" or "[country] UNHCR"

Watch Out For

Make sure you find what they HAVE done, not just what they SAID they would do. Politicians promise things but follow-through varies. Look for evidence of ACTIONS, not just announcements.

4 Identify Consequences

For every action (or inaction), ask: What happened because of this?

Use this framework:

  • Intended - Was this the result they wanted?
  • Unintended - Did something unexpected happen too?
  • Short-term - Is this happening now or soon?
  • Long-term - Will this matter in 10+ years?
  • Who suffers - Citizens? The environment? The economy?

Example

If your country built a seawall: The intended consequence was flood protection. But an unintended consequence might be that seawalls elsewhere eroded faster. Short-term: jobs created. Long-term: could the seawall actually worsen erosion elsewhere?

5 Research Canada

Find Canada's response to the SAME issue. This isn't a geography lesson - you need:

  • What has Canada actually DONE about this issue?
  • Is Canada's approach similar or different from your country?
  • Why might their approaches be different? (resources? politics? geography?)

Good Search Terms

"Canada climate adaptation strategy", "Canada [issue] policy", "Canada climate finance"

6 Evaluate Your Sources

Not all sources are equal. Before using one, ask:

Government of Canada. (2023). Canada's National Adaptation Strategy. https://www.canada.ca/adaptation-strategy.html
UNFCCC. (2023). Tuvalu's Updated NDC. https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/NDC/2023-02/Tuvalu.pdf
The Guardian, Reuters, BBC. News articles from established outlets with named journalists.
"Climate change is bad and countries need to act!" - no author, no date, blog post
Wikipedia without checking the citations - use Wikipedia as a STARTING POINT but verify claims in the citations

Source check:

  • Who wrote it? (name or organization)
  • When was it written? (is it recent enough?)
  • Is it biased? (does it try to persuasion rather than inform?)

7 Build Your Outline First

Before opening PowerPoint or Google Slides:

Your Checkpoint 2 Outline Should Include:

Slide 1: Title + Country + Issue + Names
Slide 2: Country background (2-3 sentences on geography, culture, economy)
Slide 3: What the global issue is + why it matters + how it affects your country
Slide 4: What your country HAS done (actions) + what they have NOT done (inaction)
Slide 5: Consequences organized by intended/unintended + short/long-term + who is affected
Slide 6: Canada's response + comparison to your country + why Canadians should care
At least 2 credible sources noted for each slide

Less is More

6 slides doesn't mean 6 pages of text per slide. Use bullet points, not sentences. Your audience needs to read AND listen to you at the same time.

8 Build the Slides

Now you can make your slideshow. Some tips:

  • One idea per slide - Don't cram multiple topics onto one slide
  • Visuals > text - Photos, maps, and diagrams help your audience understand
  • Cite as you go - Add source info at the bottom of each slide, not just at the end
  • Skip the full sentences - Bullet points = faster to read, clearer to understand

Practice Your Presentation

Your slides should guide you, not BE your presentation. You should be able to explain each slide in 1-2 minutes without reading directly from it.

Recommended Timeline

Claim your topic and write 1-2 paragraphs on your country and the issue

Suggested: Week 1-2

Checkpoint 2: Outline

6-slide outline with bullet points and 2+ sources noted

Suggested: Week 2-3

Actual slideshow (can be rough). Focus on content first.

Suggested: Week 3-4

Checkpoint 4: Final Presentation

Polished slides + presentation practice

Suggested: Week 4-5

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Too much text per slide - Maximum 5-6 bullet points per slide, keep bullets short
  • Describing the problem but not the response - Your project is about WHAT THE COUNTRY DID
  • Using only Wikipedia - Great starting point, but check the actual sources Wikipedia cites
  • No citations - Every slide with information needs a source noted
  • Waiting until the last minute - This project requires research time, not just slide design
