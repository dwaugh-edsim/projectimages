### Section 2: Short Answer (Model Responses)

**26.** Section 88 (1951) of the *Indian Act* allowed provincial laws — including child welfare laws — to apply on reserves. It matters because it gave provincial child welfare agencies the legal power to remove Indigenous children from reserves, which is what made the mass apprehensions of the Sixties Scoop (1960s–1980s) legally possible.

**27.** The three main goals of the residential school system were: (1) separate Indigenous children from their families and communities; (2) ban their languages, traditions, and cultural practices; (3) replace Indigenous culture with English (or French) language, Christianity, and Euro-Canadian ways of life. The TRC describes this forced assimilation as "cultural genocide."

**28.** Section 35 of the *Constitution Act, 1982* recognizes and protects existing Indigenous and treaty rights in Canada. It matters because it is the highest legal recognition of Indigenous and treaty rights in the Canadian constitution, and it has been used in courts (including the 1999 *Marshall* decision) to confirm that Mi'kmaw treaty rights — including the right to hunt, fish, and gather for a moderate livelihood — continue today.

**29.** Donald Marshall Jr. was a Mi'kmaw teenager in Sydney, Nova Scotia, who was wrongly convicted of murder in 1971 and spent 11 years in prison. The 1989 Royal Commission on his case concluded that systemic racism in the Nova Scotia justice system caused his wrongful conviction, and it pushed the province to create restorative justice supports (such as the Mi'kmaw Legal Support Network) for Indigenous people.

**30.** Gladue Rights come from a 1999 Supreme Court decision and require Canadian judges to consider the unique background and circumstances of Indigenous offenders when sentencing, and to look for community-led alternatives to jail where possible. The purpose is to reduce the overrepresentation of Indigenous people in Canadian prisons and to recognize that traditional and restorative justice approaches may be more appropriate.

**31.** *Sample for Call #62–65:* These Calls ask schools to integrate Indigenous history, residential schools, treaties, and Indigenous contributions into the curriculum. They matter for a Grade 9 classroom because students need to learn the truth about this history to participate in reconciliation. Without this teaching, the Calls stay on paper and the next generation cannot carry the work forward.

### Section 3: Long Answer (Rubric Above)

*Sample strong response:*

For situation (a), two concepts apply: the **Sixties Scoop** and **Section 88**. Section 88 of the *Indian Act* (1951) allowed provincial child welfare laws onto reserves, which made the mass apprehension of Indigenous children legally possible. Although the Sixties Scoop officially ended in the 1980s, many Indigenous children are still removed from their families at rates higher than non-Indigenous children — sometimes called the "Millennial Scoop." The family may also be experiencing **intergenerational trauma** from residential schools, which makes child welfare involvement feel especially threatening.

For situation (b), the school's failure to follow protocol disrespects **treaty relationships** and Indigenous knowledge systems. The Peace and Friendship Treaties (1725–1779) established an ongoing relationship between the Crown and the Mi'kmaq; schools, as Crown institutions, are part of that relationship. Proper protocol (opening prayer, gift, introduction) is not a formality — it is how the school shows it takes the relationship seriously.

Reconciliation asks the school to: learn the truth about why these patterns exist; follow the TRC's Calls to Action (especially those on child welfare and education); build respectful relationships with Mi'kmaw communities; and respect treaty rights.

Two concrete actions: (1) Partner with a Mi'kmaw organization (e.g., the Mi'kmaw Native Friendship Centre or Treaty Education Nova Scotia — *teacher verify current status*) to review child welfare cases involving Indigenous families and to support culturally appropriate responses. (2) Adopt and consistently follow Mi'kmaw protocol for Elder visits — including a territory acknowledgment, opening prayer if the Elder offers one, an honorarium or gift, and proper introduction. Train all staff on this protocol.
