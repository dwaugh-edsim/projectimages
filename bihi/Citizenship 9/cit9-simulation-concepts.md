# Citizenship 9 — Simulation / Activity Concepts (for selection)

**Purpose:** 2–3 activity/simulation concept options per unit. Mark your choice per unit with `[x]`, amend notes inline. Selected concepts get scaffolded into `outline.md` as multi-session blocks (with session counts calibrated to term length).

**Key difference from HL9:** Cit9 is NOT a simulation-heavy course. It's a discussion, case-study, project, and advocacy course. Most "simulations" here are **lighter** than HL9's branching narrative sims. Default formats: **case-study analysis, Socratic seminar, mock trial / mock parliament / mock city council, role-play scenarios, media analysis, project-based learning (especially service learning), and case-file investigations.** No `code.gs` / Sheets dashboard needed for most — Google Classroom / Docs / Slides submissions are usually enough.

**The course's spine:** Unit 1 (Engaged Citizenship / Service Learning Project) is the central project of the course and spans ~6–8 weeks minimum. Everything else orbits around it.

**Shared architecture (lighter than HL9):**
**Briefing → Activity → Discussion/Reflection → Documentation/Submission → Teacher Feedback**

**Legend per concept:**
- **Hook** — the scenario/setup that pulls students in
- **Activity** — what students actually do (2–3 sentences)
- **Discussion/debrief** — 2–3 prompts that close the activity
- **Documentation** — what students submit (Doc, Slides, audio, etc.)
- **Build** — rough effort (Light / Medium / Heavy)
- **Resources** — which Cit9 NAS folders/files it draws on
- **Notes** — concerns, cross-unit connections, scaffolding tips

> **Note on resources:** the `Cit9` folder on the NAS holds starting materials for several units. Folder references in each concept are real (e.g., `ServiceLearningProposal/`, `PerceptionVsReality/`, `political-spectrum/`, `cuba62/`, etc.). For folders I couldn't directly inspect from this sandbox, I've flagged them as "verify contents" rather than guess at filenames.

## MM Studies 11 Spiral Note (How this course ladders up)

Several Indigenous-focused simulations in this file (specifically **all three Unit 2 options** and **three of the five Unit 5 options**) are **deliberately recycled from Mi'kmaw Studies 11** at a Grade 9 conceptual level. The goal is **spiral curriculum**, not redundancy: in Grade 9, students encounter the *concepts, characters, and case files* for the first time; in Grade 11, they encounter them again with more depth, more analysis, and the assessment weight.

**What gets recycled:**
- **Annie Francis Case File** (Grade 9 Unit 2A → Grade 11 sees the same fictional family in fuller context)
- **Donald Marshall Jr.** case (Grade 9 Unit 2B → Grade 11 Unit 13 "Justice Denied" revisits as one of five wrongful convictions)
- **Sentencing Circles vs Western Court** (Grade 9 Unit 2C → Grade 11 Unit 13 deepens with Gladue Rights + Bill C-5)
- **Sipekne'katik 2020** dispute (Grade 9 Unit 5C → connects to Grade 11 Unit 11 Government / Self-Determination)
- **Idle No More** (Grade 9 Unit 5D → Grade 11 Unit 12 Protest analysis)
- **Ipperwash 1995 / Dudley George** (Grade 9 Unit 5E → Grade 11 Unit 12 "One Dead Indian" feature)

**Why this matters pedagogically:**
1. **First-exposure safety net.** Grade 9 students meet Indigenous histories, characters, and frameworks in a low-stakes context. By Grade 11, these aren't "new" — they're familiar, which frees cognitive load for deeper analysis.
2. **Cultural humility windows.** Grade 9 introductions should ideally be **co-facilitated with Mi'kmaw education consultants or Knowledge Keepers**. Where that's not possible, lean more heavily on written sources, archive materials, and explicit framing ("this is a *fictional* case study created for our class to show how policies affected real families"). Grade 11 sims then take students deeper into real cases with proper contextual scaffolding.
3. **Honest scope.** We are NOT trying to teach the full depth of Mi'kmaw Studies 11 in Grade 9. We are laying foundations of *awareness, vocabulary, case familiarity, and empathy* — the harder analytical work happens in Grade 11.

**What stays non-Indigenous in this course:** Units 1, 3, 4, 6 activities remain on their existing non-Indigenous tracks (Service Learning, Financial Citizenship, Digital Citizenship, Global Citizenship). Those units have strong existing concepts (Living Wage Month, Spot the Bias, Global Issue Court, etc.) that don't need MM recycling.

## Unit 1 — Engaged Citizenship (Service Learning) — *COURSE SPINE*

*Sub-themes: Identifying a community need · Evaluating personal skills/strengths/fit · Planning a service-learning project · Implementing the plan · Evaluating citizenship-skill development*

> This unit is the **backbone of the entire course** and spans roughly 6–8 weeks minimum. Most of the "simulation" is real-world implementation, not role-play. Below are three concept scaffolds — pick the project model that best fits the school/community.

### [ ] A — "THE NEED HUNT" → Real Project (community-identified-need model)
- **Hook:** The class is a "citizen action team." Before any planning, they need to find a real need in the school or local community that's worth their time. The wrong need means a wasted project.
- **Activity:** Teams of 3–4 spend the first week doing a structured **Need Audit**: 5 informant interviews (students, staff, neighbours, community orgs), 2 walks of the surrounding neighbourhood with a photo journal, and a review of local news + charity data. They pitch 2 candidate needs to the class; the class votes on ONE shared need to tackle. The remainder of the unit becomes the actual project (planning → implementation → evaluation). Service Learning runs **alongside** Units 2–6 as a recurring thread.
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) Which need had the strongest evidence? (2) Whose voices were easiest/hardest to include? (3) If the vote went differently, what need would you have chased and why?
- **Documentation:** Need Audit Report (Doc), Interview Transcripts (anonymized), Photo Journal (Slides), final Service-Learning Proposal (from `ServiceLearningProposal/` template), Implementation Log, Evaluation Reflection.
- **Build:** Heavy (this is the term-long project — not a one-shot sim). · **Resources:** `ServiceLearningIntro.html`, `ServiceLearningProposal/`, `ServiceLearningProject/`, `ServiceLearningMenu.html`, `ServiceLearningResponse.html`
- **Notes:** This is the canonical model. Real implementation with a real community partner is the highest-value, highest-effort option. Strongest fit if a community partner is already lined up (food bank, seniors' home, school breakfast program, habitat for humanity, local environmental group).

### [ ] B — "PARTNER PROJECT" (partner-organization model)
- **Hook:** The teacher has pre-arranged partnerships with 2–3 community organizations. Each student picks (or is assigned) a partner and works within their existing structure — no need to "discover" the need, it's already validated.
- **Activity:** Week 1 — orientation to each partner org (a representative visits or video-calls in). Weeks 2–3 — students research the org, interview staff, identify where they can add value. Weeks 4–7 — actual service (sorting, mentoring, building, cleaning, outreach). Week 8 — students present back to the partner AND the class. Stronger focus on **student-led reflection** rather than project design.
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) What surprised you about how this organization actually operates? (2) Where did your plan and reality diverge? (3) What does "useful help" vs. "performative help" look like?
- **Documentation:** Partner Profile (Doc/Slides), Hours Log, Implementation Journal (weekly), final Reflection Presentation back to the partner, Evaluation Rubric (self + partner feedback).
- **Build:** Medium (teacher logistics dominate; student design work is lighter). · **Resources:** `ServiceLearningProposal/` (lighter use), `ServiceLearningProject/`, `ServiceLearningResponse.html`
- **Notes:** Lower student agency but higher reliability. Good fit for first-year teachers or schools with established partnerships. Easier to assess because each partner can give direct feedback.

### [ ] C — "STUDENT-DESIGNED MICRO-PROJECT" (autonomy model)
- **Hook:** Each student (or pair) identifies and runs **their own** small service-learning project. The teacher's role is coach, not designer. Highest student agency, highest variance in quality.
- **Activity:** Students propose, plan, implement, and evaluate a small project of their choosing — minimum 8 hours of direct service, must address a real community need, must involve at least one partner org or stakeholder group. Weekly check-ins with the teacher. Mid-point pitch (like a reality-show "judges' table") to surface struggling projects early. Final showcase at term's end.
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) Where did the plan go sideways and what did you learn from it? (2) What citizenship skills did you develop that you didn't expect? (3) Whose definition of "the community" did your project centre?
- **Documentation:** Individual Proposal (using `ServiceLearningProposal/` template), Weekly Reflection Journal, Hours Log + Verification (signed by partner), Final Showcase Presentation, Self-Evaluation using `ServiceLearningResponse.html`.
- **Build:** Heavy on teacher coaching (light on teacher design). · **Resources:** `ServiceLearningProposal/`, `ServiceLearningProject/`, `ServiceLearningMenu.html`, `ServiceLearningResponse.html`
- **Notes:** Best for self-directed classes. Risky if students lack follow-through — the mid-point "judges' table" exists precisely to catch drift. Consider pairing weaker students with stronger ones during proposal stage.

## Unit 2 — Who Am I as a Citizen? (Rights, Mi'kmaw + Reconciliation) — *MM-RECYCLED*

*Sub-themes: Rights & responsibilities · Worldview & citizenship · Personal strengths · Evolving citizenship over time · Mi'kmaw rights & treaty rights · Reconciliation*

> **MM Spiral:** All three Unit 2 options below are recycled from Mi'kmaw Studies 11 at a Grade 9 conceptual level — see the **MM Studies 11 Spiral Note** at the top of this file for the rationale. Students meet the case files, characters, and concepts first; Grade 11 deepens them. Where possible, **co-facilitate with a Mi'kmaw knowledge-keeper** for the Unit 2 opening session to set a respectful tone.

### [ ] A — "ANNIE FRANCIS CASE FILE" (Sixties Scoop simulation)

> *Source: MM Studies 11 — fictional case study of an 8-year-old Mi'kmaw girl apprehended in 1963, renamed Anne Peters. Full source character/family tree in `mm-june-guide.md` Part 04.*

- **Hook:** It's 1963. You are a Nova Scotia child welfare worker. A form lands on your desk: an apprehension recommendation for an 8-year-old Mi'kmaw girl named Annie Francis (she'll be renamed Anne Peters in your files). You must decide — apprehend, or not?
- **Activity:** Students receive the **fictional 1963 welfare form** (to be assembled by teacher — see Build note below) and complete a **decision matrix**: family's "deficiencies" as listed, your reading of the underlying causes, your decision, your one-paragraph rationale. Then a structured debrief reveals the systemic-bias layer: the form's language ("material poverty," "lacks standard municipal infrastructure," "demonstrate lack of resource management") judges the family by wealthy-city standards, while ignoring that reserve poverty was *created* by government reserve rules, land loss, and Section 88 (added to the Indian Act in 1951 to apply provincial child-welfare laws on reserve). Optional second pass: read the **fictional family tree** (Joseph & Lena Francis with Branches A and B) and compare how Annie Francis (physical removal = Sixties Scoop) and Mary Francis Johnson (legal status loss = Indian Act second-generation cut-off) represent two methods of the same colonial erasure.
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) Where did the welfare form's language already pre-judge the family before you arrived at a decision? (2) How did Section 88 (1951) make this kind of apprehension legally possible? (3) What did changing Annie Francis's name to Anne Peters do — symbolically and bureaucratically?
- **Documentation:** Completed Decision Matrix (Doc), one-page Written Recommendation, one-page Reflection on systemic bias.
- **Build:** Light · **Resources:** `mm-june-guide.md` Part 04 (Annie Francis Case File, fictional family tree, Section 88 context); MM Studies 11 Unit 14 "Sixties Scoop Timeline"; MM Studies 11 Unit 11 Government folder.
- **Notes:** ⚠️ **The Annie Francis / Anne Peters / Joseph & Lena Francis characters are FICTIONAL** — created for classroom use. Make this explicit on the form and on the assignment sheet. The historical context (Section 88, the Sixties Scoop's actual scale, the proportion of Indigenous children in care from 1955 to today) is REAL. Where possible, **coordinate with a Mi'kmaw education consultant or Elder before delivery** — at minimum, review the source `mm-june-guide.md` Part 04 with the school board's Indigenous education lead. Two branches of the family tree (Annie = physical erasure; Mary = legal erasure) map nicely to two comparative debrief questions. Optional extension: read *The Outside Circle* (Patti LaBoucane-Benson) as companion text — already used in MM 11.

### [ ] B — "DONALD MARSHALL JR.: WRONGFUL CONVICTION" (case study)

> *Source: MM Studies 11 Part 07 + the real Donald Marshall Jr. case (1971 Sydney, NS murder conviction; 11 years in prison; 1989 Royal Commission).*

- **Hook:** In 1971 in Sydney, Nova Scotia, a 17-year-old Mi'kmaw teenager named Donald Marshall Jr. was convicted of a murder he did not commit. He spent 11 years in prison. Twenty-two years later, a judge would call it "a gross miscarriage of justice" caused by systemic racism. Your job: trace how it happened.
- **Activity:** Working with a teacher-prepared **case timeline** (built from the MM source: `mm-june-guide.md` Part 07 "Real Precedents: Donald Marshall Jr. & Gladue Rights"), students map the **points of systemic failure**: how was the investigation conducted? Who was charged and why? What evidence was ignored? What role did race play? Then they propose a **restorative justice response** — what should have happened once the injustice was clear, and what did the 1989 Royal Commission actually establish (Mi'kmaw Legal Support Network, focus on Gladue Rights, etc.)?
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) How does a wrongful conviction happen, step by step, in a "working" justice system? (2) What does the Marshall case tell us about how Indigenous people were (and are) treated in Canadian courts? (3) What role does Gladue Rights play today — and what's the "Gladue funding gap"?
- **Documentation:** Timeline Analysis (visual timeline with annotated points of failure), one-page Reflection, Restorative Response Proposal (1 page).
- **Build:** Light · **Resources:** `mm-june-guide.md` Part 07 (Donald Marshall Jr. section); MM Studies 11 Unit 13 "Justice Denied" folder (5 wrongful-conviction case studies); secondary real-world sources (CBC archives, Mi'kmaw Legal Support Network public materials). The original `donaldmarshall/` folder referenced in MM is **not present in this sandbox** — teacher should pre-build a 1-page case brief from public sources if it cannot be located.
- **Notes:** ⚠️ **This is a real case with a real person and his family.** Donald Marshall Jr. died in 2009; the case is regularly covered in NS Mi'kmaw-community education. Handle with care — avoid any tone of "solving a mystery"; the answer (systemic racism) is known, the learning is in *seeing* how. Pair with a note about Marshall's later life and his role in the 1999 *Marshall Decision* on treaty fishing rights. Strong connection to Unit 5 governance content.

### [ ] C — "SENTENCING CIRCLE vs WESTERN COURT" (compare/contrast activity)

> *Source: MM Studies 11 Part 07 — Western Adversarial Court vs Mi'kmaw Sentencing Circle comparison framework, and the AI-generated graphic novel "Two Paths: Liam's Journey" in the `MM Studies\Justice` folder.*

- **Hook:** Two paths of justice. Same incident. Two completely different legal responses. One is the conventional system you know — courtroom, robes, gavels, elevated bench, and a checklist scoring poverty as "risk." The other is a circle of chairs at equal height, an Elder, community support, and the question: "what harm was done and how do we repair it?"
- **Activity:** Students compare the two systems using the visual and conceptual frameworks in the graphic novel **"Two Paths: Liam's Journey"**. They analyze Liam's arrest (breaking a store window in Sydney, Cape Breton to escape freezing cold) and compare a conventional court sentencing to a community-led Sentencing Circle (comprising Elder, store owner Mr. Thomas, support worker Claire, Liam, and the judge). They run role-plays or analyze case guides based on the script, then compare outcomes, physical setup, and felt experience. Culminating question: what is the difference between formal equality and substantive equality?
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) How did the physical structure of the circle change how Liam and the store owner interacted compared to a conventional court? (2) How did the Gladue background (residential school legacy) change how the circle understood the harm? (3) Why does treating everyone *exactly the same* (formal equality) sometimes lead to unjust results?
- **Documentation:** **Venn Diagram comparison** (goals, participants, structure, outcomes, harms repaired), one-page Reflection on substantive equality based on Liam's story.
- **Build:** Light–Medium · **Resources:** `MM Studies\Justice` folder, including the graphic novel script, `studentactivity.md`, and the image file `liam_snowstorm.png`.
- **Notes:** ⚠️ **Cultural humility required** — do NOT have non-Indigenous students role-play Mi'kmaw Elders or knowledge keepers. Keep the Elder role as a guide or facilitator role focusing on restorative circle principles, or use the published script case study analysis rather than role-playing the ceremony. Strong bridge to Unit 5 governance content (protest, self-determination, and Gladue Rights).

## Unit 3 — Financial Citizenship

*Sub-themes: Cultural ideas of needs/wants/money/value · Purpose & types of taxation · Government priorities & economic equality · Economic disparity · Fairness vs. equality · Personal financial management · Goal setting · Impact of financial choices on self and others*

### [ ] A — "LIVING WAGE MONTH" (budget simulation)
- **Hook:** You're 22, just graduated, working a full-time minimum-wage job in Halifax. Rent is due. So is the phone bill. Your car just made a noise. And your friend just asked you to chip in for a weekend trip. Can you make it work for 30 days — and still save anything?
- **Activity:** Each student is given (or picks) a fictional profile: age 22, single, full-time minimum-wage job, specific rent scenario (with roommate / solo / living at home), a debt load (or not), and a list of recurring expenses based on real Halifax-region costs. They maintain a **30-day budget log** with weekly checkpoints. Unexpected events hit at random (car repair, phone replacement, medical expense, friend gets married out of province, layoff threat). At the end of the month, students write a one-page reflection: what worked, what didn't, and what this experience taught them about decisions made by people on low incomes.
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) Where in the budget did you have actual choice, and where was it fixed? (2) What did the "unexpected events" teach you about how thin the margin is? (3) How would your decisions shift if you had a child? A disability? A sick parent?
- **Documentation:** Budget Log (Sheet or paper), Profile Justification (why you chose your scenario), one-page Reflection Essay, Discussion Post in Google Classroom.
- **Build:** Medium · **Resources:** Real local cost-of-living data (HRM rent averages, NS minimum wage, NB/NS utility averages — teacher compiles), optional guest speaker from a credit counselling service.
- **Notes:** This activity has real empathy-building power when done seriously. Some students will struggle emotionally if they're already living this reality — have an opt-out alternative (e.g., analyse a published budget diary from a published source) and a clear note that this is fictional. Excellent bridge into the taxation unit.

### [ ] B — "FOLLOW THE TAX DOLLAR" (taxation scenarios)
- **Hook:** You just got your first paycheque and it's smaller than you expected — where did the missing money go? Follow it. All of it. Where does your tax dollar actually end up?
- **Activity:** Students start with their own (or a fictional) paystub and identify every deduction (income tax, CPP, EI, maybe union dues, etc.). They pick **one tax stream** to trace — say, $5 of income tax — and follow it through three levels of government: federal (where does it go? defence? health transfers? Indigenous services?), provincial (healthcare? education? highways?), municipal (transit? garbage? libraries?). Each student builds a **"Tax Dollar Flow Map"** showing where their dollar actually ended up, then compares it to where they *thought* it went before the research. Then a Socratic seminar: if you had to CUT 10% of next year's spending, what would you cut and why?
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) Where did your dollar go that surprised you? (2) Which programs have you personally benefited from that you didn't realize were publicly funded? (3) What's the difference between "I don't want to pay taxes" and "I want my taxes to go somewhere different"?
- **Documentation:** Tax Dollar Flow Map (visual — Slides, poster, or infographic), Pre/Post Survey ("Where did you think your tax dollar went?" before and after), Socratic Seminar Prep Notes + Reflection.
- **Build:** Medium · **Resources:** Real federal/provincial/municipal budget summaries (teacher-curated, available publicly), CRA resources, NS Budget documents.
- **Notes:** Strongest fit for the "purpose/value/types of taxation" indicator AND the "government priorities & economic equality" indicator. Don't get trapped in partisan arguments — frame it as "where does the money go?" not "is taxation good?" Pairs naturally with Unit 5 (Governance) on political party platforms — same data, different frame.

### [ ] C — "FAIR vs. EQUAL" (economic decision-making scenarios)
- **Hook:** Three neighbours each earn different incomes. The government has $1,000 to give them. Do you split it equally? By need? By merit? By what they "deserve"? There's no neutral answer — and the answer you pick reveals what you think "fair" means.
- **Activity:** Students work through **5–6 economic decision scenarios**, each with three distribution options: equal split, need-based split, merit-based split (and sometimes a 4th: lottery / random). Scenarios include: welfare payments, scholarship distribution, disaster relief, employment insurance, tax breaks for low-income workers, infrastructure spending on wealthy vs. poor neighbourhoods. For each scenario, students pick the option they'd defend and write a one-paragraph rationale. Then small-group debates: who picked differently and why? Culminating concept-map: how is "fairness" different from "equality" — and when do they overlap or collide?
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) Did your distribution preference shift as the scenarios changed? (2) Where does "desert" (what you deserve) come from — effort, luck, or context? (3) How does your answer change if one of the neighbours is you?
- **Documentation:** Scenario Response Log (Doc), Concept Map (visual), Reflection: "What kind of economic citizen am I?"
- **Build:** Light–Medium · **Resources:** No specific NAS files — teacher-curated scenarios. Could pair with `countryoftheday/` case studies (Unit 6) for cross-national economic comparisons.
- **Notes:** Very high discussion yield, very low prep. Excellent Socratic seminar material. Watch for students who shut down when politics enters — set ground rules that the goal is to understand positions, not win them. Feeds directly into Unit 5 political party platform analysis (liberals vs. conservatives vs. NDP often disagree on exactly these scenarios).

## Unit 4 — Digital Citizenship

*Sub-themes: Media representation & omission · Social/mass media influence on awareness · Citizens' and governments' choices affected by media · Digital opportunities & risks · Digital footprint · Anonymity in production & consumption of media*

### [ ] A — "SPOT THE BIAS" (10-source media audit)
- **Hook:** Same story. Same day. Ten different outlets. The headline is different in each. Who's framing it how — and why does it matter which one you read?
- **Activity:** Pick one current news story (ideally with political/ethical weight — a protest, a court ruling, a policy announcement, an international event). Students collect coverage from **at least 10 sources spanning the spectrum**: CBC, CTV, Global, National Post, Globe & Mail, Toronto Star, Breitbart/True North (or its equivalent), a Mi'kmaw/Indigenous outlet (e.g., APTN, SaltWire), an international outlet (BBC, Al Jazeera), and at least one social-media thread (X, Reddit, TikTok). For each source they record: **headline, lead, who gets quoted, who doesn't, what visuals are used, what words are loaded, what's missing.** Then a class comparison: where do the stories converge, where do they diverge, and what does the pattern tell you?
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) Whose voice was most often quoted? Whose was missing? (2) Which headline would most change your opinion of the event if it were the only one you saw? (3) What does "objectivity" actually mean when sources frame the same event differently?
- **Documentation:** Source Comparison Chart (Sheet), Bias Audit Essay (one source, deep dive), Class Discussion Post.
- **Build:** Medium · **Resources:** `PerceptionVsReality/` (verify contents — looks directly on-theme), `DigCit/` (verify contents), current news archives.
- **Notes:** This is the centerpiece activity for the "media shapes perception" indicators. Time it for a news-heavy week. Strongest learning happens when students discover that even their "trusted" source has blind spots. Set ground rules for discussing partisan sources — focus on framing, not on whether the source is "right."

### [ ] B — "FOOTPRINT FORENSICS" (digital footprint audit)
- **Hook:** A college admissions officer, a future employer, and a first-date Googler all type your name into a search bar. What do they find? Are you who you think you are online?
- **Activity:** Each student does a **personal digital footprint audit** of themselves (with an opt-out option that uses a fictional profile): Google themselves, screenshot the first 3 pages of results, audit their public social-media profiles (last 10 posts on each), review privacy settings on 3 platforms, identify what data each platform collects (check privacy policies briefly), and reflect on what a stranger could learn about them. They build a **"Footprint Profile"** showing what they intended to project vs. what actually shows up. Then a class discussion: who is the "you" that the internet sees — and is it the you you'd choose?
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) What did you find that you didn't expect? (2) What's the gap between your "real" self and your "footprint" self? (3) What should change — your behaviour, the platforms' design, or both?
- **Documentation:** Footprint Profile (Doc or Slides), Privacy Settings Action Plan ("3 changes I'll make this week"), Reflection Essay.
- **Build:** Light–Medium · **Resources:** `DigCit/` (verify contents), `PerceptionVsReality/`.
- **Notes:** ⚠️ Some students may have complicated home situations visible in their footprints (absent parent searches, court records, etc.). Build in a real opt-out: students may use a fictional profile for the audit, OR you may do the audit on a public figure instead. Avoid forcing vulnerability. Strong fit for the "digital footprint influences others' perceptions" indicator.

### [ ] C — "ANONYMOUS OR NOT" (anonymity case studies)
- **Hook:** A Reddit user posts a racist rant about a Halifax business. The business loses customers and sues for defamation. The user says: "I'm anonymous, you can't touch me." Are they right? Who decides?
- **Activity:** Students work through **4–5 case studies** where anonymous online speech caused real consequences (defamation, doxxing, harassment, whistleblowing, false accusations, organized pile-ons). For each case, they analyse: **What harm was done? Who was harmed? Was anonymity the cause, an enabler, or a red herring? What should the response be — legal, platform, cultural?** Then a Socratic seminar on the central tension: anonymity protects dissidents AND protects abusers. How do we tell which is which?
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) When has anonymous speech changed the world for the better? (2) When has it destroyed someone's life? (3) Where's the line between privacy and accountability — and who draws it?
- **Documentation:** Case Study Analyses (one Doc per case or one combined Doc), Socratic Seminar Prep + Reflection, "Anonymity Position" one-pager (where do YOU land and why?).
- **Build:** Light–Medium · **Resources:** `DigCit/` (verify contents), teacher-curated real cases from news archives.
- **Notes:** Strongest fit for the "role of anonymity in production and consumption of media" indicator — that's the most unique indicator in the curriculum and shouldn't be skipped. Be careful with cases that hit close to student experience (cyberbullying) — frame as systemic, not personal.

## Unit 5 — Governance — *MM-RECYCLED AUGMENTED*

*Sub-themes: Worldview & values · Canadian values & identity · Political party platforms · Government formation · Indigenous & non-indigenous governance · Working within/outside government · Advocacy · Resistance & protest · Unequal access to democracy*

> **MM Spiral:** Of the five Unit 5 options below, **two were existing (A and B)** — strong non-Indigenous concepts that work as-is. **Three are NEW (C, D, E)**, recycled from MM Studies 11 Unit 12 (Protest) and Unit 11 (Government / Sipekne'katik) at a Grade 9 conceptual level. The three new options replace the older "Protest & Response" placeholder. See the **MM Studies 11 Spiral Note** at the top of this file for the rationale.

### [ ] A — "MOCK NS ELECTION" (full campaign simulation)
- **Hook:** A provincial election has been called. Your class is now the candidates, the parties, the press corps, and the voters. You have 3 weeks. May the best platform win.
- **Activity:** Class forms **4–5 political parties** (or use real NS parties — Liberal, PC, NDP, Green, plus an independent or new party). Each party picks a leader, a platform (3–5 key policies researched from real party platforms), a brand (name, slogan, logo, colour), and runs a 2-week campaign: policy launch, leader's debate (formal), attack ads (must be factual), media interviews (teacher plays hostile press), and a final all-class vote. Press corps (rotating role) writes articles and editorial endorsements. Every student must: vote, justify their vote in writing, and participate in a post-election reflection on what they learned about democracy when they ran one.
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) How did you decide which platform to adopt? Whose interests did your party centre? (2) What did the campaign reveal about persuasion vs. substance? (3) Did the actual vote match what the platforms predicted — and if not, why?
- **Documentation:** Party Platforms (Slides/Doc), Campaign Materials (poster, video ad, debate script), Press Articles (Docs), Vote Justification + Reflection.
- **Build:** Heavy (multi-week) · **Resources:** `political-spectrum/`, `election-polparties-marking/` (verify contents — looks like it has party-marking material ready to use).
- **Notes:** This is the heaviest "simulation" in Cit9 and the most directly mapped to multiple indicators (party platforms, government formation, advocacy, unequal access). Strongest single activity in the course for engagement. Time it close to a real NS election if possible for relevance. Make sure the post-mortem includes critical reflection on what the simulation couldn't capture (real money, real media, real voter suppression).

### [ ] B — "ADVOCACY IN ACTION" (advocacy campaign simulation)
- **Hook:** You care about something — pick anything. Now convince a politician to act on it. You have 4 weeks, $0, and the rules of a real advocacy campaign.
- **Activity:** Each student (or pair) picks an issue they actually care about (school policy, local issue, provincial issue, national issue). They research it, identify the decision-maker who has the power to act on it (school principal, MLA, MP, municipal councillor — teacher approves), and design a **real advocacy campaign**: research brief, petition (real, can be shared), letter to the decision-maker, social media plan, in-person meeting or call. They run the campaign and document what actually happened — did they get a response? A meeting? A commitment? Reflection on what worked, what didn't, and why some voices get heard and others don't.
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) Whose voices get heard fastest by decision-makers — and why? (2) What was the hardest part of the campaign? (3) What does "equal access to democracy" actually look like in practice, and who lacks it?
- **Documentation:** Research Brief, Letter to Decision-Maker, Petition Results, Reflection Log (week-by-week), Final Campaign Post-Mortem.
- **Build:** Medium–Heavy · **Resources:** `political-spectrum/` (verify contents), teacher connections to local politicians (school board trustee, councillor, MLA office — often happy to have a brief student meeting).
- **Notes:** This is where Unit 1 (Service Learning) and Unit 5 (Governance) can fuse — if the service-learning project has a policy dimension, students can use it as their advocacy campaign. Strongest fit for the "working within/outside government" indicator. Set up front: not every campaign will "win," and that's a feature — the goal is to learn the process, not to guarantee outcomes.

### [ ] C — "SIPEKNE'KATIK 2020: SELF-DETERMINATION IN ACTION" (case study)

> *Source: Real 2020 dispute — Mi'kmaw fishers in Sipekne'katik First Nation (NS) exercised treaty right to a self-regulated lobster fishery outside the DFO-regulated commercial season; non-Indigenous commercial fishers and RCMP responded with conflict. Connects to MM Studies 11 Unit 11 (Government / Self-Determination) and Unit 12 (Protest).*

- **Hook:** It's October 2020. You're a journalist assigned to cover a tense lobster dispute in Sipekne'katik First Nation, Nova Scotia. Mi'kmaw fishers are exercising a treaty right to harvest lobster outside the federally regulated season. Non-Indigenous commercial fishers are angry. The RCMP has been called. The Department of Fisheries and Oceans says it's their jurisdiction. The Assembly of Nova Scotia Mi'kmaq says it's Mi'kmaw jurisdiction. Your editor wants a 600-word news article by Friday. Go.
- **Activity:** Teacher prepares a **media packet** (see Notes on URLs): 5–8 curated news articles and transcripts representing four perspectives — Mi'kmaw fishers, DFO, non-Indigenous commercial fishers, NS provincial government. Students **read all sources**, then write a 1-page news article that fairly represents the dispute. They also complete a **1-page perspective analysis** that answers: from whose sovereignty does this dispute arise? Whose jurisdiction applies? What does the 1999 Marshall Decision say, and how does it complicate (not resolve) the situation?
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) Whose sovereignty is in tension here, and what does each side consider the legitimate authority? (2) What does the Marshall Decision actually say, and what did the Supreme Court *leave unclear* (e.g., what counts as a "moderate livelihood")? (3) Whose voices were easiest to find in the news — and whose were hardest?
- **Documentation:** 1-page News Article (with byline + date), 1-page Perspective Analysis (4 perspectives compared).
- **Build:** Medium · **Resources:** ⚠️ Real news sources required — CBC, APTN, SaltWire, Global News 2020 archives. **To-do for Antigravity (downstream task):** find and capture URLs for 5–8 representative sources spanning the four perspectives + 1 explainer on the 1999 Marshall Decision. Local sources to prioritize: CBC Nova Scotia, APTN National News, SaltWire Network (Halifax Chronicle-Herald), Global News Atlantic. Cross-reference `mm-summary.md` Unit 11 "Self-Government & Sovereignty" and `mm-june-guide.md` Part 06 "Marshall Decision & Moderate Livelihood Fishing Disputes."
- **Notes:** ⚠️ **This is a real and recent event.** Some students (or their families) will have heard about it; some may live in affected communities. Handle conflict sensitively. Frame as a jurisdictional and treaty-rights dispute, not as "Mi'kmaw fishers vs. non-Indigenous fishers" — the real complexity is between Indigenous self-determination, federal regulation, and provincial political pressure. Avoid asking students to "take a side."

### [ ] D — "IDLE NO MORE" (movement analysis)

> *Source: Real 2012–2013 movement that began with the Attawapiskat First Nation housing crisis and the proposed federal Bill C-45 (Omnibus Bill), then spread nationally and globally. Connects to MM Studies 11 Unit 12 (Protest).*

- **Hook:** In late 2012, a movement called Idle No More erupted across Canada — flash-mob round dances in shopping malls, blockades of highways and rail lines, the Chief of the Assembly of First Nations calling a national day of action. It was the largest Indigenous mobilization in Canadian history. What triggered it? What were they demanding? What worked?
- **Activity:** Teacher curates a **media packet**: ~3 short articles or video clips explaining Idle No More's origins (Attawapiskat housing crisis, Bill C-45's environmental-impact changes), tactics (round dances, social media, direct action), demands (Indigenous sovereignty, environmental protection, Treaty rights, government accountability), and outcomes (some legislative changes, ongoing movements). Students build a **movement map**: what triggered it, what were the demands, what tactics were used, what changed? Then a 1-page reflection on protest as citizenship — when does protest become political action, and when is it effective?
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) Which tactics seemed most/least effective, and why? (2) How does a movement become a national political force? (3) Why did round dances and hashtags both matter?
- **Documentation:** Movement Map (visual: trigger → demands → tactics → outcomes), 1-page Reflection on protest as citizenship.
- **Build:** Light · **Resources:** Idle No More background materials (Idle No More website archive; APTN coverage; CBC archives); `mm-summary.md` Unit 12 (Protest); `mm-june-guide.md` Part 06 cross-references; **protest/** folder from MM Studies 11 Unit 12 (verify contents — folder not present in this sandbox). Optional documentary: *Rumble: The Indians Who Rocked the World* for context on Indigenous cultural reclamation that preceded/dovetailed with the movement.
- **Notes:** Strong bridge to **CURRENT** Indigenous land defense movements (Wetʼsuwetʼen, 1492 Land Back Lane, ongoing treaty-rights defense). Can pair with Unit 5C (Sipekne'katik 2020) chronologically: 2012 Idle No More sets the movement context, 2020 Sipekne'katik is one of its inheritors. Frame as ongoing, not historical.

### [ ] E — "IPPERWASH 1995: WHEN PROTEST TURNS DEADLY" (case study)

> *Source: MM Studies 11 Unit 12 "One Dead Indian" — real 1995 events at Ipperwash Provincial Park (Ontario), where Dudley George, an Anishinaabe protester, was shot and killed by Ontario Provincial Police on September 6, 1995, during a land dispute over a former WWII military base and alleged burial ground.*

- **Hook:** On the night of September 6, 1995, Dudley George — an Anishinaabe protester at Ipperwash Provincial Park, Ontario — was shot and killed by an Ontario Provincial Police sniper. He and others were occupying the park to press a land claim. The Ipperwash Inquiry (2007) would later find the Ontario government partly responsible. How does a protest end with a death?
- **Activity:** Working from a teacher-prepared case timeline (built from MM 11 source materials — `mm-summary.md` Unit 12, `One Dead Indian` documentary/PDF materials), students map **the escalation**: the original land claim (Stoney Point/Soldiers' Memorial land, taken for a WWII military base in WWII, never returned), the 1993 occupation by Indigenous people, the decision to protest at adjacent Ipperwash Provincial Park (which itself contained what some claimed were burial grounds), the political pressure (Ontario Premier Mike Harris's "I want the park back" order), the police raid, and the shooting. Students identify the **points where escalation could have been de-escalated**, then write a critical response: what should police protocols look like when Indigenous people are exercising treaty-rights-based land defense?
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) When does protest become conflict — and who decides? (2) How should police respond to Indigenous land defenders? (3) What did the Ipperwash Inquiry recommend, and how much of it has been implemented?
- **Documentation:** Case Timeline (with annotated escalation points), Critical Response (1–2 pages: police protocols, Indigenous land defense, role of political leadership).
- **Build:** Medium · **Resources:** MM Studies 11 Unit 12 `protest/` folder; *One Dead Indian* (2006 documentary by Adam Garnet Jones, also a book); 2007 Ipperwash Inquiry report (publicly available — Indigenous Affairs Canada archives); **protest/** folder from MM Studies 11 Unit 12 (verify contents — folder not present in this sandbox). WARNING: documentary and primary materials include graphic discussion of police violence and a real death.
- **Notes:** ⚠️ **Real death of a real person.** Dudley George's family has spoken publicly about the case for decades. Handle with care — avoid glamorizing or dramatizing the killing itself. Focus on the systemic decisions (political pressure on police, inadequate protocols for Indigenous land defense, failure of prior peaceful negotiations) rather than on the act of shooting. Consider a content/trigger warning at the start and an opt-out alternative (students can analyse the Ipperwash Inquiry recommendations specifically rather than walking through the shooting in detail). Pair with **Dudley George's brother**, Sam George's, continued activism for context on family/community aftermath.

## Unit 6 — Global Citizenship

*Sub-themes: Global issue from multiple perspectives · Intended/unintended consequences · Universal human rights & globalization · Canada's response to a global issue*

### [ ] A — "GLOBAL ISSUE COURT" (prosecution/defence simulation)
- **Hook:** Country X took Action Y on global issue Z. The Hague has convened a Citizens' Court to decide: was this action defensible, or should there be consequences? You are the prosecution, the defence, or the justices.
- **Activity:** Pick a real global issue (climate response, refugee policy, AI governance, trade sanctions, pandemic response, arms sales). Each country in the simulation represents a real country that took a real action — and the simulation argues whether that action was defensible under international law and human rights norms. Half the class is the **Citizens' Prosecution** (arguing the action violated global norms), half is the **Citizens' Defence** (arguing the action was justified). Smaller group plays **Justices** who question both sides and render a verdict — with the caveat that no verdict is purely "right." Cases drawn from: `cuba62/` (Cuban Missile Crisis as a historical case — perspective-taking exercise), plus current cases.
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) Which side made you shift your position? (2) Where did international law actually speak clearly, and where was it ambiguous? (3) If your verdict contradicted the real-world outcome, what does that tell you?
- **Documentation:** Prosecution Brief, Defence Brief, Justice Questions, Verdict + Rationale, Reflection: "What did this exercise teach me about judging from a distance?"
- **Build:** Medium–Heavy · **Resources:** `cuba62/` (verify contents — Cuban Missile Crisis case study), `Country-Profile-Project/` (verify contents), current news archives, Universal Declaration of Human Rights text.
- **Notes:** Strongest fit for "global issue from differing perspectives" AND "intended/unintended consequences" AND "Canada's response to a global issue" (Canada can be one of the countries in the simulation). Be careful not to flatten real conflicts into classroom debates — these are real people's lives. Frame as "trying to understand," not "scoring points."

### [ ] B — "COUNTRY DEEP-DIVE" (research + presentation)
- **Hook:** You can't be a citizen of the world if you don't know the world. Pick a country. Live inside it for a month — its news, its economy, its government, its challenges, its joys.
- **Activity:** Each student picks (or is assigned) a country — strong fit if the class builds a list that ensures global diversity (don't let everyone pick Western Europe). Over 3–4 weeks, they research: **history, government type, economy, human rights record, current pressing issue, Canada's relationship with this country.** They build a **Country Profile** (Slides or Doc) and present it to the class. Each presentation ends with: **"If I were a citizen of [Country], what's one thing I'd want Canadians to know or do?"** Pairs well with `countryoftheday/` (verify contents) and the `Country-Profile-Project/` (verify contents) — possibly there's a template to use.
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) What surprised you most about the country? (2) How did your assumptions going in differ from reality? (3) Which country's profile changed how you think about Canada?
- **Documentation:** Country Profile (Slides or Doc), "Daily Life" simulation (write a fictional diary entry from a citizen of that country), "Canadian Connection" analysis, Presentation to class.
- **Build:** Medium · **Resources:** `Country-Profile-Project/`, `countryoftheday/`, `guess-the-country game/` (verify contents), CIA World Factbook, BBC Country Profiles, Amnesty International reports.
- **Notes:** This is the safest, most scaffolded option — high completion rate, low emotional risk. Pair with a "guess-the-country" game as a warm-up to build engagement. Good fit for the "global issue from differing perspectives" indicator. Lower-stakes than Option A (Global Issue Court) — pair the two if time allows.

### [ ] C — "ONE GLOBAL ISSUE, FIVE LENSES" (case-study deep dive)
- **Hook:** Pick one global issue — climate, migration, child labour, ocean plastics, AI, pandemics. Now look at it from five angles: a person living it, an NGO responding to it, a government deciding on it, a corporation profiting or losing from it, and a young person your age in that country. Then tell me what "global citizenship" even means.
- **Activity:** Class picks (or votes on) ONE global issue. Working in five-person teams, each member adopts one of the five lenses above. They research the issue from that lens for one week, then come together to build a **multi-perspective case file** (one Doc/Slides per lens + a synthesis Doc). Final class discussion: across the five lenses, where is there agreement? Where is there tension? What does "global citizenship" require of someone in Canada who has no direct stake in the issue? Where does Canada's response succeed or fail? This format works very well with **migration/refugees**, **climate**, or **child labour** as the issue.
- **Discussion/debrief:** (1) Which lens did you find hardest to argue for, and why? (2) Whose perspective is most often missing from Canadian coverage of this issue? (3) What would "responsible action" from Canada actually look like?
- **Documentation:** Five-Lens Case File (per lens: 1 Doc; +1 synthesis Doc), Class Discussion Notes, Personal Position Statement ("What am I going to do about this?").
- **Build:** Medium · **Resources:** Teacher-curated sources per lens, NGO reports (Amnesty, Human Rights Watch, UNICEF), government policy documents, corporate statements, youth voices from the affected region.
- **Notes:** Excellent fit for the "intended/unintended consequences" indicator — each lens exposes different consequences. Also fits the "Canada's response" indicator when one of the lenses is "Canadian government." Lower drama than the Global Issue Court, higher depth than Country Deep-Dive.
