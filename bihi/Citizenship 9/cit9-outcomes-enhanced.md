# Citizenship 9 Curriculum Guide

## About This Guide

**Grade:** 9 (ages 14–15) | **Province:** Nova Scotia | **Prepared for:** September 2026 teaching appointment

### What Grade 9 Citizenship Covers

Citizenship 9 is the compulsory civics and citizenship course that caps off Nova Scotia's Social Studies program. It asks learners to move beyond knowing *about* citizenship to actively *practising* it. Where Grade 7–8 Social Studies introduces history, geography, and civics concepts, Cit 9 challenges students to evaluate, construct, and act — running a service learning project, debating political values, weighing taxation policy, and examining their role as a local, national, and global citizen.

The course is built around **six units** and **~12 foundational outcomes**. The Province groups outcomes using curriculum concept codes — Citizenship (CZ), Critical Thinking (CT), Personal Career Development (PCD), Communication (COM), Creative/Innovative (CI), and Technological Fluency (TF). Each outcome in this guide lists its codes inline so you can see the cross-curricular concept emphasis at a glance.

Key shifts across the six units:
- **From knowing to doing:** Every unit expects an action component — students run a project, write a brief, lead a discussion, or design a campaign.
- **From Canadian-by-default to plural:** Multiple worldviews (especially Mi'kmaw, Acadian, African Nova Scotian, newcomer) are centred rather than treated as add-ons.
- **From individual to structural:** Economic decisions, media influence, and political action are unpacked as systems with winners and losers, not just personal choices.
- **From local to global:** The course opens on a service learning project in the school community and closes on Canada's role in global issues — a deliberate arc from "right here" to "out there."

### How Cit9 Builds on Grade 8 Social Studies

Grade 8 Social Studies in NS is anchored in Canadian history, identity, and worldviews (often through a regional/Atlantic-Canadian lens), with growing emphasis on inquiry, primary sources, and student-led questions. Cit 9 picks up those threads and pushes them forward: where Grade 8 students *investigate* how Canada was formed, Cit 9 students *evaluate* whose interests are served by current Canadian institutions. Where Grade 8 introduces rights and responsibilities through the lens of identity, Cit 9 requires students to apply those concepts to real economic decisions, real media, and real governance. The Grade 8 focus on worldviews and reconciliation becomes the Cit 9 Unit 2 deep-dive on treaty rights and reconciliation. The Grade 8 introduction to inquiry-based research becomes the Unit 1 service learning project that runs throughout the term. In short: Grade 8 builds the conceptual toolkit, Cit 9 asks students to use it on real civic problems.

### **Unit 1 — Engaged Citizenship**

#### Main Outcome (verbatim from curriculum)
> Learners will construct a collaboratively-designed service learning project which addresses a need in the school or larger community.

#### Curriculum Indicators (verbatim)
- **Evaluate** the merit of a collaboratively-selected need *(CZ/CT)*
- **Evaluate** personal skills, strengths and interests in relation to the service learning project *(PCD/CT)*
- **Plan** a service learning project to achieve the goal *(CZ/COM/CI/CT/TF)*
- **Implement** a service learning plan *(COM/PCD/CI/TF)*
- **Evaluate** the development of citizenship skills in relation to the service learning project *(CZ/COM/CT)*

#### Main Themes (Concepts)
- Identifying authentic community needs (not "charity" but "service")
- Collaborative decision-making and democratic process
- Project planning, role allocation, logistics
- Reflection on personal growth and citizenship skill development

#### Suggested Activities
1. **"What's a Need vs. a Want?" Pitch Slam** — Students brainstorm 8–10 possible needs in the school/local community, then pitch them in 60-second elevator pitches. Class uses a structured rubric (evidence of need, scope, fit with grade 9 capacity, equity implications) to narrow to 2–3 finalists. Whole-class vote selects the project. Builds the *Evaluate the merit* indicator.
2. **Skills & Strengths Inventory** — Students complete a personal inventory: skills, interests, languages, networks, accessibility needs, schedules. They map themselves to project roles (researcher, designer, communicator, fundraiser, evaluator). Builds the *Evaluate personal skills* indicator and previews Unit 3 PCD work.
3. **Service Learning Project Plan** — Teams draft a one-page project plan: goal, activities, timeline, roles, resources, risks, success indicators. Plans are critiqued by peers using a planning rubric. Build the *Plan* indicator and rehearse Plan–Do–Reflect cycle. Use `ServiceLearningProposal/` and `ServiceLearningIntro.html` templates.
4. **Implementation Sprint (multi-week)** — Teams execute the plan over 3–4 weeks with weekly stand-up check-ins (what we did, what's next, blockers). Teacher acts as consultant, not director. Logs of hours and activities maintained by students. Builds the *Implement* indicator.
5. **Reflection Showcase** — Each team delivers a 5-minute presentation covering: what they did, what worked, what didn't, how their understanding of citizenship shifted, and how they'll evaluate impact. Public audience invited (other classes, admin, families). Builds the *Evaluate citizenship skills* indicator.
6. **"Citizen Selfie" Exit Reflection** — Individually, students write a one-page reflection: "Three citizenship skills I exercised during this project, one I want to develop further, and one thing I'll do differently next time." Assesses personal growth against the outcome.
7. **Cross-Project Learning Circle** — In the final week, students from different teams visit each other's projects (where possible) or watch short documentary-style video summaries. Goal: see patterns across projects, identify transferable lessons about civic action.

### **Unit 2 — Who Am I as a Citizen?**

> **Recycling note (Sept 2026):** Unit 2B is being taught almost entirely from **recycled Mi'kmaw Studies 11 content** (June study guide — Annie Francis Sixties Scoop simulation, Donald Marshall Jr. case study, Sentencing Circle vs. Western Court, Sugar Falls / Hard Road to Walk graphic novels). Content is simplified for 14–15-year-olds and trimmed to 2B's curriculum indicators. See the **MM Studies 11 Spiral Reference** section near the bottom of this guide for how Cit9 grades 9 and MM grades 11 connect.

#### Main Outcome 2A (verbatim)
> Learners will evaluate evolving concepts and attributes of citizenship as members of different kinds of communities.

#### Curriculum Indicators — 2A
- **Compare** rights and responsibilities as current and future citizens *(CZ/CT)*
- **Investigate** how worldview impacts understanding of citizenship *(CZ/CT)*
- **Evaluate** personal strengths and skills and how these contribute to communities *(CZ/PCD)*
- **Predict** how a citizen's role may change over time *(CZ/PCD/CT)*

#### Main Outcome 2B (verbatim)
> Learners will evaluate key changes in the citizenship rights of Mi'kmaw and other traditionally disempowered people.

#### Curriculum Indicators — 2B
- **Investigate** how and why citizenship rights have changed for various people in Canada *(CZ/PCD/CT)*
- **Evaluate** the importance of treaties as integral to citizenship rights and responsibilities *(CZ/PCD/CT)*
- **Analyse** the role of all citizens in reconciliation *(COM/CT)*

#### Main Themes (Concepts)
- Rights and responsibilities across life stages and contexts (2A)
- Worldview (cultural, religious, regional, generational) as a lens on citizenship (2A)
- Citizenship as layered (local, national, global; legal, lived) (2A)
- **Mi'kmaw citizenship under the Indian Act: forced assimilation, residential schools, the Sixties Scoop, intergenerational trauma** (2B — recycled from MM)
- **The 1940s Centralization Policy — relocating Nova Scotia Mi'kmaq to Eskasoni and Sipekne'katik** (2B — recycled from MM)
- **IRSSA (Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement), the TRC, and the 94 Calls to Action** (2B — recycled from MM)
- **Wrongful conviction & the justice system: Donald Marshall Jr., Gladue Rights, Sentencing Circles vs. Western Court** (2B — recycled from MM)
- **Peace and Friendship Treaties and the treaty relationship in Mi'kma'ki** (2B)
- **Reconciliation as an active, ongoing civic responsibility for all settlers and newcomers** (2B — recycled from MM)

#### Suggested Activities

**Unit 2A — Evolving concepts of citizenship (kept from prior version)**

1. **"Rights & Responsibilities at 14, 25, 50, 75" Timeline Activity** — Students plot what rights/responsibilities they have now, anticipate changes at 25, then reflect on those at 50 and 75. Compares youth civic rights with adult and elder responsibilities. Builds the *Compare* indicator.
2. **Worldview Windows Case Studies** — Three to four short case studies (a Mi'kmaw elder reflecting on citizenship; a newcomer teen; a 4th-generation African Nova Scotian; a 2nd-generation Chinese-Canadian). Structured comparison: how does each describe citizenship? What does the Canadian state mean to them? What tensions or contradictions? Builds the *Investigate worldview* indicator.
3. **"Citizenship Skills I Bring" Personal Inventory + Mapping** — Students list their skills and map them onto a "community contribution wheel" (school, neighbourhood, family, faith community, hobby community, online community). Builds the *Evaluate personal strengths* indicator.
4. **Citizenship Role Timeline Prediction** — In pairs, students write a 1-page prediction: "How will their role as a citizen change at ages 18, 30, 65?" Builds the *Predict* indicator and bridges to Unit 5.

**Unit 2B — Mi'kmaw citizenship & reconciliation (MM-RECYCLED — adapted for Grade 9)**

5. **"Citizenship Through Time" Canadian Rights Storyboard (light version)** — Two short historical research tasks:
   - Group A: Status of women (Persons Case 1929, employment equity)
   - Group B: Status of African Nova Scotians (Viola Desmond, Africville, immigration reforms)
   - *(Note: Indigenous citizenship rights get their own dedicated activities below — activities 6–12 — rather than being squeezed into Group B of this storyboard.)*
   Builds the *Investigate how and why citizenship rights have changed* indicator.
6. **Annie Francis Sixties Scoop Simulation** — Students work through the fictional 1963 child welfare case from the MM June guide. They examine the "Annie Francis → Anne Peters" name change in welfare files, the hidden "box in the closet" wrapped in a scarf (photo + sweetgrass sweater), and the two-branch family tree (Branch A: physical erasure via apprehension / Branch B: legal erasure via Indian Act second-generation cut-off → Sarah Johnson). They answer the driving question: *What is being erased, and how?* Grade 9 students get the simulation scaffold and the MM glossary terms — they do not get the full practice question rubric (that lives in MM). Builds the *Investigate how and why citizenship rights have changed* indicator. **Resource path:** MM Studies `leo/` folder (the Annie Francis Sixties Scoop case materials).
7. **Sugar Falls — Residential School Survivor Story (graphic-novel excerpt)** — Read the *Sugar Falls* by David A. Robertson excerpt (Betty Ross's story). Focus on the smudging ceremony frame, forced separation, cultural erasure, and the line "killing the Indian in the child." Grade 9 students describe what they see, name the policy mechanisms, and respond to: *If you were Betty's grandchild today, what questions would you ask, and what would you want to know?* Builds the *Evaluate the importance of treaties-as-relationship* indicator by anchoring it in residential school context. **Resource path:** MM Studies `SugarFalls/` folder.
8. **The Outside Circle (Hard Road to Walk) — Intergenerational Trauma** — Excerpt from Patti LaBoucane-Benson graphic novel about Joey and Pete, two Indigenous brothers caught in cycles of gang involvement and incarceration. Focus on the **Director of Child Welfare** / **Child Welfare Act** references and the concept of **Historical Trauma**. Discuss: *How does what happened to a grandparent still shape a grandchild?* Build toward understanding the link between colonial policy and contemporary outcomes. **Resource path:** MM Studies `Hard Road to Walk/` (a.k.a. `OutsideCircle/`).
9. **The Indian Act, Section 88, and the Sixties Scoop — How the Law Enabled the Scoop** — Short lecture + primary-source reading (one or two pages of the actual Indian Act text, simplified). Show the *numbers* from the MM guide: 1955 → <1% / 1964 → 34% / 2012 → 68% in care. Discuss the modern "Millennial Scoop" briefly. Grade 9 takeaway: the law is a tool, and Section 88 let provincial child welfare law apply on reserves. Builds the *Evaluate the importance of treaties and the law* indicator. **Resource path:** MM Studies `Residentialschools-1/` and the glossary entries in MM `leo/`.
10. **The TRC's 94 Calls to Action — A Primer** — Introduce the TRC's six pillars and pick **3–5 essential Calls** most relevant to 14–15-year-olds (e.g., #1 child welfare, #10 UNDRIP, #14 language, #23 health-care equity, #62–65 education for reconciliation). Grade 9 students do not get the Beyond 94 tracking analysis (that's MM depth). They pick one Call and write a one-paragraph "what would this look like in my school?" response. Builds the *Analyse the role of all citizens in reconciliation* indicator. **Resource path:** MM Studies `TRC/` and `Residentialschools-1/`.
11. **Donald Marshall Jr. — A Mi'kmaw Teen Wrongfully Convicted** — Case study of Donald Marshall Jr., the Sydney, NS teenager convicted of murder in 1971 and exonerated after 11 years. Use the MM-packaged version of the case (Mi'kmaw teenager, Mi'kmaw community, NS location). Focus question: *What does it mean when a 14- to 15-year-old — your age — gets caught up in a system that was not built for them?* Brief tie to the 1989 Marshall Inquiry and the founding of the Mi'kmaw Legal Support Network (MLSN). Connects 2B and bridges to Unit 5 governance. **Resource path:** MM Studies `donaldmarshall/` folder.
12. **Sentencing Circle vs. Western Court — Two Paths of Justice** — Use the comparison from the MM June guide (Two Paths: Liam's Journey graphic-novel excerpt). Compare goals (punishment vs. healing), structure (elevated judge bench vs. chairs in a circle at equal height), participants (lawyers vs. Elder + judge + victim + community + sweetgrass braid + eagle feather), and outcomes (criminal record vs. active repair plan). Two-column comparison handout. Optional Grade 9 reflection: *If a friend of yours was accused of a non-violent crime and your class could design how the case was handled, what would it look like?* Builds the *Analyse the role of all citizens in reconciliation* indicator by showing what justice *could* look like. **Resource path:** MM Studies `Justice/` folder.
13. **Peace and Friendship Treaties — Map and Meaning (kept)** — Using the 1760–1763 Peace and Friendship Treaties (NS context) as the case, students investigate: what was agreed? By whom? What's the relationship between Mi'kmaw and Crown today? Why are treaties "living" rather than historical? Builds the *Evaluate the importance of treaties* indicator.
14. **"Reconciliation in Action — What Can I Do?"** (kept) — After establishing what reconciliation *is* (TRC primer + Mi'kmaw-led definitions), students generate a list of concrete, age-appropriate actions: language learning, supporting Indigenous businesses, reading Indigenous authors, advocating for land acknowledgements with substance behind them. Builds the *Analyse the role of all citizens in reconciliation* indicator.

- `cit9sites/` — general curated web links; check for citizenship-identity and Indigenous-rights entries

#### Resources (MM Studies folder paths — primary source for Unit 2B)

#### Vocabulary (Unit 2B — aligned with MM June Guide glossary)

**Core citizenship terms (2A):** rights, responsibilities, worldview, citizenship, civic identity

**Mi'kmaw / Indigenous citizenship (2B, MM-aligned):**
- **Assimilation** — forcing a minority group to give up its culture and adopt the dominant group's
- **Indian Act** — 1876 federal legislation governing status, bands, reserves; contains discriminatory provisions
- **Section 88** — 1951 amendment to the Indian Act that allowed provincial child welfare laws to apply on reserves
- **Sixties Scoop** — mass removal of Indigenous children by child welfare agencies (1960s–1980s) into non-Indigenous families
- **IRSSA** — Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement (2006) — largest class-action settlement in Canadian history
- **TRC** — Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2008–2015) — documented residential school history and issued 94 Calls to Action
- **Intergenerational Trauma** — trauma passed from survivors to descendants through parenting, community disruption, and systemic factors
- **Centralization** — failed 1940s federal policy forcing Mi'kmaq onto central reserves (Eskasoni and Sipekne'katik)
- **Gladue Rights** — constitutional right of Indigenous offenders to have their background factors considered, requiring judges to seek alternatives to jail
- **Sentencing Circle** — community-led restorative sentencing process; offender, victim, Elder, judge, and community sit at equal height to agree on a healing and repair plan

#### Assessment Ideas
- **Formative:** Rights timeline participation; worldview case study graphic organizer; Sixties Scoop simulation worksheet completion; Sugar Falls / Outside Circle exit tickets; Sentencing Circle vs. Western Court two-column handout; treaty map annotation
- **Summative (2A):** Citizenship role prediction (written, assessed on reasoning and use of evidence)
- **Summative (2B):** Indigenous citizenship rights essay (one of: Annie Francis simulation reflection, Sugar Falls response, or Donald Marshall Jr. case analysis — assessed on evidence use and analysis depth); reconciliation actions written reflection ("One thing I'm already doing, one thing I'll start, one thing I'll keep learning about")
- **Cross-cut (2A + 2B):** "How has my understanding of citizenship changed?" reflective paragraph connecting worldview work to Mi'kmaw content

### **Unit 3 — Financial Citizenship**

#### Main Outcome 3A (verbatim)
> Learners will compare the impact of economic decisions from the perspectives of various groups of people.

#### Curriculum Indicators — 3A
- **Investigate** different cultural ideas surrounding needs, wants, money, and value *(PCD/CT)*
- **Investigate** the purpose, value, and types of taxation *(COM/TF)*
- **Investigate** the relationship between government priorities and economic equality with regard to indigenous peoples *(CZ/COM/CT)*
- **Investigate** economic disparity between various groups of people *(CZ/COM/CT)*
- **Compare** the concepts of economic fairness and economic equality *(COM/CT)*

#### Main Outcome 3B (verbatim)
> Learners will analyse how effective decision-making and informed participation in society affect, and are affected by, personal financial management.

#### Curriculum Indicators — 3B
- **Investigate** personal financial management practices, including short- and long-term goal setting *(COM/PCD/TF)*
- **Investigate** the impact of informed financial choices on personal goals *(COM/PCD/CT)*
- **Investigate** the direct and indirect impacts of informed financial choices on others *(CZ/PCD/CT)*
- **Analyse** internal and external factors that affect personal financial capability *(PCD/CT/TF)*

#### Main Themes (Concepts)
- Cultural and economic perspectives on wealth, value, and exchange
- Taxation: purpose, mechanisms, types (income, sales, property, sin, carbon), critiques
- Economic inequality and disparity in Canada (Indigenous, racialized, gender, regional)
- Fairness vs. equality vs. equity (economic)
- Personal finance fundamentals: budgeting, saving, credit, debt, goal setting
- Systemic factors that shape financial capability

#### Suggested Activities
1. **"Money Around the World — Cultural Money Map"** — Students research and present on how a specific culture conceptualizes money, value, or exchange (e.g., Mi'kmaw gift economies and trade networks, gifting traditions in Pacific Islander cultures, Islamic finance, haggling cultures). Builds the *Investigate different cultural ideas* indicator. Use `countryoftheday/` and `Country-Profile-Project/` as comparison hooks.
2. **Taxes — Who Pays What and For What?** — Students complete a mock-payroll activity: given fictional jobs and incomes, calculate federal and NS provincial income tax, GST/HST, property tax (via simplified rent proxy), carbon tax (fuel cost simulation). Class discusses who bears what burden and where the revenue goes (health, education, infrastructure, defense). Builds the *Investigate taxation* indicator.
3. **Budget vs. Reality Simulation** — A week-long simulation: students are assigned a job, income, and life situation (e.g., single parent, dual-income family, student living alone). They must make spending decisions in response to weekly scenarios (car repair, medical bill, birthday, grocery price spike). Reflection: what surprised you? What factors outside your control shaped choices? Connects 3A economic perspective work with 3B personal financial management.
4. **"Is It Fair?" Scenarios Gallery Walk** — 8–10 short scenarios posted around the room: "Two workers do the same job, one earns more — that's fair if…", "Inheriting wealth is…", "Reserves have less access to clean water because…", "A carbon tax hurts rural low-income families but helps…". Students walk, annotate, position themselves on a "fair / not fair" spectrum, justify with evidence. Builds the *Compare economic fairness and equality* indicator.
5. **Short & Long-Term Financial Goal Setting** — Students write three goals: one within a month, one within a year, one within 5–10 years (education, travel, car, business). For each, they outline what it costs, what they can do now, what could derail them, and what financial literacy skill they need. Builds the *Investigate personal financial management* indicator.
6. **"Decision Dominoes" — Impact Mapping** — Pick a real-life financial decision (e.g., buy fast fashion vs. secondhand; take a payday loan; rent vs. buy; invest in fossil fuel companies). Map out: who benefits, who loses, ripple effects on family/community/environment. Students present their domino maps. Builds the *Investigate direct and indirect impacts* indicator.
7. **Indigenous Economic Inequality Case Study** — Brief research on economic outcomes for Mi'kmaw and other Indigenous peoples in Canada (income gaps, on-reserve infrastructure, access to capital, treaty rights to fisheries). Discussion: how do government priorities (spending, law, policy) shape these outcomes? Connects 3A economic disparity and 3A government-priorities indicators, and bridges to Unit 5 governance work. (See Indigenous Citizenship Emphasis section for resources.)

#### Resources (in Cit9 NAS folder)
- `Country-Profile-Project/` — country economic/political profile frameworks; adaptable for Unit 3 cultural-economic comparison work
- `countryoftheday/` — short daily country profiles; use as warm-ups or for comparative economic perspectives
- `cuba62/` — Cuba-focused materials; a strong case study for Unit 3 economic system comparison (single-party command economy vs. capitalist mixed economy)
- `cit9sites/` — general curated web links; check for personal finance and taxation entries

#### Assessment Ideas
- **Formative:** Cultural money map research notes; payroll calculation check-in; budget simulation reflection exit ticket
- **Summative:** "Is It Fair?" written response (assessed on reasoning, evidence, engagement with fairness vs. equality distinction); personal financial goals portfolio with action plan; impact map presentation

### **Unit 4 — Digital Citizenship**

#### Main Outcome 4A (verbatim)
> Learners will evaluate how perceptions of current issues are influenced by various media, and how this shapes actions, choices, and reactions.

#### Curriculum Indicators — 4A
- **Evaluate** how various groups are represented or omitted in media and the influence this has on people's perspectives *(CZ/COM/CT/TF)*
- **Analyse** how social and mass media impact the awareness of certain issues *(CZ/COM/CT/TF)*
- **Investigate** how citizens' and governments' choices can be affected by different media *(CZ/COM/CT/TF)*

#### Main Outcome 4B (verbatim)
> Learners will evaluate risks, rights, and responsibilities of digital citizens.

#### Curriculum Indicators — 4B
- **Evaluate** the opportunities and risks of being digitally engaged or connected *(COM/PCD/CT/TF)*
- **Investigate** how a digital footprint influences others' perceptions *(CZ/PCD/TF)*
- **Investigate** the role of anonymity in both the production and consumption of media *(COM/PCD/CT/TF)*

#### Main Themes (Concepts)
- Media representation and omission (who's visible, who's invisible, why)
- Media agenda-setting and "issue salience"
- The attention economy, algorithmic curation, filter bubbles, echo chambers
- Digital footprint: what we leave, what it says, who reads it
- Anonymity: protection vs. harm, accountability, voice
- Digital rights and responsibilities (legal, ethical, social)

#### Suggested Activities
1. **"Who's in the Story?" Media Representation Audit** — Students track 3 different news sources (CBC, a tabloid, an international outlet) for one week on a single current issue. They tally: who is quoted, who is missing, what framing is used, what images appear. Class compares coverage patterns. Builds the *Evaluate representation/omission* indicator. Use `PerceptionVsReality/` and `DigCit/` folders as foundational materials.
2. **Algorithmic Awareness Exercise** — Students review one of their own social media feeds for 15 minutes. They log: what posts appeared, why (likely), what didn't appear (likely). Discussion: who decides what they see? What does an algorithm optimize for? Connects to *Investigate how citizens' choices are affected by media*.
3. **"Issue Awareness" Heat Map** — Pick 5 issues that have been in the news recently. Students investigate: how long has each been in the news? Why this issue and not that one? What forces shape issue visibility (lobbying, social movements, tragedy, election cycles)? Builds the *Analyse how media impact awareness* indicator.
4. **Digital Footprint Self-Audit** — Students Google themselves, search tagged photos, check old social posts, review app permissions. They write a one-page reflection: "What does my digital footprint say about me? What would a future employer / university / romantic partner / landlord think?" Builds the *Investigate digital footprint* indicator. Use `DigCit/` and `PerceptionVsReality/` resources.
5. **"With or Without Your Name" — Anonymity Debate & Case Study** — Case studies: a whistleblower; a teen cyberbully; a person posting racist comments under a fake name; a domestic abuse survivor protecting location; a journalist's anonymous source. Students evaluate: when is anonymity protective, when is it harmful, who decides? Builds the *Investigate the role of anonymity* indicator.
6. **"Clickbait vs. Substance" Mini-Investigation** — Students bring in three headlines/articles they've seen online. Class deconstructs: what's the headline promising? Does the article deliver? What emotional cues are used? What's the share motivation vs. the information value? Builds *Evaluate how media shapes actions and reactions*.
7. **Government & Media — A Targeted Ad Case Study** — Look at the Cambridge Analytica / micro-targeting / elections interference story (or a more recent Canadian equivalent). Students investigate: how was media used to shape government and citizen choices? What are the policy responses? What responsibilities do platforms bear? Builds the *Investigate citizens' and governments' choices affected by media* indicator. Use `election-polparties-marking/` folder as cross-reference for political messaging analysis.
8. **"Digital Citizen Charter" Class Creation** — As a culminating activity, students co-write a class Digital Citizen Charter (rights: privacy, expression, access, safety; responsibilities: honesty, accuracy, accountability, kindness). Charter is posted in the classroom and reviewed annually. Builds the *Evaluate risks, rights, responsibilities* outcome.

### **Unit 5 — Governance**

> **Recycling note (Sept 2026):** Unit 5B and 5C are being augmented with **recycled Mi'kmaw Studies 11 protest and self-determination content** (Unit 12 — Protest — for Sipekne'katik 2020, Idle No More, Ipperwash 1995; Unit 13 — Justice — for Gladue Rights, Sentencing Circles, restorative justice; Unit 14 — Marshall Decision). Content is simplified for 14–15-year-olds and tied to the 5B governance / 5C engagement indicators. See the **MM Studies 11 Spiral Reference** section near the bottom of this guide for the spiral structure.

#### Main Outcome 5A (verbatim)
> Learners will analyse how issues become valued within and across all areas of government and society.

#### Curriculum Indicators — 5A

#### Suggested Activities
1. **"Where Do My Views Come From?" Worldview Mapping** — Students trace three of their own values to where they came from (family, faith, school, media, community, identity). Then they trace one of those values to a policy position. Discussion: if my worldview shaped my policy, what does that mean about how we should talk to people who hold different policies? Builds the *Analyse the role of worldview* indicator.
2. **"What Are Canadian Values?" Class Survey & Critique** — Students conduct a short survey (class + family) asking what people think Canada's core values are. Compile, then critically examine: whose Canada? Which voices are missing? How have "Canadian values" been used historically to exclude? Builds the *Investigate concepts of Canadian values and identity* indicator.
3. **Political Party Platform Comparison** — Students pick two current federal parties, read their actual platforms on one issue they care about, then compare values and policy proposals. Use `election-polparties-marking/` and `political-spectrum/` resources. Builds the *Analyse the values of political parties* indicator.
4. **"Issue I Care About" Brief** — Each student picks an issue they care about, researches how it's currently valued (or not) in Canadian society and government, and writes a one-page brief: What is the issue? Who values it? Who doesn't? Why? What would shift its status? Builds the *Reflect on reasons an issue could be valued* indicator.
5. **Governance Model Comparison Case Study** — Choose a real-world policy decision and trace how it flows through (or fails to flow through) different Canadian governments. Example: a Mi'kmaw fishery dispute (federal–provincial–Mi'kmaw government); a municipal zoning decision (city council + province); a provincial healthcare decision (provincial + federal funding). Students chart: who's involved, who's accountable, who's missing. Builds the *Investigate how various governments are formed* indicator.
6. **Indigenous vs. Non-Indigenous Governance — Side-by-Side Investigation** — Mi'kmaw governance (elected band council + traditional governance + 1999 Nisga'a precedent + ongoing self-government negotiations) vs. Canadian parliamentary system. Students compare: how are leaders chosen? How are decisions made? How is accountability structured? What happens when the two systems collide (e.g., fishery conflicts, land disputes)? Builds the *Investigate interactions in indigenous and non-indigenous governance* indicator.
7. **The Marshall Decision — Indigenous Treaty Rights and Jurisdictional Conflict** — Brief case study of the 1999 Supreme Court Marshall Decision affirming Mi'kmaw right to a "moderate livelihood" from hunting, fishing, and gathering under the 1760–61 Treaties. Use it to discuss: why did the Supreme Court confirm this right? Why is the term "moderate livelihood" still being argued over? What does it tell us about overlapping jurisdictions (Mi'kmaw self-regulation vs. federal DFO)? Builds the *Investigate how various governments are formed* and *Investigate interactions* indicators. **Resource path:** MM Studies `Marshall-1/` folder.
8. **"Three Lanes of Change" — Engagement Strategy Mapping (kept + Indigenous-protest infusion)** — Students pick a cause. They map three lanes for change: **Lane 1 — Inside government** (lobby an MP, petition, vote, run for student council); **Lane 2 — Outside government** (advocacy orgs, social media, public education); **Lane 3 — Resistance** (protest, strike, civil disobedience). Each lane gets a case study, a debate, and an evaluation of effectiveness. Builds the *Evaluate ways to work within and outside government* and *Investigate resistance and protest* indicators.
9. **Indigenous Protest in Canada: Three Cases** — Three short cases of Indigenous protest, each tied to a different scale and era:
   - **Ipperwash 1995** — Dudley George, an Anishinaabe protester, was shot by Ontario Provincial Police at the Ipperwash Crisis (1995). Read the excerpt ("One Dead Indian" summary) from MM Unit 12. Discuss: *When is protest met with force, and what does that tell us about power?*
   - **Idle No More (2012–2013)** — The Indigenous-led movement founded by four women (Jessica Gordon, Sylvia McAdam, Sheelah McLean, Nina Wilson) following the 2012 federal omnibus Bill C-45. Flash-mob round dances in shopping malls, blockades of highways and rail lines, the Attawapiskat chief Theresa Spence's hunger protest on an island in Ottawa. Discuss: *What moves people to dance in a mall? What moves people to a hunger strike?*
   - **Sipekne'katik 2020 (Nova Scotia)** — In September 2020, Sipekne'katik First Nation launched a "self-regulated" lobster fishery outside the federal DFO season, citing the 1999 Marshall Decision. Non-Indigenous commercial fishers responded with violence — burning a lobster pound, smashing a community truck, intimidation at wharves. RCMP presence became a flashpoint. Discuss: *When Canada's courts confirm a treaty right and citizens respond with arson, what does that show about whose voice counts?*
   Builds the *Investigate resistance and protest* indicator and the *Analyse how unequal access to democracy* indicator by concretely connecting them to Canadian historical events. **Resource paths:** MM Studies `protest/` (general), `Marshall-1/` (Sipekne'katik context).
10. **Restorative Justice & Gladue Rights — Engagement Beyond Traditional Politics** — Pick one Indigenous-led sentencing circle or restorative justice panel (can be drawn from MM Unit 13). Discuss: *If we treat an offence as a community harm that needs repair, who has a seat at the table? Who gets to speak?* Connect to: *Is this kind of engagement more or less "democratic" than a courtroom?* Builds the *Evaluate ways to work within and outside government structures* indicator and the *Investigate resistance and protest* indicator by reframing "resistance" as "Indigenous-led institutional design." **Resource path:** MM Studies `Justice/` folder.
11. **"Whose Voice Counts?" Power Audit** — Students investigate a recent Canadian civic decision (election, policy, protest response). They ask: who showed up to vote? who organized? who was consulted? who was excluded? They reflect: what makes some voices easier to amplify than others (time, money, language, race, geography, age)? Builds the *Analyse how unequal access to democracy* indicator.
12. **Political Spectrum Self-Location & Class Spectrum Map** — Using `political-spectrum/` resources, students self-locate on a class-generated political spectrum (built from real current issues, not abstract labels). Discussion: do my views fit one party? Do I have views that span parties? What's my "fit" — and what does that mean for civic engagement?

**Indigenous governance & engagement (MM-augmented):**

### **Unit 6 — Global Citizenship**

#### Main Outcome 6A (verbatim)
> Learners will evaluate the consequences of action and inaction as twenty-first century global citizens.

#### Curriculum Indicators — 6A
- **Investigate** a global issue from differing perspectives *(CZ/COM/CT)*
- **Evaluate** the intended and unintended consequences of an action or inaction in regard to a global issue *(CZ/COM/CT)*
- **Analyse** the relationship between universal human rights and globalization *(CZ/COM/CT)*
- **Evaluate** Canada's response to a selected global issue *(CZ/COM/CT)*

#### Main Themes (Concepts)
- Globalization: trade, movement, technology, culture, environment
- Global issues: climate change, migration, poverty, human rights, conflict, pandemics, technology governance
- Perspective-taking across borders, languages, economies
- Action and inaction as choices (and consequences)
- Universal human rights (UDHR) vs. cultural relativism vs.全球化 tensions
- Canada's role in the world: foreign policy, aid, trade, military, climate commitments

#### Suggested Activities
1. **"Same Issue, Five Lenses" — Global Issue Perspective Activity** — Pick a global issue (e.g., climate change, child labour, migration, AI governance). Five student groups each take a different lens (Global South activist, Global North corporation, youth activist, government policymaker, refugee/affected person). Each group prepares a 3-minute position. Class hears all five, then discusses whose perspective gets the most airtime in Canadian media and why. Builds the *Investigate a global issue from differing perspectives* indicator. Use `Country-Profile-Project/` and `countryoftheday/` for cross-country perspectives.
2. **"Action or Inaction — What Really Happens?" Consequence Mapping** — Pick a global issue (e.g., plastic pollution in oceans). Map both branches:
   - **Action:** A country bans single-use plastics. Intended consequences? Unintended consequences (industry shifts, jobs lost, alternative materials' own impacts)?
   - **Inaction:** No ban. Intended consequences (continuity)? Unintended consequences (microplastics in food chain, fishery collapse, health costs)?
   Students present both, then argue which "cost" is greater. Builds the *Evaluate intended and unintended consequences* indicator.
3. **Universal Human Rights Investigation** — Students pick 3–4 articles from the UDHR. For each, they investigate: how is this right respected in Canada? How is it respected in 3 other countries (varied economies and political systems)? What happens when globalization brings these rights into conflict with local laws or traditions? Builds the *Analyse the relationship between universal human rights and globalization* indicator.
4. **Canada's Response Brief** — Each student picks one global issue and writes a 1–2 page brief on Canada's response: what is Canada doing? Through what mechanisms (foreign policy, trade, aid, military, treaties)? What's working? What's not? What would a critic say? Builds the *Evaluate Canada's response* indicator.
5. **"If I Were a Canadian Diplomat" Simulation** — Students are placed in role as Canadian delegates to a fictional international summit on a global issue they've researched. They must negotiate a resolution with peers representing other countries (assigned). Reflection: what compromises are needed? What does Canada actually have leverage to do? Builds all four Unit 6 indicators through applied practice.
6. **"Globalization — Who Benefits?" Inequality Mapping** — Pick a global commodity (coffee, cobalt, fast fashion, smartphones). Students map the chain: who extracts the raw materials, who manufactures, who sells, who buys, who profits most at each stage, who bears the environmental and human costs. Discussion: is globalization making the world more equal, or concentrating wealth? Builds perspective-taking and connects to Unit 3 economic disparity.
7. **"Action Plan for a Global Citizen" Capstone** — As a culminating task (and natural bookend to the Unit 1 service learning project), each student writes a personal action plan: "Three things I will do as a 21st-century global citizen this year — one in my personal life, one in my community, one in solidarity with a global community." Connects all of Cit 9 — Unit 6 is where students synthesize.

## Indigenous Citizenship Emphasis — A Unifying Thread

Nova Scotia is Mi'kma'ki, the traditional and unceded territory of the Mi'kmaw people. Treaty education and reconciliation are not a "unit" — they are a **cross-cutting requirement** that anchors Units 2 and 5 and is referenced throughout. The Province's commitment to Mi'kmaw and Indigenous education (and to TRC Calls to Action #62–#65 specifically) means this thread should not be optional or tokenistic in any unit.

## MM Studies 11 Spiral Reference

Citizenship 9 introduces Indigenous citizenship at Grade 9 level for the 14–15-year-olds in Citizenship 9. The same content is taught in greater depth in **Mi'kmaw Studies 11** (Grade 11 elective). Most students will take *only* Cit 9; students who take **both** courses will encounter a **spiral learning effect**:

- **Cit 9** introduces concepts, basic cases, foundational understanding, and Grade 9–appropriate vocabulary
- **MM Studies 11** deepens with primary sources, more cases, more sophisticated analysis, and Grade 11–level writing/rubric expectations

This section is written for the **teacher** and for **Antigravity** (the AI assistant helping plan courses). It explains:

1. Why this recycling is happening now (Sept 2026 — the teacher just finished teaching MM Units 10–14 in spring 2026 and has the full content fresh)
2. Which Grade 9 concepts map to which MM Units
3. What the depth difference looks like (what Cit 9 students *don't* get, that MM students do)
4. How the resource folders in the MM Studies directory map to the Cit 9 activities

### Spiral design intent (for the teacher / Antigravity)

- **Cit 9 students** should leave with: the *facts* of residential schools, the Sixties Scoop, the Gladue framework, the Marshall Decision, three concrete protest cases, and the *vocabulary* (Assimilation, Indian Act, Section 88, Sixties Scoop, IRSSA, TRC, Intergenerational Trauma, Centralization, Gladue Rights, Sentencing Circle).

- **Students who later take MM Studies 11** will encounter the same content presented with primary sources, more cases per topic, more sophisticated evaluation rubrics, and more writing. The Cit 9 work is the *spoke*; MM is the *hub*.

### Appendix B — Cross-Unit Themes

Several big ideas recur across the six units. Tracking these helps avoid repetition and surface deeper learning:

| Theme | Appears in | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Perspective / Worldview** | Unit 2 (explicitly), Unit 3 (cultural money), Unit 4 (media representation), Unit 5 (values), Unit 6 (global issues) | Cit 9's deepest cross-cutting concept — students must move from "my view" to "whose view is missing" |
| **Rights & Responsibilities** | Unit 2 (explicitly), Unit 4 (digital rights), Unit 5 (democratic participation), Unit 6 (human rights) | The "what can I do?" question is the throughline of the course |
| **Action & Civic Participation** | Unit 1 (service learning), Unit 5 (engagement), Unit 6 (global action) | Opens and closes the course — the arc is from local action to global action and back |
| **Power & Equity** | Unit 2 (Indigenous rights), Unit 3 (economic disparity), Unit 4 (media representation), Unit 5 (democratic access), Unit 6 (globalization) | Whose voice? Whose interest? Whose cost? — the critical question for every unit |
| **Indigenous Citizenship & Reconciliation** | Units 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 (unifying thread) | NS is Mi'kma'ki — this is foundational, not additive |
| **Critical Evaluation of Information** | Unit 3 (financial decisions), Unit 4 (media), Unit 5 (party platforms) | Source criticism, evidence weighing, distinguishing claim from fact |
| **Personal → Collective → Systemic Thinking** | Every unit | Move from "I" → "we" → "the system" — students must reach the systemic level by year-end |
