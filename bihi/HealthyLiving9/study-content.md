## Unit 1 — Outcome 1: Health Behaviours (Promoting Short- and Long-Term Health)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Health behaviour** — Any action a person takes that affects their health, either positively (eating well, sleeping enough) or negatively (skipping meals, excessive screen time).
- **Sleep hygiene** — Habits and practices that support consistent, good-quality sleep (consistent bedtime, limiting screens before bed, dark/cool room).
- **Sleep deficit** — The cumulative effect of not getting enough sleep over time; leads to impaired focus, mood, memory, and physical health.
- **Circadian rhythm** — The body's internal 24-hour clock that regulates sleep-wake cycles, hunger, and alertness.
- **Technology overuse** — Spending excessive time on screens in ways that replace sleep, physical activity, social interaction, or other health-promoting activities.
- **Disease prevention** — Actions taken to reduce the risk of getting sick (handwashing, vaccination, healthy habits, safe food handling).
- **Health identity** — How a person sees themselves in relation to their own health — their beliefs, values, and sense of responsibility for their well-being.
- **Short-term health** — Health in the present moment or near future (how you feel today or this week).
- **Long-term health** — Health outcomes over months or years (chronic disease risk, organ health, lifespan).
- **Protective factor** — A condition or behaviour that reduces the likelihood of a negative health outcome.

### Core Concepts
1. **Sleep & Youth Health**
   - Teens need 8–10 hours of sleep per night; many get far less
   - Sleep deficit impacts: academic performance, emotional regulation, immune function, reaction time, decision-making, mental health
   - Circadian rhythms shift in adolescence — teens naturally fall asleep later and wake later
   - Sleep hygiene practices: consistent wake time (even weekends), limit caffeine after noon, no screens 30–60 min before bed, cool/dark/quiet room, wind-down routine
   - Short-term cost: tired in class, irritability, poor focus
   - Long-term cost: chronic health conditions, weakened immunity, increased mental health risk

2. **Technology Use & Health**
   - Moderate tech use is normal; overuse becomes a health concern
   - Impacts across 5 dimensions of health:
     - Physical: eye strain, disrupted sleep, reduced activity, posture issues
     - Mental: reduced attention span, information overload
     - Emotional: anxiety from social comparison, FOMO (fear of missing out)
     - Social: replacing face-to-face interaction, cyberbullying risk
     - Spiritual: less time for reflection, hobbies, meaning-building activities
   - Healthy tech habits: set daily limits, tech-free zones (bedroom, meals), curfew for devices, balance with offline activities
   - Short-term benefit: entertainment, connection; long-term risk: addiction patterns, sleep disruption

3. **Disease Prevention**
   - Review: communicable disease transmission (airborne, direct contact, indirect contact, bodily fluids, vectors)
   - Youth-focused prevention: handwashing, respiratory etiquette, vaccination, staying home when sick, not sharing personal items
   - Lifestyle prevention for chronic disease: regular activity, balanced diet, adequate sleep, avoiding substance use
   - Evaluate: which daily choices reduce disease risk long-term?

4. **Identity & Health Decisions**
   - A person's sense of identity shapes their health choices (e.g., "I'm an athlete" → values fitness; "I don't care" → may neglect health)
   - Values, culture, family expectations, peer groups, and media all influence health identity
   - Reflect: How do you see yourself? How does that shape your choices about sleep, technology, and prevention?
   - Growing health identity means taking increasing ownership of personal health decisions in the teen years

### Sample Quiz/Test Items
- **Define:** Sleep hygiene · Health identity · Short-term vs long-term health
- **Analyse:** How does sleep deficit affect each of the 5 dimensions of health?
- **Evaluate:** "Playing video games until 2 AM is fine if you still get 8 hours by sleeping in." — Is this accurate? Why or why not?
- **Identify:** 3 healthy technology habits and 3 signs of technology overuse
- **Apply:** Scenario — "Morgan sleeps 5 hours on weeknights and 12 hours on weekends." Analyse the short- and long-term health impacts of this pattern.
- **Reflect:** How has your personal identity influenced a health decision you've made recently?
- **List:** 3 disease-prevention behaviours and explain how each protects long-term health

## Unit 2 — Outcome 2: Sexual and Reproductive Health

### Key Vocabulary
- **Sexual health** — A state of physical, emotional, mental, and social well-being in relation to sexuality; requires a positive and respectful approach to sexuality and sexual relationships.
- **Reproductive health** — Health of the reproductive systems and processes across all stages of life.
- **STBBI** — Sexually transmitted and blood-borne infection; includes infections passed through sexual contact or blood (e.g., chlamydia, HIV, HPV, hepatitis B).
- **Contraception** — Methods or devices used to prevent pregnancy (condoms, birth control pills, IUDs, implants, patches).
- **Dual protection** — Using two methods together (e.g., condom + hormonal method) to protect against both pregnancy AND STBBIs.
- **Consent** — Voluntary, informed, specific, ongoing, and freely given agreement to engage in a specific activity; can be withdrawn at any time. Silence, pressure, or impairment do not equal consent.
- **Informed decision** — A choice made after understanding the facts, options, risks, and benefits.
- **Reproductive health service** — A healthcare service that supports reproductive and sexual health (e.g., family planning clinics, public health units, doctors' offices).
- **Barrier method** — A contraceptive device that physically blocks sperm (e.g., external condom, internal condom, dental dam).
- **Hormonal method** — A contraceptive method that uses hormones to prevent pregnancy (pill, patch, IUD, implant, injection); does NOT prevent STBBIs.
- **Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)** — A medication taken by people at high risk for HIV to prevent infection.
- **HPV vaccine** — A vaccine that protects against the human papillomavirus, which can cause cervical cancer and genital warts.

### Core Concepts
1. **Finding Accurate & Reliable Information**
   - Trusted sources: healthcare providers, public health units, reputable websites (SexandU.ca, Planned Parenthood, Kids Help Phone), school counsellors, sexual health clinics
   - Unreliable sources: peer gossip, anonymous forums, unverified social media, pornography (does not represent real relationships or consent)
   - Evaluate sources by asking: Who created this? Is it evidence-based? Is it current? Does it come from a health authority?
   - Media literacy matters — ads, social media, and peer culture can spread misinformation about bodies, sex, and relationships

2. **Options, Supports & Treatments**
   - **Contraception options:**
     - Abstinence — choosing not to engage in sexual activity (100% effective for pregnancy and STBBI prevention)
     - Barrier methods — condoms (external and internal); dental dams — prevent STBBIs
     - Hormonal methods — pill, patch, ring, injection, IUD, implant — prevent pregnancy only
     - Emergency contraception — Plan B / morning-after pill; must be taken within a specific timeframe
   - **STBBI testing and treatment:**
     - Many STBBIs have no visible symptoms — testing is important
     - Testing is confidential and available at public health units, doctors, and sexual health clinics
     - Most bacterial STBBIs (chlamydia, gonorrhea) are curable with antibiotics; viral STBBIs (HIV, herpes, HPV) are manageable with treatment
   - **Vaccination:** HPV vaccine (offered in NS schools), hepatitis B vaccine
   - **Where to access care in Nova Scotia:** public health clinics, family doctors, IWK Health Centre, university health services, Planned Parenthood Atlantic

3. **Factors Influencing Sexual Health Decisions**
   - Personal: values, readiness, body comfort, goals, emotional maturity
   - Relational: partner's wishes, communication quality, trust, power balance
   - Social: peer norms, media messages, cultural/religious beliefs, family expectations
   - Knowledge: level of accurate information, understanding of risks and protections
   - Substance use: alcohol and other drugs impair judgement and can affect ability to give or read consent
   - Informed decision-making = considering all these factors before acting

### Sample Quiz/Test Items
- **Define:** Consent · Dual protection · STBBI
- **Identify:** Which contraceptive methods protect against pregnancy only vs pregnancy AND STBBIs
- **Investigate:** Name 3 reliable sources of sexual health information and explain why each is trustworthy
- **Apply:** Scenario — "Taylor wants to find accurate information about STBBIs but isn't sure what websites to trust." What criteria should Taylor use?
- **Compare:** Barrier methods vs hormonal methods — what does each protect against?
- **Determine:** List 4 factors that might influence a teen's decision about sexual activity
- **Reflect:** Why is it important for a person to consider multiple factors (not just peer pressure) when making decisions about sexual health?

## Unit 3 — Outcome 3: Health Literacy & Accessing Health Resources

### Key Vocabulary
- **Health literacy** — The ability to find, understand, and use health information and services to make good decisions about your health.
- **Healthcare system** — The organizations, professionals, services, and facilities that provide medical care (hospitals, clinics, family doctors, public health, pharmacies, mental health services).
- **Self-advocacy** — Speaking up for your own needs, asking questions, and making sure your concerns are heard, especially in healthcare settings.
- **Referral** — A recommendation from one healthcare provider to another for further assessment or treatment (e.g., family doctor refers you to a specialist).
- **Confidentiality** — The legal and ethical duty of healthcare providers to keep your personal health information private.
- **Public health unit** — A government health service that provides programs like immunization, sexual health clinics, and health promotion (e.g., Nova Scotia Health Authority public health).
- **Triaging** — The process of determining how urgent a health concern is and directing care accordingly (e.g., 811 advice line, emergency room assessment).
- **Pharmacist** — A healthcare professional who dispenses medications and can provide health advice without an appointment in many cases.
- **811 (Telecare)** — A free, confidential health advice phone line available in Nova Scotia — registered nurses answer health questions and help you decide where to go for care.

### Core Concepts
1. **Building Health Literacy**
   - Skills: finding credible sources, understanding medical terms, reading medication labels, knowing when to seek help, asking questions
   - Health literacy matters for all 5 dimensions — not just physical
   - Low health literacy leads to: delayed care, misunderstanding instructions, relying on poor information
   - Improving health literacy: ask questions, use reputable sources, practice reading health information, talk to trusted adults
   - Evaluate information: Is it from a recognized health authority? Is it up to date? Is it selling something?

2. **Places to Access Reliable Health Information & Care**
   - **Nova Scotia specific:**
     - Family doctor / nurse practitioner — ongoing care, referrals, prescriptions
     - Public health units — immunizations, sexual health clinics, health screening
     - 811 Telecare — 24/7 nurse advice line (free, confidential)
     - Pharmacists — medication advice, minor ailment assessment, vaccinations
     - IWK Health Centre — pediatric and youth specialty care (Halifax)
     - Emergency departments — urgent/emergency care
     - Walk-in clinics — non-emergency care without an appointment
     - School counsellors — first point of contact for health and mental health concerns
     - Kids Help Phone — 1-800-668-6868 / text CONNECT to 686868 / kidshelpphone.ca
   - **Online:** Nova Scotia Health Authority website, Health Canada, SexandU.ca, Centre for Addiction and Mental Health (CAMH)

3. **Navigating the Healthcare System**
   - When to use what: 811 for advice, walk-in for minor illness, family doctor for ongoing care, emergency for life-threatening situations
   - Prepare for appointments: write down symptoms and questions, bring medication list, bring a trusted adult
   - Self-advocacy skills: "I've been experiencing ___ for ___ days," "Can you explain that in simpler terms?" "What are my options?"
   - Understanding confidentiality: minors (under 16 in NS) can access certain health services (sexual health, mental health) without parent/guardian consent
   - Informed consent: understanding what a treatment involves before agreeing to it

### Sample Quiz/Test Items
- **Define:** Health literacy · Self-advocacy · Confidentiality
- **Identify:** 3 places in Nova Scotia where a teen can access health information or care
- **Investigate:** What is 811 and when should someone call it?
- **Apply:** Scenario — "Ava has had a sore throat for 5 days and isn't sure if she should see a doctor." Walk through her options in the NS healthcare system.
- **Compare:** When would you use a pharmacist vs a family doctor vs 811 vs the emergency department?
- **Evaluate:** "I read on a TikTok that drinking vinegar cures infections." How would you assess this claim using health literacy skills?
- **List:** 3 self-advocacy skills to use during a healthcare appointment

## Unit 4 — Outcome 4: Communication Skills for Healthy Relationships

### Key Vocabulary
- **Assertiveness** — Communicating your needs, opinions, and feelings clearly, directly, and respectfully — without being aggressive or passive.
- **Passive communication** — Not expressing your needs or opinions; going along with others to avoid conflict; leads to resentment.
- **Aggressive communication** — Expressing your needs by dominating, attacking, or disrespecting others; damages relationships.
- **Negotiation** — A discussion where people work together to find a solution that is acceptable to everyone involved.
- **Conflict resolution** — The process of addressing a disagreement in a way that is respectful and leads to a workable outcome.
- **Refusal skills** — Strategies for clearly saying "no" to pressure while maintaining relationships and self-respect.
- **"I" statement** — A communication formula: "I feel ___ when ___ because ___ I would like ___" — owns your feelings without blaming.
- **Active listening** — Fully focusing on what the other person is saying, showing understanding through body language and responses.
- **Non-verbal communication** — Messages sent without words: body language, tone of voice, facial expressions, eye contact.
- **Digital communication** — Exchanging messages through technology (texting, social media, gaming chat, video calls).

### Core Concepts
1. **Expressing Needs, Disagreement & Perspectives**
   - Use "I" statements to express feelings without blaming: "I feel hurt when you make jokes about my appearance because I think you don't respect me. I'd like you to stop."
   - Needs are valid — expressing them is healthy, not selfish
   - Selecting appropriate language: calm vs heated, private vs public, in-person vs text
   - Disagreement is normal — how you disagree matters (respectful vs hostile)
   - Perspectives: recognizing that others may have different experiences and viewpoints

2. **Assertiveness, Negotiation & Refusal Skills**
   - **Assertiveness spectrum:**
     - Passive → Assertive (balanced) → Aggressive
   - **Assertive techniques:**
     - Clear "no" without over-explaining
     - State your position and stick to it
     - Acknowledge the other person's perspective: "I hear you, but I'm not comfortable with that"
   - **Negotiation steps:**
     1. Listen to the other person's position
     2. State your own position clearly
     3. Brainstorm options together
     4. Find a compromise both can accept
     5. Agree on the solution and check in later
   - **Refusal skills (expanded for Grade 9):**
     - Direct refusal: "No, I'm not doing that."
     - Reasoned refusal: "No thanks, I have practice tomorrow."
     - Walk away: physically leaving the situation
     - Delay: "Not right now" or "Maybe later" (buying time to think)
     - Broken record: repeating your "no" calmly without escalating
     - Alternative suggestion: "Instead of that, let's ___"

3. **Conflict Resolution Strategies**
   - **Healthy strategies:** calm down first, listen without interrupting, use "I" statements, seek compromise, agree to disagree, ask a mediator for help
   - **Unhealthy strategies:** yelling, name-calling, giving the silent treatment, physical aggression, avoiding the issue entirely
   - Not all conflict can be resolved — sometimes the healthy choice is to end the relationship or set a firm boundary
   - Power imbalances make fair conflict resolution harder (see Unit 5)

4. **Impacts of Language — Online & Offline**
   - Words can build people up or tear them down — language has real consequences
   - Online language risks: permanence (screenshots), misinterpretation (no tone/body language), wider audience, anonymity → cruelty
   - Offline language: tone, body language, and context all matter — same words can mean different things
   - Hate speech, slurs, and degrading language are harmful — they create hostile environments and can cause lasting emotional damage
   - Reflect: How do you want people to remember the way you speak to them?

### Sample Quiz/Test Items
- **Define:** Assertiveness · Negotiation · "I" statement
- **Compare:** Passive vs assertive vs aggressive communication — give an example of each
- **Apply:** Scenario — "Jordan's friend wants Jordan to send an inappropriate photo. Jordan is uncomfortable but doesn't want to lose the friendship." Write an assertive refusal response using an "I" statement.
- **Analyse:** How does digital communication make conflict resolution harder than face-to-face?
- **Select:** Which conflict resolution strategy would work best in this scenario — "Two friends are arguing about a group project and both want different topics."
- **Reflect:** Describe a time when language (yours or someone else's) affected a relationship. What would you do differently?
- **List:** 4 refusal skills and give a brief example of each

## Unit 5 — Outcome 5: Gender Norms, Stereotypes & Biases in Relationships

### Key Vocabulary
- **Gender norm** — A societal expectation about how people of a particular gender should behave, appear, or think.
- **Gender stereotype** — A generalized belief about the characteristics, roles, or abilities of a specific gender (e.g., "boys are tough," "girls are emotional").
- **Gender bias** — Preferring one gender over another, often unconsciously, in ways that affect decisions, opportunities, or treatment.
- **Gender expression** — How a person expresses their gender through appearance, clothing, behaviour, or voice.
- **Gender identity** — A person's internal sense of their own gender (man, woman, non-binary, genderfluid, etc.).
- **Power dynamics** — The way power (control, influence, decision-making) is distributed between people in a relationship or group.
- **Equality** — The state of being equal in rights, responsibilities, opportunities, and treatment regardless of gender.
- **Equity** — Fairness — recognizing that people may need different support to achieve equal outcomes.
- **Toxic masculinity** — A set of harmful cultural expectations placed on men and boys (e.g., never show emotion, be dominant, use violence to solve problems).
- **Misogyny** — Dislike of, contempt for, or prejudice against women and girls.
- **Patriarchy** — A social system where men hold primary power and predominate in roles of political leadership, moral authority, and social privilege.

### Core Concepts
1. **Conditions that Influence Gender Norms, Practices & Behaviours**
   - **Media:** movies, TV, advertising, social media, gaming — often reinforce narrow gender roles (e.g., men as heroes, women as objects, specific body ideals)
   - **Family & culture:** traditions, expectations, division of labour at home, parental messages ("boys don't cry," "be a lady")
   - **Peers:** social groups enforce norms through teasing, exclusion, and approval
   - **Institutions:** schools, sports, religion, workplaces — policies and practices that may favour one gender
   - **Historical context:** gender roles have changed over time — what was "normal" 50 years ago may not be today
   - These conditions are not fixed — individuals and communities can challenge and change them

2. **Power Dynamics in Relationships**
   - Healthy power dynamics: shared decision-making, equal say, mutual respect, both people's needs matter equally
   - Unhealthy power dynamics: one person controls decisions, money, social life, appearance, or emotional state
   - Power imbalances can develop from: age difference, social status, gender expectations, financial control, emotional manipulation
   - Signs of imbalance: one person always gets their way, fear of disagreeing, feeling "walked on"
   - Gender expectations often create unequal power (e.g., expecting the male partner to be in charge, or expecting the female partner to do all emotional labour)

3. **Promoting Gender Equality**
   - **Individual actions:** challenge stereotypes when you hear them, use inclusive language, support people who break gender norms, examine your own biases
   - **Relational actions:** equal division of responsibilities, respecting all gender expressions, not tolerating sexist jokes/comments
   - **Community actions:** support gender-inclusive policies, call out discrimination, amplify voices that are underrepresented
   - **Media literacy:** question portrayals of gender in media, seek diverse representation, recognize manipulation
   - Equality benefits everyone — rigid gender norms harm people of all genders (pressure on boys to be "tough," limits on girls' opportunities, harm to non-binary and trans people)

### Sample Quiz/Test Items
- **Define:** Gender norm · Power dynamics · Equity vs Equality
- **Identify:** 3 conditions that influence gender norms in teens' lives
- **Investigate:** How does social media reinforce or challenge gender stereotypes? Give an example of each.
- **Analyse:** In a relationship where one partner "always decides what to do," what does this suggest about power dynamics?
- **Apply:** Scenario — "A classmate says, 'That's a girl sport — you shouldn't play it.'" How could you respond in a way that promotes gender equality?
- **Compare:** How do toxic masculinity and misogyny affect relationships differently?
- **Reflect:** Think about a gender expectation you've experienced or observed. How did it make people feel? What would be a more equitable approach?

## Unit 6 — Outcome 6: Gender-Based Violence

### Key Vocabulary
- **Gender-based violence (GBV)** — Violence directed at someone because of their gender; disproportionately affects women, girls, and gender-diverse people.
- **Sexual violence** — Any sexual act committed without consent (including harassment, assault, exploitation).
- **Physical violence** — The use of physical force to cause harm (hitting, pushing, restraining).
- **Emotional/psychological violence** — Non-physical behaviour intended to control, intimidate, or degrade (insults, threats, isolation, gaslighting).
- **Coercion** — Pressuring or manipulating someone into doing something they don't want to do, using threats, guilt, or power.
- **Gaslighting** — A form of psychological manipulation where the abuser makes the victim doubt their own memory, perception, or sanity.
- **Cyber harassment** — Using digital tools to threaten, embarrass, or target someone repeatedly.
- **Non-consensual image sharing** — Distributing intimate images of someone without their consent (sometimes called "revenge porn"); illegal in Canada.
- **Victim-blaming** — Suggesting that the person who experienced harm is at fault (e.g., "What were you wearing?" "Why did you go there?").
- **Survivor** — A term used for someone who has experienced violence or abuse; recognizes their strength and agency.
- **Trauma-informed support** — Approaching someone who has experienced violence with understanding, patience, and care, recognizing the impact of trauma.

### Core Concepts
1. **Contributing Factors to Gender-Based Violence**
   - **Societal conditions:** gender inequality, patriarchal norms, normalization of violence in media, poverty, colonialism and racism
   - **Cultural norms:** victim-blaming attitudes, "boys will be boys" mentality, stigma around reporting, silence around gender violence
   - **Individual behaviours:** lack of respect for boundaries, entitlement, poor emotional regulation, substance abuse, learned violent behaviour
   - **Silo effect:** these factors reinforce each other — individual behaviour is shaped by and reinforces societal norms
   - GBV can happen in any community, relationship, or setting — no one is immune

2. **Countering Harmful Behaviours**
   - **Individual level:** call out sexist language and jokes, don't laugh at degrading comments, model respectful behaviour
   - **Peer level:** challenge peers who use coercive language, support friends who set boundaries, create a culture of respect in friend groups
   - **Online:** don't share non-consensual images, report harassment, challenge harmful content
   - **Community level:** support organizations working to end GBV, educate others, participate in awareness campaigns
   - Recognizing early warning signs in relationships (controlling behaviour, jealousy framed as "love," isolation from friends)

3. **Supporting Self & Others**
   - If someone discloses GBV to you:
     - Listen without judgment — believe them
     - Don't blame them (no "what did you expect?" or "why didn't you leave?")
     - Don't share their story without permission
     - Help them connect with support (counsellor, helpline, trusted adult)
     - Take care of your own emotional health too — supporting a survivor is hard
   - If you are experiencing GBV:
     - It is not your fault
     - Tell a trusted adult — a parent, teacher, counsellor, doctor
     - You have the right to safety and support

4. **Strategies for Getting Help in Abusive Relationships**
   - **Talk to someone:** trusted adult, school counsellor, friend's parent
   - **Helplines:** Kids Help Phone (1-800-668-6868), text CONNECT to 686868
   - **211 Nova Scotia:** dial 2-1-1 to find local support services
   - **Professional help:** school counsellor, therapist, doctor, Nova Scotia Health mental health services
   - **Safety planning:** identifying safe adults, safe places, and steps to leave an unsafe situation
   - **Legal protections:** non-consensual image sharing is illegal; police can be involved in physical or sexual violence
   - You are never required to handle abuse alone

### Sample Quiz/Test Items
- **Define:** Gender-based violence · Coercion · Victim-blaming · Gaslighting
- **Identify:** 3 contributing factors to gender-based violence and explain how each one operates
- **Analyse:** How does the phrase "boys will be boys" contribute to a culture where GBV is tolerated?
- **Apply:** Scenario — "A friend tells you their partner constantly checks their phone and gets angry when they talk to other people." Identify the warning signs and explain how you would respond.
- **Investigate:** What actions can peers take to counter behaviours that contribute to gender-based violence?
- **Compare:** Physical violence vs emotional/psychological violence — how are they similar and different in terms of impact?
- **Reflect:** Why is it important to avoid victim-blaming language when someone discloses an experience of violence?
- **List:** 3 resources a person experiencing gender-based violence in Nova Scotia could access

## Unit 7 — Outcome 7: Personal Safety & Injury Prevention (Online and Offline)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Risk factor** — A condition or behaviour that increases the likelihood of injury or harm.
- **Protective factor** — A condition or behaviour that reduces the likelihood of injury or harm.
- **Intentional injury** — Harm caused deliberately by another person (violence, assault, bullying).
- **Unintentional injury** — Harm caused by accident (falls, sports injuries, vehicle crashes, drowning).
- **Online victimization** — Being targeted for harm through digital means (cyberbullying, harassment, grooming, exploitation, identity theft).
- **Digital footprint** — The trail of data and content a person creates online; persists even after deletion.
- **Grooming** — A process where someone builds trust with a young person online to manipulate or exploit them.
- **Sextortion** — A form of blackmail where someone threatens to share intimate images unless demands are met (money, more images).
- **Personal boundary** — A limit you set on what is acceptable behaviour from others and what you are comfortable with.
- **Digital rights** — The rights individuals have when using technology (privacy, safety, freedom from harassment, consent for image sharing).
- **Digital responsibility** — The obligation to use technology in ways that respect others' rights and well-being.

### Core Concepts
1. **Age-Related Injury Risk Factors**
   - **Teens are at higher risk for certain injuries because:**
     - Developing brains take more risks (prefrontal cortex still maturing)
     - Peer influence increases risk-taking behaviour
     - New independence (driving, sports, part-time jobs, social situations)
   - **Common youth injury categories:**
     - Sports and recreation (concussions, sprains, fractures)
     - Falls (skateboarding, cycling, playgrounds)
     - Motor vehicle (passenger risk, new drivers, distracted walking)
     - Drowning (swimming without supervision, alcohol-involved)
     - Violence and assault
   - **Prevention strategies:** helmets and protective gear, seatbelts, supervision, substance-free activities, training and skill-building

2. **Rights & Responsibilities in Online Spaces**
   - **Rights:**
     - Right to privacy — you control your personal information
     - Right to safety — you should not be threatened, harassed, or exploited online
     - Right to be free from non-consensual image sharing
     - Right to report and block
     - Right to disconnect — you can step away from any online space
   - **Responsibilities:**
     - Don't share others' private information or images without consent
     - Don't harass, bully, or threaten others
     - Think before you post — content can be permanent and public
     - Respect community guidelines
     - Be honest about your identity in spaces where it matters

3. **Preventing, Navigating & Responding to Online Victimization**
   - **Prevention:** privacy settings, don't share personal details with strangers, don't send intimate images, be cautious about friend requests, use strong passwords
   - **Recognizing grooming:** someone older who gives lots of attention, asks for personal info, tries to move to private chat, sends gifts, asks for images, keeps secrets from parents
   - **Recognizing sextortion:** demands for money or more images after sharing something intimate; threats to share images
   - **Responding:**
     - Don't comply with demands (it often escalates)
     - Take screenshots for evidence
     - Tell a trusted adult immediately
     - Report the account to the platform
     - Contact police if threats involve images or personal safety
     - Cybertip.ca — Canada's tipline for reporting online sexual exploitation of children
     - NeedHelpNow.ca — support for teens affected by sextortion

4. **Personal Boundaries**
   - Boundaries are essential for safety and healthy relationships
   - Setting boundaries: know what you're comfortable with, communicate limits clearly, be specific
   - Respecting others' boundaries: listen when someone says no, don't pressure, don't push past stated limits
   - Boundaries can be physical, emotional, digital, and social
   - Everyone has the right to set and maintain boundaries — this is not selfish or rude

### Sample Quiz/Test Items
- **Define:** Online victimization · Grooming · Sextortion · Digital footprint
- **Analyse:** Why are teens at higher risk for certain injuries? Name 3 age-related risk factors.
- **Identify:** 3 rights and 3 responsibilities a person has in online spaces
- **Investigate:** What are the signs that someone may be grooming a teen online?
- **Apply:** Scenario — "Priya receives a friend request from someone she doesn't know who says they're 16. They quickly start asking personal questions and want to move to a private chat." What should Priya do?
- **Compare:** Prevention vs response strategies for online victimization — how are they different?
- **Reflect:** Why is it harder to set boundaries online than in person? What makes digital boundaries important?

## Unit 8 — Outcome 8: Bystander Intervention Skills

### Key Vocabulary
- **Bystander** — A person who witnesses a situation involving risk, harm, or emergency but is not directly involved.
- **Bystander intervention** — Taking action to help, prevent harm, or support someone in a risky or dangerous situation.
- **Active bystander** — Someone who witnesses a situation and chooses to take constructive action rather than remaining passive.
- **Passive bystander** — Someone who witnesses a concerning situation but does nothing — which can enable harm to continue.
- **Diffusion of responsibility** — The tendency for people to feel less responsible to act when others are present ("someone else will do something").
- **Bystander effect** — The phenomenon where the more people who witness a situation, the less likely any single person is to intervene.
- **Emergency** — A situation that requires immediate action to prevent serious harm or death (medical emergency, violence, accident).
- **Risk scenario** — A situation where harm could occur but isn't immediately life-threatening (bullying, harassment, pressure to do something unsafe).

### Core Concepts
1. **Emergency & Risk Scenarios**
   - **Medical emergencies:** someone collapses, is bleeding heavily, is choking, has a seizure, overdose
   - **Violence/safety emergencies:** physical fight, assault, someone being followed, weapon present
   - **Social risk scenarios:** bullying (in-person or online), peer pressure to use substances, harassment, sexual harassment, hazing, exclusion/bullying
   - **Digital risk scenarios:** cyberbullying, sharing of non-consensual images, sextortion, hate speech online
   - **Substance-related scenarios:** someone has had too much to drink, drug use, someone passing out
   - **Mental health scenarios:** someone expressing suicidal thoughts, self-harm, panic attack

2. **Taking Action — The 5 D's of Bystander Intervention**
   - **Direct** — Speak up directly: "Hey, stop that." "That's not okay."
   - **Distract** — Interrupt the situation indirectly: start a conversation, spill something, ask for help with something unrelated
   - **Delegate** — Get someone with more authority: tell a teacher, call 911, find a supervisor
   - **Document** — Record what's happening (video/notes) if safe — can be evidence later; do NOT share publicly
   - **Delay** — Check in with the person after: "Are you okay? What happened? Do you need help?"
   - Not all situations are safe to intervene in directly — assess before acting

3. **Overcoming Barriers to Intervention**
   - **Fear of retaliation:** What if they come after me? → Intervene safely (delegate, anonymous report, stay in groups)
   - **Diffusion of responsibility:** Someone else will handle it → You may be the ONLY person willing to act
   - **Not sure what's happening:** Is it serious? → Trust your instincts; check in with the person
   - **Social pressure:** Everyone else is laughing → Doing the right thing isn't always popular
   - **Fear of making it worse:** What if I say the wrong thing? → Any action is usually better than no action; even a small gesture matters
   - **Lack of confidence:** I don't know what to do → Practice scenarios in advance, start small

### Sample Quiz/Test Items
- **Define:** Bystander intervention · Diffusion of responsibility · Bystander effect
- **Identify:** 3 types of emergency or risk scenarios a teen might encounter
- **Apply:** Scenario — "You see a classmate being cornered in the hallway by a group of students who are threatening them." Walk through how you would respond using the 5 D's.
- **Investigate:** List 4 barriers that prevent bystanders from taking action. For each, explain a strategy to overcome it.
- **Compare:** Direct intervention vs delegate — when is each more appropriate?
- **Evaluate:** "I don't need to help because there are lots of other people around." Explain why this thinking is dangerous using the concept of diffusion of responsibility.
- **Reflect:** Describe a situation where you witnessed something and chose to act (or chose not to). What influenced your decision?

## Unit 9 — Outcome 9: Navigating Change & Challenging Circumstances

### Key Vocabulary
- **Protective factor** — A condition or behaviour that buffers against stress and helps a person cope with difficulty (supportive relationships, healthy habits, problem-solving skills).
- **Risk factor** — A condition that increases vulnerability to negative outcomes during challenging times (isolation, substance use, poor coping skills).
- **Resilience** — The ability to adapt and recover from adversity, stress, or significant change.
- **Coping strategy** — A thought or action used to manage stress, emotions, or difficult situations.
- **Adaptive coping** — Healthy strategies that address the problem or manage emotions constructively (talking to someone, exercising, problem-solving, journaling).
- **Maladaptive coping** — Unhealthy strategies that avoid the problem or create new problems (substance use, self-harm, avoidance, aggression, isolation).
- **Self-care** — Intentional actions taken to maintain or improve one's own health and well-being.
- **Rest** — Giving the body and mind time to recover and recharge (sleep, quiet time, breaks from stress).
- **Relaxation technique** — A deliberate practice to calm the body and mind (deep breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, meditation, mindfulness).
- **Transition** — A significant change in life circumstances (moving to a new school, family changes, graduation, relationship changes).

### Core Concepts
1. **Protective Factors for Resilience**
   - **Internal protective factors:** problem-solving skills, emotional regulation, self-esteem, sense of purpose, optimism, communication skills, help-seeking ability
   - **External protective factors:** supportive family, trusted friends, caring adults (teachers, coaches, mentors), involvement in community/activities, access to services, stable living conditions
   - Protective factors can be built and strengthened — they are not fixed
   - More protective factors = greater resilience to navigate change and challenge

2. **Coping with Life Challenges**
   - **Common challenges for teens:** family changes (divorce, new family members), school transitions, friendship changes, grief/loss, identity exploration, academic pressure, financial stress, health problems
   - **Emotional responses are normal:** sadness, anger, fear, confusion, relief — there's no "wrong" emotion
   - **Adaptive coping strategies:**
     - Talk to a trusted person
     - Physical activity (walking, sports, dance)
     - Creative expression (art, music, writing)
     - Problem-solving (break the problem into parts, take small steps)
     - Mindfulness and breathing exercises
     - Maintaining routines (meals, sleep, activities)
     - Journaling
     - Seeking professional help when needed
   - **Maladaptive coping to avoid:** substance use, self-harm, isolating from everyone, lashing out at others, ignoring the problem entirely, excessive screen time to escape
   - Coping strategies are skills — they improve with practice

3. **Practices for Rest, Fun & Relaxation**
   - **Rest:** adequate sleep (8–10 hours), downtime without screens, breaks during study, saying "no" to overcommitment
   - **Fun:** activities you enjoy for their own sake — hobbies, games, creative projects, time with friends, outdoor activities, music
   - **Relaxation techniques:**
     - Deep breathing (box breathing: inhale 4 counts, hold 4, exhale 4, hold 4)
     - Progressive muscle relaxation (tense and release each muscle group)
     - Mindfulness (noticing the present moment without judgment)
     - Physical relaxation (stretching, yoga, warm bath)
     - Nature time (walks outside, sitting in a park)
   - Rest, fun, and relaxation are not optional extras — they are essential for mental and physical health, especially during challenging times
   - Schedule them deliberately, just like homework and commitments

### Sample Quiz/Test Items
- **Define:** Resilience · Protective factor · Adaptive coping
- **Identify:** 3 internal and 3 external protective factors
- **Evaluate:** Is playing video games for 4 hours adaptive coping or maladaptive coping? Explain your reasoning (consider context).
- **Compare:** Adaptive vs maladaptive coping strategies — give 2 examples of each
- **Apply:** Scenario — "Jayden's family just moved to a new province. Jayden is starting a new school and doesn't know anyone." Identify 2 protective factors Jayden could strengthen and 3 coping strategies they could use.
- **List:** 3 relaxation techniques and describe how to do one of them
- **Reflect:** What is one rest, fun, or relaxation practice that works for you? Why is it important to have multiple strategies?

## Unit 10 — Outcome 10: Addiction & Substance Use Impacts

### Key Vocabulary
- **Addiction** — A chronic, relapsing condition where a person compulsively engages in a behaviour or uses a substance despite harmful consequences; involves changes in the brain's reward system.
- **Continuum of use** — A spectrum ranging from no use → experimental use → regular use → problematic use → addiction.
- **Protective influence** — A factor that decreases the likelihood of developing a substance use problem (strong family bonds, school engagement, refusal skills, healthy coping).
- **Enabling influence** — A factor that increases the likelihood of developing a substance use problem (easy access to substances, peer pressure, trauma, stress, lack of supervision, substance use in the home).
- **Tolerance** — Needing more of a substance to get the same effect; a sign the body is adapting to the substance.
- **Dependence** — The body needing a substance to function normally; stopping causes withdrawal symptoms.
- **Withdrawal** — Physical and mental symptoms that occur when a person stops using a substance their body has become dependent on (headaches, nausea, anxiety, shakes).
- **Substance use disorder** — A medical diagnosis for problematic substance use that causes significant impairment or distress.
- **Behavioural addiction** — An addiction to a behaviour rather than a substance (e.g., gambling, gaming, social media, shopping).
- **Co-use** — Using two or more substances at the same time, which amplifies risks (e.g., alcohol + cannabis = significantly greater impairment).
- **Harm reduction** — Strategies that reduce the negative consequences of substance use without requiring abstinence (e.g., designated driver, using with trusted people, knowing your limits).

### Core Concepts
1. **Protective & Enabling Influences on the Continuum of Use**
   - **The continuum:** Abstinence → Experimentation → Social/occasional use → Regular use → Problematic use → Addiction
   - Not everyone who experiments will develop addiction — protective and enabling factors influence the path
   - **Protective influences:**
     - Strong, supportive relationships with family and friends
     - Engagement in school, sports, hobbies, community
     - Good mental health and coping skills
     - Accurate knowledge about substance risks
     - Clear personal values and goals
     - Refusal skills and assertiveness
     - Positive role models
   - **Enabling influences:**
     - Availability of substances (easy access)
     - Peer pressure and social norms that normalize use
     - Family history of addiction
     - Trauma, adverse childhood experiences, chronic stress
     - Mental health challenges (anxiety, depression, ADHD)
     - Curiosity without accurate information
     - Media portrayal of substance use as glamorous or risk-free

2. **Impacts of Various Kinds of Addictions**
   - **Substance addictions:**
     - Alcohol: liver disease, impaired judgement, addiction, affects all 5 dimensions of health
     - Cannabis: impaired memory and attention, mental health risk (especially with early use), lung damage (if smoked)
     - Tobacco/vaping: nicotine addiction, lung damage, heart disease, cancer; vaping can deliver high nicotine doses quickly
     - Prescription drug misuse: opioid addiction risk, overdose, organ damage
     - Stimulants (cocaine, meth, excess caffeine): heart problems, anxiety, addiction, psychosis
   - **Behavioural addictions:**
     - Gaming: disrupted sleep, social isolation, neglect of responsibilities
     - Social media: anxiety, comparison, compulsive checking, reduced attention span
     - Gambling: financial ruin, relationship damage, mental health crisis
   - **Common impacts across all addictions:**
     - Damage to relationships and trust
     - Decline in school/work performance
     - Financial problems
     - Mental health deterioration (anxiety, depression, paranoia)
     - Loss of interest in previously enjoyed activities
     - Physical health decline
     - All 5 dimensions of health are affected

3. **Substance Use & Co-Use Impacts on the Dimensions of Health**
   - **Physical:** organ damage, impaired immune system, injury risk, sleep disruption, nutritional problems
   - **Mental:** impaired thinking, memory problems, poor decision-making, reduced focus, brain development interference (adolescent brain is especially vulnerable)
   - **Emotional:** increased anxiety, depression, emotional instability, mood swings, emotional dependence on substance
   - **Social:** relationship conflict, isolation, strained family bonds, association with risky peer groups
   - **Spiritual:** loss of purpose, disconnect from values, loss of meaning, substance becomes the priority
   - Co-use (e.g., alcohol + cannabis, alcohol + energy drinks, mixing medications) compounds risks: unpredictable effects, greater impairment, higher overdose risk

### Sample Quiz/Test Items
- **Define:** Addiction · Continuum of use · Tolerance · Behavioural addiction
- **Analyse:** How does substance use impact each of the 5 dimensions of health?
- **Identify:** 3 protective influences and 3 enabling influences on substance use
- **Investigate:** How is behavioural addiction similar to and different from substance addiction?
- **Compare:** Regular use vs problematic use on the continuum — what distinguishes them?
- **Evaluate:** "Vaping is safe because it's not smoking." Analyse this statement using evidence about the health impacts of vaping.
- **Apply:** Scenario — "A friend has been drinking alcohol and is offered cannabis at the same party." Explain the added risks of co-use.
- **Reflect:** Why might someone develop an addiction even if they know the risks? Consider the role of enabling factors and brain chemistry.

## Unit 11 — Outcome 11: Help-Seeking Efficacy Skills

### Key Vocabulary
- **Help-seeking efficacy** — The confidence and skill to identify when help is needed and to successfully access it.
- **Signs of concern** — Observable indicators that someone may be struggling with a mental health problem, illness, or addiction (changes in behaviour, mood, appearance, or habits).
- **Self-harm** — Deliberately injuring oneself, usually as a way of coping with emotional pain (cutting, burning, hitting).
- **Suicidal ideation** — Thoughts about killing oneself; can range from passing thoughts to detailed plans.
- **Substance use disorder** — A diagnosable condition where substance use causes significant impairment or distress (see Unit 10).
- **Mental health crisis** — A situation where a person's mental health deteriorates to the point that they may be at risk of harming themselves or others.
- **Community resource** — A service or organization available locally to support health and well-being (counselling centres, helplines, hospitals, support groups).
- **Active listening** — Fully focusing on what someone is saying, showing empathy, and reflecting back to show understanding.
- **Empathy** — The ability to understand and share the feelings of another person; not the same as sympathy (feeling sorry for someone).
- **Gatekeeper** — A person trained to recognize signs of mental health concerns and connect the person with help (e.g., teachers, coaches, counsellors).

### Core Concepts
1. **Signs That Someone May Need Help**
   - **Mental health concerns:**
     - Persistent sadness, hopelessness, or emptiness lasting more than 2 weeks
     - Withdrawal from friends, activities, or family
     - Big changes in eating or sleeping patterns
     - Difficulty concentrating or sudden drop in school performance
     - Excessive worry, fear, or panic
     - Expressing feelings of worthlessness or guilt
     - Talking about death, dying, or suicide
   - **Addiction concerns:**
     - Increasing use of substances or inability to cut down
     - Neglecting responsibilities (school, family, activities)
     - Changes in friend groups or social behaviour
     - Needing more of the substance to get the same effect (tolerance)
     - Withdrawal symptoms when not using
     - Financial problems related to substance use
     - Mood swings, secrecy, defensiveness about use
   - **When signs appear in yourself:** don't ignore them — everyone struggles sometimes, and seeking help is a strength

2. **Communication Skills for Asking for & Offering Help**
   - **Asking for help (for yourself):**
     - Identify a trusted person (friend, family member, teacher, counsellor)
     - Be direct: "I'm not doing well and I need someone to talk to"
     - Be specific about what you're experiencing: "I've been feeling really anxious and can't sleep"
     - Be persistent — if one person doesn't help, ask someone else
     - It's okay to not know exactly what's wrong — start by saying "I don't feel right"
   - **Offering help (to someone else):**
     - Notice the signs and don't wait for them to come to you
     - Start with empathy: "I've noticed you seem down lately. How are you doing?"
     - Listen without judgment — don't minimize their feelings ("You'll get over it")
     - Don't promise to keep secrets if someone is in danger
     - Offer specific help: "Do you want to talk, or would you like me to help you find someone?"
     - Follow up — check in again later
     - If you're worried about immediate safety: stay with them, tell an adult immediately, call 911 or take them to emergency

3. **Community Resources for Help (Nova Scotia Specific)**
   - **For mental health concerns or crisis:**
     - Kids Help Phone — 1-800-668-6868, text CONNECT to 686868, kidshelpphone.ca (24/7, anonymous, free)
     - 211 Nova Scotia — dial 2-1-1 (connects you to local community services)
     - Nova Scotia Mental Health and Addictions Crisis Line — 1-888-429-8167
     - 911 — immediate danger (suicide attempt, overdose, medical emergency)
     - Hospital emergency department — for mental health crisis
   - **For ongoing support:**
     - School counsellor — first point of contact in school
     - Family doctor / nurse practitioner — assessment, referral
     - Nova Scotia Health — Mental Health and Addictions services (counselling, group programs)
     - THRIVE — NS youth mental health hubs
     - IWK Mental Health — specialty services for youth
   - **For substance use concerns:**
     - Nova Scotia Health — Addiction Services (counselling, detox, treatment programs)
     - Your Life — Your Choice — NS youth substance use resources
   - **For sexual health:**
     - Public health clinics (confidential, free)
     - Planned Parenthood Atlantic
     - Family doctor or pharmacist
   - **Online resources:**
     - kidshelpphone.ca
     - mindyourmind.ca
     - camh.ca
     - foundrybc.ca (BC-based but has useful national content)
     - NeedHelpNow.ca (for sextortion / online exploitation)

### Sample Quiz/Test Items
- **Define:** Help-seeking efficacy · Signs of concern · Empathy · Gatekeeper
- **Identify:** 5 signs that someone may need help with a mental health problem, and 5 signs of possible substance use disorder
- **Apply:** Scenario — "You notice your friend has stopped coming to soccer practice, seems distant when you text, and posted something dark on social media." Write out the steps you would take using help-seeking communication skills.
- **Compare:** Sympathy vs empathy — how are they different? Why does empathy matter more when offering help?
- **Investigate:** List 5 Nova Scotia-specific resources where a teen could seek help for mental health or addiction concerns.
- **Use:** Write a script of what you would say to a trusted adult if you were worried about your own mental health.
- **Reflect:** Why might a teen hesitate to ask for help? What would make it easier?
