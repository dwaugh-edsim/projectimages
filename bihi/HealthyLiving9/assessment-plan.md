# Healthy Living 9 — Assessment Plan (90 Classes)

## 1. Assessment Philosophy

This course uses a balanced mix of **formative ongoing checks** and **summative per-unit assessments**, with an emphasis on scenario-based and reflective assessment rather than rote recall. Grade 9 students (14–15) are assessed on their ability to *apply* health knowledge to realistic situations, *analyse* influences on health decisions, and *reflect* on their own experiences and growth.

### Why 90 Classes Changes the Approach

With **90 classes** across the year (5 classes per 2-week cycle, ~36 weeks), there is significantly more instructional time than the original 36-class model assumed. This creates opportunities that were impossible before:

- **Formative depth:** Every outcome gets 3–5 formative checks instead of 1–2, giving teachers real data on understanding *before* summatives. Students get repeated practice with vocabulary, scenarios, and self-assessment.
- **Mid-unit checks:** With 7–9 classes per outcome, a mid-unit quiz catches misconceptions while there's time to correct them.
- **Multi-class projects:** Projects can span 3–4 classes with in-class work sessions, teacher check-ins, and peer feedback — not just assigned as homework.
- **Peer assessment:** Dedicated class time for students to present work, give structured feedback, and revise before final submission.
- **Self-assessment routines:** Students regularly rate their own understanding and participation, building metacognitive skills.
- **Substantial capstone:** The final portfolio has dedicated class time (Classes 85–90) for curation, reflection writing, and optional presentation.
- **Sensitive topic pacing:** Outcomes 2, 6, and 10 can be taught with extra breathing room — anonymous formative checks, reflection-only days, and community-building before heavier content.

The goal is for every assessment to feel relevant to students' lives — not like a test to cram for and forget.

## 2. Formative Assessment Toolkit (Expanded Menu)

These tools are used throughout every unit. The table below shows each tool, when it works best, time required, and how results are used.

### Exit Tickets (Quick Knowledge Checks)
- **Format:** Half-sheet, sticky note on the door, or Google Form (3–5 questions)
- **Time:** Last 5 minutes of class
- **Mix:** 1–2 recall questions + 2–3 application/analysis questions
- **Use:** Teacher reads responses before next class to adjust instruction; identifies whole-class misconceptions
- **Example (O1):** "What are the 5 dimensions of health?" + "Give an example of how poor sleep hygiene could affect emotional health"
- **Google Forms tip:** Create a reusable template; duplicate and swap questions each use

### Think-Pair-Share + Class Vote
- **Format:** Pose a scenario → solo thinking (1 min) → partner discussion (2 min) → share with class → optional vote
- **Time:** 5–8 minutes
- **Use:** Reveals class-wide understanding; great for communication skills, bystander intervention, decision-making
- **Follow-up:** Quick hand raise or Google Form vote: "How many chose Option A? Option B?"
- **Best for:** O4 (communication), O5 (gender norms), O8 (bystander intervention), O9 (coping)

### Journal Prompts (Every 2 Weeks)
- **Format:** Weekly Google Doc journal; students add a new entry each week in the same ongoing Doc
- **Time:** 10–15 minutes at start or end of class (alternating)
- **Graded for:** Completion and thoughtfulness, not "right answers"
- **Frequency:** ~18 entries across 90 classes (one every ~5 classes)
- **Sample prompts:**
  - "Describe a time you had to say no to something. Which refusal skill did you use (or wish you had)?"
  - "How does your identity affect the health choices you make?"
  - "What's one protective factor in your life? How does it help you?"
- **Teacher response:** Brief comments every 2–3 weeks to build connection
- **Google Classroom tip:** Create one ongoing Journal assignment per term; students add entries to the same Doc
- **Sensitive outcomes note:** For O2, O6, O10 — journal prompts are explicitly optional, private (submitted only to teacher), and never shared with peers

### Scenario Cards (Situational Response)
- **Format:** 8–10 short scenarios on index cards, Google Slides, or printed cards
- **Time:** 10–15 minutes
- **Use:** Students read a scenario, write a 3–5 sentence response applying relevant skills
- **Example:** "Your friend keeps texting you late at night and you're exhausted. How do you communicate this using an 'I' statement?"
- **Can be:** Individual (written), pairs (discussion), or small groups (role-play prep)
- **Best for:** O4 (communication), O7 (online safety), O8 (bystander), O10 (substance scenarios)

### Google Form Quizzes (Self-Marking Check-Ins)
- **Format:** Self-marking Google Forms, 5–8 items
- **Time:** 10–15 minutes (in-class) or homework
- **Item types:** Vocabulary matching, true/false, multiple choice, one-sentence short answer
- **Weight:** Low-stakes (participation marks) — not graded heavily
- **Use:** Mid-unit understanding checks; track results in Google Sheets to identify gaps
- **Frequency:** 1–2 per outcome (at least 14 across the year)
- **Example (O3):** Match the resource to its use (811, public health, pharmacist, emergency)

### Peer Feedback (Structured)
- **Format:** Peer feedback form (Google Form or paper) with specific criteria
- **Time:** 15–20 minutes after project presentations or draft submissions
- **Structure:** Students use a rubric or checklist to give one "glow" (strength) and one "grow" (suggestion)
- **Use:** Improves project quality before final submission; builds assessment literacy
- **Best for:** O3 (mini-project), O4+O5 (major project), O8 (bystander role-plays), O11 (help-seeking project)
- **Tip:** Rotate peer pairs so students get diverse feedback; model good feedback first

### Self-Assessment Rubrics
- **Format:** Simple 1–4 rating scale on a Google Form or printed sheet
- **Time:** 5 minutes at end of class or unit
- **Criteria:** "I can explain [concept] in my own words," "I participated in discussions," "I can apply [skill] to a scenario"
- **Use:** Builds metacognition; teacher compares student self-ratings to actual performance
- **Frequency:** End of each outcome (11 times across the year)
- **Tip:** Track self-assessment trends over time; use in student-teacher conferences

### Observation Checklists (Teacher Walking)
- **Format:** Teacher carries a clipboard or tablet during group work, discussions, or role-plays
- **Time:** Ongoing during class activities
- **Checklist items:** Participation, on-task behaviour, use of vocabulary, collaboration, contribution to discussion
- **Use:** Informs participation grade; identifies students who may need extra support or extension
- **Best for:** All outcomes, especially O4 (communication role-plays), O5 (group discussions), O8 (bystander practice)
- **Tip:** Track 4–6 students per class on a rotating schedule so every student is observed regularly

## 3. Per-Outcome Assessment Plan

> **Design principle:** 11 outcomes are assessed through **9 summative assessments + 1 capstone portfolio**. Each outcome gets dedicated formative support (3–5 formative checks). Related outcomes are combined where they share themes (O4+O5, O6+O7). Outcomes with sensitive content (O2, O6, O10) have special assessment considerations noted below.

### Outcome 1: Health Behaviours (Classes 1–9, 9 classes)

**Allocation:** 9 classes
**Primary summative:** Quiz (Google Forms or paper)
**Secondary assessments (formative):** Exit tickets, think-pair-share, journal prompt, mid-unit Google Form check, self-assessment rubric

**Approximate timing:**
- Classes 1–2: Health behaviours intro, sleep
- Classes 3–4: Technology use, disease prevention
- Classes 5–6: Identity and health decisions
- Class 7: Mid-unit Google Form check (5–8 items, self-marking)
- Class 8: Review, practice scenarios, scenario cards
- **Class 9: 📋 Summative — Health Behaviours Quiz**

**Formative schedule:**
| Class | Formative Tool | Focus |
|-------|---------------|-------|
| 2 | Exit ticket | Sleep hygiene vocabulary |
| 3 | Think-pair-share | "Is playing games until 2 AM OK if you sleep in?" |
| 5 | Journal prompt | "How does your identity affect your health choices?" |
| 7 | Google Form check | Mid-unit quiz (self-marking, 6 items) |
| 8 | Scenario cards | Apply concepts to Morgan's sleep pattern scenario |
| 9 | Self-assessment rubric | Rate your understanding of O1 concepts |

**Summative 1 — Health Behaviours Quiz (6% of final grade):**
- **Format:** 15–18 items (MC, short answer, one extended scenario)
- **Curriculum verbs:** Evaluate, Analyse, Investigate, Reflect
- **Sample items:**
  1. *Define* sleep hygiene and list three practices that support it.
  2. *Analyse:* How does sleep deficit affect each of the 5 dimensions of health? Name at least 3.
  3. *Evaluate:* "Playing video games until 2 AM is fine if you still get 8 hours by sleeping in." Is this accurate? Why or why not?
  4. *Identify:* 3 healthy technology habits and 3 signs of technology overuse.
  5. *Scenario:* Morgan sleeps 5 hours on weeknights and 12 hours on weekends. Analyse the short- and long-term health impacts of this pattern.
  6. *Reflect:* How has your personal identity influenced a health decision you've made recently?

**Summative 4 — Healthy Relationships Major Project (10% of final grade):**
- **Format:** Google Doc (3–4 pages) or Google Slides. May include a brief (3–5 min) small-group presentation.
- **Outcomes covered:** O4 + O5

- **Task — Part B (Gender Norms & Power Dynamics — O5):**

  5. *Investigate:* How do media reinforce OR challenge gender stereotypes? Give one example of each.
  6. *Scenario:* A classmate says, "That's a girl sport — you shouldn't play it." Write a response promoting gender equality. Explain your reasoning.
- **Task — Part C (Reflection):**
  7. *Reflect:* Think about a gender expectation you've experienced or observed. How did it make people feel? What would be a more equitable approach?

**Summative 5 — Safety Scenario Assessment (O6 + O7) (10% of final grade):**
- **Format:** 20–25 items (vocabulary/definitions, short-answer analysis, 4 extended scenario responses). Done in class over one period. May include one take-home reflection.
- **Curriculum verbs (O6):** Analyse, Investigate, Identify — (O7): Analyse, Investigate, Identify
- **Why combine:** Gender-based violence (O6) and personal safety (O7) are tightly linked — both involve recognizing risk, understanding boundaries, and knowing how to respond.
- **Sample items:**
  1. (O6) *Define* gender-based violence, coercion, and victim-blaming.
  2. (O6) *Analyse:* How does "boys will be boys" contribute to a culture where GBV is tolerated?
  3. (O6) *Scenario:* A friend tells you their partner constantly checks their phone and gets angry when they talk to others. Identify warning signs and explain how you would respond using at least 2 supporting strategies.
  4. (O7) *Define* grooming and sextortion. What are the signs of each?
  5. (O7) *Scenario:* Priya receives a friend request from someone she doesn't know who says they're 16. They ask personal questions and want to move to a private chat. What should Priya do? Name at least 3 steps.
  6. (O7) *Identify* 3 rights and 3 responsibilities in online spaces.
  7. (O6+O7) *Scenario:* A classmate shares that someone is pressuring them to send photos and threatening to share their location if they don't. Apply concepts from both O6 and O7 to explain what you would do.
  8. *Reflect:* Why is it important to avoid victim-blaming language when someone discloses an experience of violence?
- **Sensitivity note:** Content warnings provided. Students may write about scenarios at a distance (third person). Alternative prompts available if any student finds a scenario triggering.

## 6. Final Assessment — Capstone Portfolio

### What It Is

The capstone portfolio is the culminating assessment for Healthy Living 9. It asks students to **curate their best work**, **reflect on their growth**, and **connect learning across all 11 outcomes** in a meaningful way. Rather than a traditional exam that tests recall in isolation, the portfolio asks students to demonstrate *how* health education applies to their real life.

### Why a Portfolio (Not an Exam)

- **Authentic:** Students select work they're proud of and explain why it matters to them
- **Integrative:** Requires connecting concepts across outcomes (e.g., how communication skills relate to bystander intervention)
- **Personal:** Reflection is grounded in students' own experiences and goals
- **Equitable:** Doesn't favour test-taking skills over understanding; multiple formats (written, digital, presented)
- **Lasting:** Students leave the course with a document that represents their learning

### Portfolio Structure

1. **Reflective Introduction (1 page)**
   - What is one thing you learned that changed how you think about your health or relationships?
   - How will you apply it?

2. **Curated Work Samples (3–4 pages)**
   - Select 3–4 pieces of best work from the year
   - Options: journal entries, project excerpts, quiz responses that showed growth, scenario responses you're proud of
   - For each: "I chose this because..." + "This shows I can..."

3. **Health Self-Portrait (1 page)**
   - Where are you now across the 5 dimensions of health?
   - What's one area you want to work on?
   - What strategies from the course will you use?

4. **Future Application (1 paragraph)**
   - Choose a scenario relevant to your life right now
   - Apply concepts from at least 2 different outcomes

### Assessment Criteria

- **Quality of reflection:** Specific, honest, connected to curriculum concepts
- **Connections across outcomes:** Shows integration of ideas from multiple units
- **Self-awareness:** Demonstrates honest self-assessment of strengths and growth areas
- **Organization:** Clear structure, easy to navigate
- **Evidence of growth:** Shows how thinking has developed over the year

### Optional Presentation

Students may choose to present their portfolio highlights in a 3–5 minute presentation to a small group (3–4 classmates). This substitutes for 2 pages of written work. Presentation rubric:
- Clear articulation of key learning moments
- Specific examples from the course
- Engagement with the audience
- Connections to personal life

### Alternative: Scenario-Based Exam (If Required)

If the school mandates a formal exam, replace the reflective introduction and future application with an in-class written component:

- 3–4 novel scenarios drawing on concepts from across the full year
- 60 minutes, open-notes
- Adjusted weights: Capstone becomes 6%, Exam becomes 4%
